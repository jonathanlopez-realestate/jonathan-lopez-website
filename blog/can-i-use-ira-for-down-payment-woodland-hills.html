Can I Use My IRA to Help Buy My First Home in Woodland Hills?

Jonathan Lopez, Woodland Hills Realtor | Jim Sandoval Group | CA DRE #02282844 · Updated September 2026

First-time buyers in Woodland Hills spend a lot of energy on down payment myths (the 20% assumption, the idea that a gift from parents is the only real shortcut) and often miss a source of funds they already have: their own retirement account. The IRS built a specific, penalty-free carve-out into the tax code for exactly this situation, and it's narrower and more particular than most people assume.

The $10,000 Lifetime Exception

Under IRS Publication 590-B, a first-time homebuyer can withdraw up to $10,000 from an IRA without paying the usual 10% early withdrawal penalty that normally applies before age 59 and a half. The catch is timing: the money has to be used to pay qualified acquisition costs, meaning a down payment, closing costs, or reasonable settlement costs, before the close of the 120th day after you receive the distribution. Miss that window and the exception doesn't apply. This is a lifetime limit, not an annual one, and it applies across every IRA you hold combined. You cannot pull $10,000 from a Roth and another $10,000 from a Traditional IRA and call both penalty-free, the cap is $10,000 total, once, ever.

Married Couples Can Double It

Because the exception is per person, a married couple can each draw on their own IRA, for a combined $20,000 penalty-free, as long as each spouse independently qualifies as a first-time homebuyer and each withdrawal comes from that spouse's own account.

Who Actually Counts as "First Time"

The IRS defines a first-time homebuyer as someone who has had no present ownership interest in a main home during the two-year period ending on the date the new home is acquired. That is a narrower, more forgiving window than some assistance programs use. CalHFA's MyHome Assistance Program, for comparison, requires three full years without ownership interest in a principal residence. It's possible to qualify for the IRS's IRA exception while still falling short of CalHFA's stricter three-year rule, so the two need to be checked separately rather than assumed to match.

Traditional IRA vs. Roth IRA, They Don't Work the Same Way

A withdrawal from a Traditional IRA under this exception skips the 10% penalty, but the withdrawn amount is still taxed as ordinary income in the year you take it, since Traditional IRA contributions were typically made pre-tax. A Roth IRA is more favorable in two ways. Your own contributions to a Roth can always be withdrawn tax-free and penalty-free at any time, for any reason, since you already paid tax on that money going in. If you're withdrawing Roth earnings (growth beyond what you contributed) under the first-time homebuyer exception, those earnings skip the 10% penalty regardless of account age, but only come out fully tax-free too if the Roth account has been open at least 5 years. A Roth opened more recently still gets the earnings out penalty-free, just not tax-free.

Sizing the Real Impact

It helps to see this against Woodland Hills' actual numbers rather than in the abstract. The current median sale price is $1,348,500 (Movoto, updated September 14, 2026). A standard 3.5% FHA minimum down payment on that median works out to roughly $47,200. A single buyer's $10,000 IRA withdrawal covers about 21% of that down payment; a married couple's combined $20,000 covers roughly 42%. That's meaningful, but it's rarely the whole down payment on a Woodland Hills purchase on its own. It stacks well with other tools already covered on this site: a parental gift under the IRS's separate $19,000 annual gift tax exclusion, or CalHFA's MyHome deferred junior loan, both of which can close the remaining gap.

The Tradeoff Nobody Should Skip

Pulling $10,000 or $20,000 out of a retirement account, even penalty-free, removes that money (and its future tax-advantaged growth) from your retirement timeline permanently, there's no repayment provision like there is with a 401(k) loan. For a buyer in their 20s or early 30s, $20,000 left invested for another 30 years is a materially larger number by retirement than the same $20,000 spent today. This is exactly the kind of tradeoff a CPA or financial advisor should walk through before anyone withdraws, not after.

Buying a first home in Woodland Hills usually means stacking several smaller tools rather than finding one silver bullet. A penalty-free IRA withdrawal is a real, IRS-sanctioned piece of that stack, worth knowing about even if it isn't the whole answer. Frequently Asked Questions

How much can I withdraw from my IRA penalty-free for a first home?

Up to $10,000 per person, as a lifetime limit across all your IRAs combined, not per account and not per year.

Do I still pay taxes on the withdrawal?

It depends on the account type. A Traditional IRA withdrawal skips the 10% early withdrawal penalty but is still taxed as ordinary income. A Roth IRA's own contributions come out both tax-free and penalty-free; Roth earnings withdrawn under this exception skip the penalty always, and skip taxes too only if the account has been open at least 5 years.

How is "first-time homebuyer" defined for this exception?

The IRS counts you as first-time if you've had no ownership interest in a main home during the 2 years before the purchase date. That's different from CalHFA's MyHome program, which requires a 3-year window, so qualifying for one doesn't automatically mean you qualify for the other.

Is there a deadline on using the withdrawn funds?

Yes. The money must be used to pay qualified acquisition costs (down payment, closing costs, reasonable settlement costs) within 120 days of receiving the distribution, or the penalty exception doesn't apply.

Can my spouse and I each use the $10,000 exception?

Yes, as long as each spouse independently qualifies as a first-time homebuyer and withdraws from their own IRA. That allows a combined $20,000 penalty-free between a married couple.

Is this the same as a 401(k) loan?

No. This is an IRA withdrawal, not a loan, so there's no repayment schedule and no way to put the money back on a tax-advantaged basis later. A 401(k) hardship withdrawal or loan follows entirely different rules and isn't covered by this IRA-specific exception.
