I Inherited My Woodland Hills Home for My Minor Children. Can I Just Sell It Myself?

Jonathan Lopez, Woodland Hills Realtor · Jim Sandoval Group · CA DRE #02282844 · Updated November 2026

If a Woodland Hills home came to your family through inheritance and one or more of the heirs is a minor child, you've likely already discovered that the usual playbook for selling an inherited home does not quite fit. A minor cannot execute a binding contract, including a listing agreement or a purchase and sale agreement, and that single fact changes the entire process from the moment you decide to sell, not just at closing.

Start with who actually has legal authority to act. Being a minor's parent does not automatically give you authority to sell real property that the minor has inherited in their own name. If the inheritance passed by will and the will named a custodian under California's Uniform Transfers to Minors Act provisions, that custodian may have more direct authority to manage and sell the asset without going back to court for every step, since the custodianship is designed to avoid a full guardianship proceeding. But if there's no UTMA custodian designation, no trust holding the property, and the minor simply inherited an ownership interest outright, by intestate succession or by a will with no such provision, the property typically ends up needing a guardianship of the estate: a court-appointed guardian (frequently the surviving parent, but the court still has to appoint them, it isn't automatic just because you're the parent) who holds legal authority over the minor's property and reports to the court on how it's managed.

Selling real property out of a guardianship of the estate is not as simple as the guardian deciding to sell and signing the paperwork. California probate procedure generally requires the guardian to petition the court for authority to sell, give notice to interested parties, and get a judge's approval of the sale before it can close, a layer of oversight on top of the ordinary escrow process any seller goes through. In some cases, particularly where there's any dispute, complexity, or conflict of interest among the parties, the court will also appoint a guardian ad litem, essentially an independent legal representative whose job is to look out for the minor's interests specifically, separate from the guardian's own interests. That's an added cost and an added step, not a formality that happens automatically in every case; whether one is needed depends on your specific facts and your probate attorney's read of the court's requirements.

Here's the part that surprises a lot of families after the sale actually closes: the minor's share of the proceeds doesn't just land in a regular account for the guardian to manage day to day. California requires those funds to go into a blocked account, an account the court controls, where no one, not even the court-appointed guardian, can withdraw money without a separate court order. If the family later needs funds released for the minor's benefit (school expenses, for example), that requires its own petition, the Judicial Council's Petition for Withdrawal of Funds from Blocked Account, Form MC-357, and another court review. The money is the minor's, fully, but access to it stays court-supervised until the minor turns 18 or the court says otherwise.

Practically, all of this adds real time to a transaction that would otherwise move at a normal escrow pace. Court calendars, notice periods, and the petition-and-approval sequence can add anywhere from several weeks to a few months before a sale can even go to market with full authority to close, longer if a guardian ad litem has to be appointed or if the petition draws any objection. It's not a reason to avoid selling, but it is a reason to start the legal side of the process early and in parallel with, not after, any decision about pricing or listing timing.

None of this is a substitute for a probate or guardianship attorney's advice on your family's specific facts, whether a UTMA custodianship already exists, whether there's a trust, whether a guardian has already been appointed changes which path applies. What I can do is handle the real estate side once the legal authority to sell is in place (or while it's being established, so you're ready to move the moment the court approves), including pricing the home accurately for Woodland Hills' current market: a $1,300,000 median sale price, 368 active listings, and homes averaging 54 days on market right now (Movoto).

If your family is navigating a minor's inheritance of a Woodland Hills home, I'd rather have the pricing and timing conversation with you now, in parallel with your attorney's court filings, than have you discover the extra timeline only after you've already listed.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can I just sign the listing agreement and purchase contract myself since I'm the parent?

Not automatically. A minor cannot hold authority to contract, and being the parent doesn't by itself give you legal authority over the minor's inherited property interest. In most cases a court has to appoint a guardian of the estate first, even if that guardian ends up being you.

What's a guardian of the estate, and is that different from being the minor's guardian generally?

Yes, they can be different roles. A guardian of the estate is specifically appointed by the probate court to manage the minor's property and finances, including real property, and reports to the court on how it's handled. It's a distinct, court-supervised authority, not something that comes automatically with custody or parenthood.

Do I need a court's permission to actually sell the house?

Generally, yes. Selling real property out of a guardianship of the estate typically requires its own court petition, a notice period to interested parties, and a judge's approval before the sale can close, in addition to the normal escrow process any seller goes through.

What happens to the minor's share of the money after the sale?

It goes into a blocked account that the court controls. No one, including the guardian, can withdraw from it without a separate court order (Judicial Council Form MC-357, Petition for Withdrawal of Funds from Blocked Account). The funds stay court-supervised, typically until the minor turns 18.

How much time does this actually add to selling the home?

It varies by court calendar and case complexity, but expect several weeks to a few months of added time for the petition-and-approval process, more if a guardian ad litem has to be appointed or if anyone objects to the petition. Starting the legal process early, in parallel with your real estate planning, helps.

Does this apply if the home is held in a trust for the minor instead of inherited outright?

Not necessarily in the same way. A trust with its own named trustee can often sell real property under the trustee's authority without the same guardianship-of-the-estate court process, since the trust document itself (not probate court appointment) is the source of authority. Whether that applies to your situation depends on the actual trust language, which a probate attorney needs to review.
