How Often Does a Buyer's Financing Actually Fall Through on a Woodland Hills Home Sale?

Jonathan Lopez · Woodland Hills Realtor, Jim Sandoval Group · CA DRE #02282844 · Updated October 2026

Woodland Hills sellers who finally get an accepted offer often exhale and assume the hard part is over. Most of the time that is a fair read. But national data on what actually happens between acceptance and closing shows a real, if modest, share of deals that stall out or fall apart entirely, and a seller who plans for that ahead of time ends up in a much better position than one who doesn't.

The National Association of Realtors publishes a monthly REALTORS Confidence Survey based on responses from its member agents nationwide. The September 2026 edition, published October 13, 2026, reports that 7% of contracts were terminated in the last three months, essentially flat from 7% the month before and up slightly from 6% a year earlier. That is the headline number: roughly 1 in 14 accepted contracts nationally does not make it to closing at all.

A larger share of deals do close, but later than planned. The same survey found 16% of contracts experienced a delayed settlement in the past three months, up from 14% both the prior month and a year ago, a real year-over-year increase worth noting. Of that group, the survey breaks out one specific cause: 5% of contracts were delayed because of appraisal issues, similar to the 6% seen both the month before and a year earlier. The survey does not separately break out what share of terminations or delays trace back to financing specifically, but financing and appraisal issues are closely linked, since a loan can't fund if the appraisal comes in short and the gap doesn't get resolved.

Context on who's buying matters here too. The same survey found 27% of buyers paid all cash, unchanged from the month before but down from 30% a year earlier. That means financed buyers, the ones whose purchase depends on a lender's underwriting and the property appraising at value, made up roughly three out of every four purchases nationally. Homes listed received an average of 2.2 offers, essentially flat from 2.1 the prior month.

None of these figures are specific to Woodland Hills or the 91367 zip code. No public source publishes contract termination or delayed-settlement rates at that level of geography, so this post uses the national figures and says so plainly rather than guessing at a local number.

What this means practically for a Woodland Hills seller is less about panic and more about preparation. A handful of things actually reduce real risk once you've accepted an offer. First, insist on a real underwritten pre-approval letter from the buyer's lender, not a pre-qualification, which is a much softer check that doesn't verify income, assets, or credit the way underwriting does. Second, it's reasonable to have your agent call the buyer's loan officer directly early in escrow to confirm the file is moving, not just take the letter at face value. Third, if a second strong offer exists when you accept the first, keeping it as a documented backup offer under the standard C.A.R. purchase agreement framework costs nothing and means you aren't starting from zero if the primary deal falls through. None of this requires asking your buyer to waive their normal inspection, appraisal, or loan contingency periods, which is a separate and riskier conversation.

Locally, Movoto's Woodland Hills page, updated October 28, 2026, shows 378 active listings, 27 new listings, a median sale price of $1,299,000 for homes that closed in September 2026, and an average 50 days on market, up from 44 a year ago. With inventory sitting where it is, a seller whose primary deal falls through generally isn't starting a search from scratch. Frequently Asked Questions

How often does a buyer's financing actually fall through after I accept an offer in Woodland Hills?

No source publishes that figure specifically for Woodland Hills. Nationally, NAR's September 2026 REALTORS Confidence Survey found 7% of contracts were terminated in the last three months, and 16% had a delayed settlement.

What's the difference between a terminated contract and a delayed settlement?

A terminated contract ends before closing, the sale doesn't happen. A delayed settlement still closes, just later than the original date. NAR's survey tracks both separately: 7% terminated versus 16% delayed in the most recent three-month period.

Does an appraisal problem cause most of these delays?

It's one documented cause. NAR's survey found 5% of all contracts were delayed specifically because of appraisal issues, a figure that's stayed close to 5-6% for the past year. The survey doesn't separately report what share of delays or terminations trace to financing or buyer qualification issues.

Should I require cash offers to avoid this risk?

Not necessarily. Only 27% of buyers nationally paid cash as of September 2026, so requiring cash-only would cut out roughly three-quarters of your potential buyer pool. A well-qualified financed buyer with a real pre-approval is a normal, usually reliable offer.

What's a backup offer, and is it hard to set up?

A backup offer is a second buyer's offer, documented and accepted in backup position under the standard California purchase agreement, that becomes active automatically if the primary contract terminates. It doesn't obligate the second buyer to anything beyond their own offer terms, and it means you aren't restarting your search for a buyer from zero.

Does keeping a backup offer mean I have to stop marketing my home?

No. Marketing typically pauses once you're in contract regardless of whether a backup offer exists. The backup offer is about what happens next if the primary deal falls through, not about keeping your home actively listed while under contract.
