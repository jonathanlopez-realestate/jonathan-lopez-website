Can I Buy a Woodland Hills Home Entirely Remotely If I'm Relocating From Out of State?

Jonathan Lopez, Woodland Hills Realtor | Jim Sandoval Group | CA DRE #02282844 · Updated September 2026

A lot of relocation clients moving to Woodland Hills come from states where a real estate closing can happen almost entirely on a laptop: sign the deed over video, click through the closing package, never sit across from anyone in person. It's a reasonable question to bring to a California purchase, and the honest answer is that California isn't there yet, at least not for the documents that actually transfer ownership.

How a California Closing Actually Works

California is what's known as an escrow state rather than an attorney state. Instead of a real estate attorney running the closing, a licensed, neutral escrow officer manages the paperwork, holds the funds, and coordinates recording the sale with the county. Neither the buyer's nor the seller's agent controls those funds or documents; the escrow company does, and it doesn't represent either side. A lot of that process already happens without anyone in a room together. Disclosures, contracts, and routine paperwork move through e-signature platforms daily in California real estate, and there's no legal requirement to physically visit an office for most of it.

Where Remote Breaks Down: The Notary

The part that trips up relocating buyers is the notary requirement on documents that actually get recorded with the county, primarily the grant deed and, for financed purchases, the deed of trust. California passed the Online Notarization Act (SB 696) in September 2023, authorizing California notaries to eventually perform notarizations over live video, the way notaries in many other states already can. But the law was written to phase in over time, and the core piece, the actual remote online notarization system, isn't active yet. California's Secretary of State has until January 1, 2030 to finish building the required technology infrastructure, or until it's certified complete, whichever happens first. Until that switch flips, a California notary has to physically watch you sign your name, in the same room, with a pen. There's no workaround for the deed itself.

What This Means in Practice for a Relocating Buyer

If your closing date lands before you've physically relocated, or before you can get back into town, this is the moment that gets missed in the general excitement of a move. Some escrow companies can arrange a mobile notary to meet a buyer wherever they currently are, but that still means scheduling an actual in-person appointment somewhere, not a video call at midnight the night before a flight. It's a solvable logistics problem, but only if it's planned for early rather than discovered the week of closing.

Timeline Expectations, Separate From the Notary Question

Aside from the notarization issue, California escrow itself has a fairly predictable rhythm. A financed purchase typically takes 30 to 45 days from an accepted offer to a recorded close, covering loan underwriting, appraisal, and the standard contingency periods. An all-cash purchase can move much faster, sometimes 7 to 21 days, since there's no lender in the process to satisfy. Either way, a relocating buyer juggling a job start date, a lease ending in another state, and a moving truck benefits from knowing these numbers going in rather than assuming a closing will match the faster, fully digital timelines common in some other markets.

Where Woodland Hills Stands Right Now

None of this changes based on the property itself, it's a statewide process. But it's worth anchoring to the actual local market a relocating buyer is stepping into: as of Movoto's September 7, 2026 update, Woodland Hills has 382 active listings and 16 new listings, with 72 of those already price-reduced. The median sale price is $1,348,500 based on August 2026 closings, and homes are taking an average of 48 days to sell, up slightly from 45 days a year ago. That's a separate question from the two other relocation angles already covered here: whether everyone is really leaving California, and where inbound relocation buyers are actually coming from. Those two are about where people are moving from. This one is about what actually happens once you've decided to buy here.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can any part of a California home purchase happen remotely?

Yes, most of it. Contracts, disclosures, and routine paperwork are commonly handled through e-signature and video calls in California real estate. The exception is the notarized documents that get recorded with the county, primarily the deed, which currently require an in-person signature witnessed by a notary.

Why can't I just use remote online notarization like I did in my last state?

California passed a law authorizing it (SB 696, signed September 2023), but the system isn't built yet. State law gives the Secretary of State until January 1, 2030 to complete the required technology, so until then, California notaries can't perform that kind of remote video notarization for real estate documents.

Does using an attorney instead of an escrow company change this?

No. California doesn't use attorney-run closings the way some other states do. Real estate purchases here go through a licensed escrow company regardless, and the in-person notary requirement on recorded documents applies either way.

How long should I expect escrow to take once I'm under contract?

Plan for 30 to 45 days for a financed purchase, covering loan underwriting, appraisal, and standard contingency periods. An all-cash purchase can close faster, often in 7 to 21 days, since there's no lender underwriting involved.

What if I can't be in California for the in-person notary signing?

Some escrow companies can arrange a mobile notary to meet you wherever you currently are, but it still requires an actual scheduled in-person appointment, not a video call. This needs to be arranged ahead of time, not discovered the week of closing.

Is any of this specific to Woodland Hills, or is it true everywhere in California?

The notarization and escrow-process rules are statewide, not specific to Woodland Hills or the San Fernando Valley. What is specific to Woodland Hills is the actual market you're stepping into: current listings, pricing, and pace, which is worth reviewing separately from the closing mechanics themselves.

Closing logistics are one piece of relocating here. The other is budgeting correctly: out-of-state buyers cannot bring their old property tax base with them, even if you would have qualified for that kind of portability moving within California.

If a job is moving you here on a tight schedule, let's build a closing plan that actually accounts for the notary step instead of discovering it the week of your move.
