Should I Buy My Next Woodland Hills Home Before I Sell My Current One?

Jonathan Lopez, Woodland Hills Realtor · Jim Sandoval Group · CA DRE #02282844 · Updated October 2026

Selling a home in Woodland Hills usually comes with a second, less-discussed decision: what to do about the home you are moving into. Do you sell your current place first and then shop, or find your next home first and figure out the rest later? There is no universally right answer, but there is a real cost to each path, and most sellers do not hear the actual numbers until they are already deep into one option.

Buying before you sell: bridge loans. A bridge loan is short-term financing, typically structured for six to twelve months, that lets you use the equity in your current home to close on the next one before your current home has sold. Per effectiveagents.com's 2026 breakdown, bridge loans generally carry interest rates of 8.5% to 11%, plus an origination fee of roughly 1% to 2.5% of the loan amount. On a meaningful loan balance, that combination adds up quickly over even a few months, so a bridge loan tends to make the most sense when you are confident your current home will sell fast, or when you need to make a non-contingent offer in a competitive situation and simply cannot wait.

A cheaper alternative: the HELOC. A home equity line of credit against your current home is generally less expensive than a bridge loan, with rates commonly in the 7% to 9% variable range and no origination fee in many cases, according to the same 2026 data. The catch is timing: most lenders will not approve or fund a HELOC once your home is actively listed for sale, since a pending sale complicates their collateral position. If a HELOC is part of your plan, it typically needs to be arranged before you put your home on the market, not after.

Selling first: the contingent offer. The other direction is to sell your current home first, then write an offer on your next one that is contingent on your current sale closing. This costs nothing upfront and avoids carrying two mortgages or a bridge loan balance at all. The tradeoff is leverage: a contingent offer is structurally the weakest offer a seller of your next home can receive, since it depends on an event (your own sale) outside that seller's control. Sellers in a competitive market often prefer, or require, a kick-out clause, which lets them keep marketing their home and accept a better offer if one comes in, giving you a limited window to remove your contingency or lose the deal.

A fourth option: the rent-back. Rather than choosing purely between carrying two mortgages or accepting a weak contingent offer, some Woodland Hills sellers negotiate a rent-back agreement: you sell your current home, close on the sale, and then lease it back from the buyer for an agreed period, often 30 to 60 days, while you finalize your own purchase. This converts the timing problem into a short-term rental arrangement instead of a bridge loan or a contingency, and it only works if your buyer is open to it, which is more likely in a market with more inventory and less urgency on the buyer's side. That is a real factor in Woodland Hills right now. Movoto's Woodland Hills city page, last updated October 30, 2026, shows 379 active listings with 83 of them, or roughly 22%, already price-reduced. The median sale price is $1,299,000 as of September, with homes averaging 50 days on market, up from 44 a year ago. A market with more price reductions and a longer average time on market tends to be one where buyers (including the buyer of your home) have more room to say yes to a rent-back request, since they are less likely to be competing for your house against a backup offer the moment you ask for extra time.

None of these four paths is automatically correct. A bridge loan protects your ability to make a strong, non-contingent offer but costs real money in interest and fees for however long you carry it. A HELOC is cheaper but has to be lined up before listing. A contingent offer costs nothing but weakens your position on the home you want. A rent-back can bridge the gap without new debt, but depends entirely on your buyer's willingness and your market's current conditions. The right choice depends on your actual home equity, how firm your timeline is, and how competitive the market is for the home you are trying to buy.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is a bridge loan, and what does it cost?

A bridge loan is short-term financing, usually six to twelve months, that lets you use your current home's equity to buy your next home before the current one sells. It typically runs 8.5% to 11% interest with a 1% to 2.5% origination fee (effectiveagents.com, 2026).

Is a HELOC cheaper than a bridge loan?

Generally yes, with rates commonly in the 7% to 9% variable range and often no origination fee, but most lenders will not open a new HELOC once your home is actively listed for sale, so it has to be arranged beforehand.

What is a contingent offer, and why do sellers dislike it?

It is an offer to buy a new home that depends on your current home selling first. It costs nothing upfront but is a weaker offer, since the seller of the home you want is relying on an outside event (your sale) they cannot control.

What is a kick-out clause?

A provision in a contingent offer that lets the seller keep marketing their home and accept a better offer, giving you a limited window to remove your contingency (often by securing financing without the sale) or lose the deal.

What is a rent-back agreement, and when does it make sense?

After closing on your sale, you lease the home back from the buyer for a set period, often 30 to 60 days, buying time to close on your next purchase without a bridge loan. It depends on your buyer agreeing, which is more likely when buyers face less competition for the home.

How do I know which option fits my situation?

It depends on your home equity, how firm your moving timeline is, and how competitive the market is for your next home. A real comparison using your actual numbers, not a general rule, is the only way to answer it with confidence.
