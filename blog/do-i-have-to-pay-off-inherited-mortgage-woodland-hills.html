Do I Have to Pay Off the Mortgage When I Inherit a House in Woodland Hills?

Jonathan Lopez, Woodland Hills Realtor | Jim Sandoval Group | CA DRE #02282844 · Updated August 2026

It's one of the first questions that comes up after inheriting a home in west Woodland Hills or anywhere in the surrounding San Fernando Valley: what happens to the mortgage that's still on it? Most people assume they have to pay it off, refinance into their own name, or qualify for a new loan right away. For a standard mortgage, none of that is true. For a reverse mortgage, the rules are different, and the timeline is real.

Regular Mortgages: the Loan Doesn't Accelerate Just Because You Inherited

Mortgages typically include a "due-on-sale" clause that lets the lender demand full repayment if the property changes hands. But the federal Garn-St. Germain Depository Institutions Act of 1982 carves out an exception for exactly this situation: when a home transfers to a relative because the borrower died, the lender cannot invoke that clause to call the loan due. As the heir, you're generally allowed to keep making payments under the same terms, interest rate, and payoff schedule the original owner had, without refinancing or requalifying.

California Adds Its Own Layer of Protection

Under California Civil Code Section 2920.7, an heir who qualifies as a "successor in interest" has the right to get information about the loan directly from the servicer and gets the same foreclosure protections a borrower would have, even though the heir never signed the original note. In practice, this means notifying the servicer of the death, then providing a death certificate (usually within 30 days of that notice) and documentation proving your relationship to the deceased and your interest in the property (typically within 90 days). Once that's done, the servicer is required to treat you as though you were a borrower on the loan for purposes of communication and loss-mitigation options.

Reverse Mortgages Work on a Different Clock

A Home Equity Conversion Mortgage (HECM), the FHA-insured product that makes up the large majority of reverse mortgages, becomes due and payable when the last surviving borrower dies or permanently moves out. Heirs typically get about 6 months from that point to decide what to do (sell the home, pay off the balance and keep it, or walk away) with up to two 90-day extensions available if the estate is actively marketing the property for sale, for a maximum of roughly 12 months. Because HECMs are non-recourse loans backed by FHA insurance, heirs are never personally responsible for a shortfall if the home ends up selling for less than the loan balance.

What Doesn't Change Either Way

Whether the loan is a standard mortgage or a reverse mortgage, payments (or, for a HECM, the underlying obligations like property taxes and insurance) still have to be kept current while the estate works through probate, which in California averages 12 to 18 months. A lender's willingness not to accelerate the loan isn't the same as a pause on the loan itself. Missing payments during that window can put the home into default and foreclosure regardless of who eventually inherits it or what they intend to do with it.

None of this changes the decision of whether to sell, keep, or move into an inherited home, and it's a separate question entirely from what happens when co-inheriting siblings disagree about selling. But it does change the timeline pressure. A regular mortgage generally buys time to make that decision without a forced refinance. A reverse mortgage puts a real clock on it. Either way, knowing which situation applies, and getting an accurate read on what the home is actually worth, is the starting point before deciding anything else.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do I have to refinance an inherited house to keep the existing mortgage?

No, not for a standard mortgage. Under the federal Garn-St. Germain Act, the lender cannot call the loan due or require a refinance just because the property transferred to a relative at death. You can typically keep making payments under the original terms.

What do I need to do to take over payments on an inherited mortgage?

Notify the loan servicer that the borrower has died, then provide a death certificate (usually within 30 days) and documentation proving your relationship and interest in the property (usually within 90 days). This establishes you as a successor in interest under California and federal rules.

How is a reverse mortgage different from a regular mortgage after the owner dies?

A reverse mortgage (HECM) becomes due when the last borrower dies or moves out, unlike a regular mortgage. Heirs get about 6 months to sell, pay it off, or refinance, with up to two 90-day extensions if the home is being actively marketed.

Can I owe more than the house is worth on an inherited reverse mortgage?

No. HECMs are FHA-insured, non-recourse loans. If the home sells for less than the loan balance, the FHA insurance covers the difference. Heirs are never personally on the hook for the shortfall.

What happens if mortgage payments aren't made while the house is in probate?

The loan can go into default and move toward foreclosure, the same as it would for any borrower, regardless of who eventually inherits the property. Probate itself doesn't pause the obligation to keep the loan current.

Where can I get help figuring out which situation applies to my inherited home?

Start with the loan servicer directly, they can confirm whether the loan is a standard mortgage or a reverse mortgage and what's required. A probate attorney can advise on the estate side.
