Do I Need a Termite and Pest Clearance Report Before I Sell My Woodland Hills Home?

Jonathan Lopez, Woodland Hills Realtor · Jim Sandoval Group · CA DRE #02282844 · Updated November 2026

Most Woodland Hills sellers think about their home inspection and assume that covers everything a buyer or lender could ask for. It usually does not. A separate document, the wood destroying pest and organism inspection report, shows up in almost every California real estate transaction, and it carries its own rules about who pays and what has to be fixed before closing.

California's Structural Pest Control Board requires licensed inspectors to split their findings into two categories. Section 1 items are evidence of current, active infestation or infection: live drywood or subterranean termites, active fungus or dry rot, or damage already caused by wood destroying organisms. These typically require correction, treatment, repair, or both, before a lender will fund a loan secured by the property. Section 2 items are different: conditions that create a risk of future infestation if ignored, things like wood-to-soil contact, excess moisture, or a leaking faucet near a wall, but with no current damage. Section 2 items do not usually require immediate correction, and many buyers and sellers simply note them and move on.

Who pays for any of this is not fixed by law. The C.A.R. Residential Purchase Agreement includes a specific Wood Destroying Pest Inspection and Allocation of Cost provision that lets buyer and seller negotiate, in the contract itself, who orders the report and who pays for Section 1 corrective work versus Section 2 corrective work. In practice, many Woodland Hills sellers agree to pay for the report and for Section 1 clearance as part of getting a clean close, since an uncleared Section 1 finding can hold up loan funding regardless of who technically agreed to pay. But none of this is automatic, and it is worth deciding your position on this before you are negotiating it inside a multiple-offer situation.

VA loans add a rule that is easy to miss. The Department of Veterans Affairs generally does not allow the veteran-buyer to pay for the termite or pest report themselves, and California is not one of the handful of states where VA makes an exception for buyer-paid pest reports. If you accept an offer from a buyer using VA financing, plan on the report, and any required Section 1 clearance, landing on your side of the closing statement as a practical matter, not just a negotiating preference. FHA loans are more situational: FHA itself does not automatically require a pest report on every transaction, but if the appraiser notes visible evidence of infestation or wood damage during the appraisal, the lender can require a clearance before closing. Conventional loans are the most negotiable of the three: pest inspection is typically a buyer-requested contingency item rather than an automatic lender requirement, though many conventional buyers request one anyway given how common drywood termites are in this climate.

Cost is the other piece worth planning for ahead of time. The inspection itself is relatively inexpensive, typically in the $75 to $325 range for a standard single-family home. What varies enormously is the cost of actually clearing a Section 1 finding. A small, localized issue might mean a few hundred dollars in spot treatment or minor wood repair. A full structure drywood termite infestation can mean tenting and fumigation, plus structural repair to damaged framing or siding, running well into the thousands depending on the extent of damage. There is no honest single number to quote here, which is exactly why a licensed structural pest control company, not a blog post or a listing agent's guess, should price out any actual finding on your specific home.

The practical move for a Woodland Hills seller is to get ahead of this rather than let it surface as a surprise mid-escrow. Ordering your own pest inspection before you list, the same way some sellers order a general pre-listing inspection, gives you time to get competitive bids on any Section 1 corrective work, decide whether to fix it yourself or credit the buyer, and avoid a last-minute scramble that can cost you leverage in a negotiation. On Woodland Hills' current market (Movoto: $1,300,000 median sale price, 368 active listings, 54 day average days on market), buyers and their lenders are not going away from this requirement, and knowing your numbers before an offer arrives puts you in a stronger position than finding them out after.

If you are getting ready to list and want a clear picture of what a pest report is likely to turn up, and what that could mean for your net proceeds, that is exactly the kind of planning conversation worth having before your home goes on the market.

Frequently Asked Questions

What's the difference between a Section 1 and Section 2 pest finding?

Section 1 means evidence of current, active infestation, damage, or fungus, generally requiring correction before a lender will fund the loan. Section 2 means a condition that could lead to infestation later (like wood-to-soil contact) but no current damage, and it typically does not require immediate correction.

Who has to pay for the pest report and any repairs, the buyer or the seller?

It's negotiable. The C.A.R. purchase agreement includes a specific provision letting buyer and seller agree on who orders the report and who pays for Section 1 versus Section 2 work. There is no fixed California rule assigning this to one side automatically, except for VA buyers, see below.

Is this different for a VA loan?

Yes. California does not allow VA buyers to pay for the pest report themselves, so if you accept a VA offer, plan for the report and any required Section 1 clearance to fall on your side of the transaction as a practical matter.

Do FHA and conventional loans require a pest inspection too?

FHA doesn't automatically require one, but if the appraiser flags visible pest damage, the lender can require clearance. Conventional loans generally treat it as a buyer-requested contingency rather than an automatic requirement, though many buyers request one given how common drywood termites are locally.

How much does this actually cost?

The inspection itself typically runs $75 to $325. Clearing a Section 1 finding varies enormously, from a small spot treatment to full structure fumigation plus structural repair, so a licensed pest control company needs to quote your specific home rather than relying on a general estimate.

Should I get this done before I list, or wait for the buyer to request it?

Getting ahead of it with your own pre-listing pest inspection lets you shop corrective work, decide how to handle it, and avoid a surprise that costs you negotiating leverage mid-escrow.
