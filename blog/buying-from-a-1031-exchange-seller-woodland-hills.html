I'm Buying a Woodland Hills Home From a Seller Doing a 1031 Exchange. Does That Change My Contract?

Jonathan Lopez, Woodland Hills Realtor · Jim Sandoval Group · CA DRE #02282844 · Updated November 2026

If you're under contract on a Woodland Hills home and your agent mentions the seller is "doing a 1031," it's worth understanding what that actually means for you, because the honest answer is: less than most buyers assume.

A 1031 exchange, named for the IRC section that authorizes it, lets an investment property owner defer capital gains tax on a sale by reinvesting the proceeds into another "like-kind" property instead of cashing out. It is exclusively a tool for the seller's own tax situation. It has nothing to do with your loan, your title, or the price you negotiated.

The mechanical requirement that makes this work is that the seller can never actually touch the sale proceeds. If the money lands in the seller's own bank account for even a moment, the IRS treats that as "constructive receipt" and the whole exchange fails, triggering the full tax bill the seller was trying to defer. To avoid that, the seller hires a Qualified Intermediary (QI), typically a company like IPX1031, Asset Preservation, or First American Exchange, that holds the proceeds at closing and later disburses them toward the seller's replacement purchase. The seller pays for this service. You don't.

Where you do show up in the paperwork is a short "cooperation clause" or assignment-of-rights addendum, which is standard boilerplate in almost every exchange transaction. It simply documents that the seller is assigning their rights (not their obligations) in your purchase contract to the Qualified Intermediary, and gives you written notice of that assignment. Under the safe-harbor rules in Treasury Regulation Section 1.1031(k)-1(g), signing this and acknowledging the notice does not make you a party to the exchange, does not expose you to any of the seller's tax risk, and does not require you to do anything differently in escrow. It is a one-page acknowledgment, not a renegotiation of your deal.

Here's the part that can actually work in your favor. A seller in the middle of a 1031 exchange is on a real clock set by federal law, not a negotiating tactic. From the day your purchase closes (because that's the day the seller's old property is officially "relinquished"), the seller has exactly 45 days to formally identify a replacement property and 180 days total to close on it. Both deadlines run concurrently, start on your closing date, and do not get extended for weekends or holidays. If the seller misses either one, the entire deferred capital gains tax becomes due.

That kind of deadline pressure tends to make a seller unusually motivated to protect a firm, predictable closing date. If you can offer a clean, well-qualified, certain closing timeline, pre-approved financing, a realistic inspection schedule, no unusual contingencies, you may find more room to negotiate on price or terms than you would with a seller who has no particular urgency.

What a 1031 exchange should never do is pressure you into giving up your own protections. Your inspection contingency, your appraisal contingency, and your loan contingency exist to protect you, and the seller's tax deadline is a problem for the seller and their intermediary to manage, not a reason for you to move faster than you're comfortable with. If an agent or seller frames a tight timeline as something you need to accommodate by waiving a contingency, that's a request worth pushing back on, not a requirement of the exchange itself.

It's also worth knowing that an exchange being "in progress" on the seller's side carries essentially no deal risk for you specific to the exchange itself. If the seller's exchange somehow falls apart after your closing (say, they can't find a replacement property in time), that is entirely the seller's tax consequence after the fact. Your purchase has already closed by then; it does not unwind or get reopened because the seller's exchange failed on their end.

Bottom line for a Woodland Hills buyer: expect to see and sign a one-page cooperation clause, expect zero cost or added risk from it, and recognize that a motivated 1031 seller can sometimes be a more flexible negotiating partner on everything except the closing date itself.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does a seller's 1031 exchange cost me anything as the buyer?

No. The seller hires and pays the Qualified Intermediary that handles the exchange mechanics. Your price, financing, title costs, and closing costs are unaffected.

Will I have to sign something unusual if my seller is exchanging?

Likely just a short cooperation clause or assignment-of-rights addendum, standard boilerplate that acknowledges the seller assigned their contract rights to a Qualified Intermediary. It doesn't change your obligations.

Does signing a 1031 cooperation clause make me part of the seller's exchange for tax purposes?

No. Safe-harbor rules under Treasury Regulation Section 1.1031(k)-1(g) specifically protect the other party to the transaction from being treated as part of the exchange just for acknowledging the assignment.

Can my seller force me to close faster because of their 1031 deadline?

No. Their 45-day identification and 180-day closing windows are their own deadlines to manage. You still keep your normal inspection, appraisal, and loan contingency periods; a tight exchange deadline is not a valid reason to ask you to waive them.

What happens to my purchase if the seller's exchange later falls through?

Nothing changes for you. Once your purchase has closed, it's complete regardless of whether the seller successfully identifies or closes on their own replacement property afterward.

Could a 1031 seller actually be easier to negotiate with?

Sometimes. A seller racing a real IRS deadline often values a clean, certain closing date highly, and may be more flexible on price or terms in exchange for that certainty, though every seller and situation is different.
