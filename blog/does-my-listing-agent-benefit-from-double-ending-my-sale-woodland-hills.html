If My Woodland Hills Listing Agent Also Brings the Buyer, Do I Actually Come Out Ahead?

Jonathan Lopez, Woodland Hills Realtor · Jim Sandoval Group · CA DRE #02282844 · Updated November 2026

Most Woodland Hills sellers think about their listing agent's commission as a fixed cost of doing business and move on. But one detail worth understanding before you sign is what happens if that same agent, or their brokerage, ends up representing the buyer too, a setup California law calls dual agency.

It is legal here, but it is not automatic or something an agent can simply decide on your behalf. Civil Code section 2079.17 requires the agent to disclose, in writing, as soon as practical, whether they are acting as a dual agent, and both you and the buyer have to confirm that in writing before or at the same time the contract is signed. Section 2079.21 then limits what the agent can share: without your express permission, they cannot tell the buyer your bottom line or motivation, and the same protection runs the other way for the buyer. A separate post on this site covers that disclosure mechanic in more depth from the buyer's side.

What that consent requirement is actually guarding against just became very visible. CalMatters reported this week, November 13, 2026, on a Sonoma County lawsuit against brokerage W Real Estate and its owner, Randy Waller, alleging that across at least five developments, hundreds of buyers were made to accept dual agency as a precondition of being allowed to submit an offer at all, not a choice they were free to decline. A jury trial is scheduled for May 2027. If the allegations hold up, that is not voluntary informed consent, it is consent in name only, which is precisely why the law requires the disclosure to be genuine and not boilerplate buried in paperwork.

There is also a real, measurable tradeoff for sellers to understand, separate from any lawsuit. The same reporting cited data showing buyers who had their own independent agent, rather than sharing the seller's agent, were roughly four times more likely to receive concessions or credits during negotiation, and when they did get one, it averaged about six times larger, $3,252 compared to $554 for buyers in a dual-agency arrangement. Read plainly, a buyer without dedicated representation tends to negotiate less aggressively against your asking price and terms. That can sound like good news for a seller in isolation, but it is worth being honest with yourself about why it happens and whether you are comfortable with the dynamic, rather than treating it as a free win.

None of this means dual agency is wrong for every transaction, plenty of Woodland Hills deals close cleanly this way with full, genuine consent on both sides. The point is to ask the question directly before you sign: does my agent intend to represent the buyer too, is that disclosure going to be real and specific rather than generic, and am I comfortable with how that affects both my own representation and the buyer's on the other side of my sale.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is dual agency, exactly?

It's when one agent, or two agents at the same brokerage, represent both the buyer and the seller in the same transaction. California allows it under Civil Code 2079.17, but only with written disclosure and informed consent from both parties.

Does my commission change if my listing agent also represents the buyer?

Not automatically; your listing agreement sets the commission structure regardless of who represents the buyer. What can change is the negotiating dynamic: data cited by CalMatters this week shows buyers without their own independent agent were about four times less likely to receive concessions, and got roughly six times less when they did.

Can an agent require dual agency as a condition of making an offer?

No. A pending Sonoma County lawsuit (W Real Estate, owner Randy Waller, jury trial scheduled May 2027) alleges exactly that happened across several developments, and it is being challenged as failing the legal requirement for voluntary, informed consent.

What can't a dual agent tell the other side?

Under Civil Code 2079.21, without your express written permission, a dual agent cannot tell the buyer your bottom-line price or motivation, and cannot tell you the buyer's maximum price or motivation either.

Should I refuse dual agency on my Woodland Hills listing?

Not necessarily. It's legal and can close cleanly when consent is genuine on both sides. The real question is whether the disclosure you're given is specific and voluntary, not generic paperwork, and whether you understand the concession-negotiation tradeoff described above before you agree.

What should I ask my listing agent before I sign?

Ask directly whether they or their brokerage currently represent, or expect to represent, any buyers likely to look at your home, and ask to see the actual written dual-agency disclosure form in advance rather than signing it for the first time at offer stage.
