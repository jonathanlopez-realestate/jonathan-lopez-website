Does My Woodland Hills Listing Agent Have to Show Me Every Offer, Even a Lowball One?

Jonathan Lopez, Woodland Hills Realtor · Jim Sandoval Group · CA DRE #02282844 · Updated November 2026

A question that comes up more often than sellers expect: does a Woodland Hills listing agent actually have to tell the seller about an offer that is obviously too low, or can the agent just handle it quietly and save everyone the conversation? It is a reasonable thing to wonder, especially in a market where a seller might assume their agent is using professional judgment to filter out offers that would just waste their time. The real answer is no, an agent does not get to make that call alone, and the reasoning behind that rule is worth understanding before you sign a listing agreement.

California Business and Professions Code Section 10176 gives the Department of Real Estate grounds to discipline a licensee for failing to present a written offer to their client in a timely way. That statute sits alongside a parallel duty described in reporting on California Civil Code Section 2079.23, which addresses an agent's obligation to present offers to the party they represent. Beyond state law, the National Association of REALTORS' Code of Ethics adds its own layer: Standard of Practice 1-6 requires REALTORS to submit offers and counteroffers "objectively and as quickly as possible," and Standard of Practice 1-7 requires a listing broker to keep submitting every offer and counteroffer to the seller "until closing or execution of a lease" unless the seller has waived that obligation in writing. On top of both of those, the MLS systems covering Woodland Hills (CRMLS and CLAW) carry their own rules, 9.4 and 9.5, requiring listing brokers to present offers "as soon as possible" and to keep submitting every offer and counteroffer to the seller unless the seller has instructed otherwise in writing.

Put those layers together and the pattern is consistent across all of them: a listing agent does not get to use personal judgment to decide an offer is not worth your time. The agent's job is delivery, not filtering. The only legitimate way to change that is for the seller to give written instructions, for example telling the agent not to present offers below a specific price or without a specific contingency structure. Until that written instruction exists, every offer that comes in on your home is supposed to land on your desk, lowball or not.

It is worth being honest about the sourcing here. California's own statute text at leginfo.legislature.ca.gov could not be fetched directly during this research (a 403 error, the same recurring access issue that has shown up on other legal-citation posts), so the specific Civil Code 2079.23 reference comes from Inman's industry reporting rather than a direct statute read. The Business and Professions Code 10176 disciplinary standard, the NAR Code of Ethics standards, and the CRMLS/CLAW MLS rules were each corroborated independently and point to the same underlying duty, which is why this post treats the rule itself as solid even though one citation traces through a secondary source rather than the statute's own text.

Why does this actually matter to a seller in today's Woodland Hills market? Homes here are currently averaging 54 days on market against a $1,300,000 median sale price, with 368 active listings competing for buyer attention (Movoto, citywide, accessed November 13, 2026). A 54-day average means plenty of listings sit long enough to draw at least one offer that feels disappointingly low before a better one shows up. If an agent were allowed to quietly screen those out, a seller could lose the chance to counter, to learn what the market is actually telling them about their price, or simply to negotiate a deal that works even if the opening number looked rough. The rule exists precisely so that decision stays with the person who owns the house, not the person representing it.

None of this means every offer deserves a long negotiation or that a seller has to respond to anything handed to them. It means the seller gets to see the offer and make that call themselves, with their agent's advice, rather than having that choice made for them before it ever reaches them. Frequently Asked Questions

Can my listing agent decide not to tell me about a lowball offer?

No. California Business and Professions Code Section 10176 gives the Department of Real Estate grounds to discipline an agent for failing to present a written offer, and NAR's Code of Ethics and the CRMLS/CLAW MLS rules require every written offer and counteroffer to reach the seller until closing.

Is there any way to tell my agent to stop bringing me offers below a certain price?

Yes, but it has to be in writing. A seller can give written instructions limiting what gets presented, for example a minimum price or required contingency terms, and the agent should keep that instruction on file. Without it, every offer comes to you.

Does this rule apply to verbal offers too, or only written ones?

The duty described across these sources centers on written offers and counteroffers. Verbal interest from a buyer typically needs to be put in writing before it becomes a formal offer an agent is obligated to present under these standards.

Why would an agent want to filter out a low offer in the first place?

Usually to save time, not out of bad intent. But the rule removes that judgment call from the agent's hands because the seller, not the agent, is the one who has to live with the decision to accept, counter, or reject.

Does a low offer in today's market mean something is wrong with my price?

Not necessarily, but it is worth paying attention to. Woodland Hills homes are averaging 54 days on market against a $1,300,000 median sale price right now (Movoto, citywide, accessed November 13, 2026), and a pattern of low offers across that kind of timeline can be useful pricing feedback.

What should I do if I think my agent sat on an offer without telling me?

Ask directly and in writing for a record of every offer received on your listing. If an agent failed to present a legitimate written offer without your prior written instruction, that is a Department of Real Estate complaint, not just a disagreement to work out informally.
