Does a Hillside or Valley View Actually Add Value to My Woodland Hills Home?

Jonathan Lopez, Woodland Hills Realtor · Jim Sandoval Group · CA DRE #02282844 · Updated November 2026

If you live on one of Woodland Hills' hillside streets with an actual view of the Valley, or even a partial glimpse past the rooftops, it's a fair question whether that view is worth anything in a sale, or whether it's just a nice thing to look at that an appraiser ignores. The honest answer is that it can be worth real money, but not through any flat formula, and not without real comparable sales to back it up.

Appraisers working on a loan-backed sale don't get to eyeball a view and assign it a number. Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac's Uniform Appraisal Dataset requires every appraisal to rate the overall view of the subject property, and each comparable sale used, as Neutral, Beneficial, or Adverse. That rating itself doesn't produce a dollar figure. It tells the appraiser which direction to look when pulling comps: if your home is rated Beneficial for its view, the appraiser is expected to find comparable sales with a similar view, or to adjust using comps that differ specifically on that point, so the final value reflects what buyers in that market actually paid for the view itself, isolated from everything else about the property.

A persistent myth worth clearing up directly: a lot of real estate content online still claims Fannie Mae caps adjustments at 15% net and 25% gross per comparable, and that an appraiser can't exceed those limits. That guideline existed, but Fannie Mae removed it from its Selling Guide around 2015. The current guide (Section B4-1.3-09, last revised June 2025) explicitly states Fannie Mae "does not have specific limitations or guidelines associated with net or gross adjustments," and instead requires every adjustment, view included, to be "fact-based and objective," supported by real market data, not a percentage ceiling. If an appraiser's view adjustment looks unusually large, the question to ask isn't whether it broke a cap that no longer exists; it's whether the comps actually support it.

So what does a view realistically add? There's no Woodland Hills-specific study that answers that with a hard number, and this post isn't going to invent one. What does exist is national appraisal-industry data giving a general sense of scale: mountain or hillside views are commonly cited as adding 3% to 10% to a home's value, city-light views 5% to 15%, and golf course views 3% to 8%, with the top of each range reserved for the most open, unobstructed, and exclusive version of that view. These are industry-wide figures pulled from appraisal-firm commentary, not from a controlled study of San Fernando Valley sales specifically, so treat them as a sense of direction rather than a quote. The same logic runs in reverse: an Adverse view rating, for something like power lines, a busy arterial, or a direct look into a neighbor's yard, can work against value the same way a Beneficial one works in its favor, and an appraiser is expected to document that too.

Locally, 91367's current numbers (Movoto, accessed 2026-11-10, search-snapshot pull; direct page fetch returned a 403 error this session) show a $1,199,000 median list price across 198 active listings, 18 new listings, a $603 median price per square foot, 41 days on market on average (up from 33 a year ago), and 46 of those active listings carrying a price reduction. None of those figures isolate view premiums specifically; they're the broader zip-level backdrop a view premium would sit on top of.

If your home has a genuine, documentable view and you're trying to figure out what it's realistically adding before you set a list price, or you're buying and want to know whether a seller's asking price is pricing the view accurately or just hoping it is, I can pull comparable sales that isolate the view itself rather than guessing from a percentage range.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does my Woodland Hills home automatically get more value just for having a view?

No. An appraiser has to support any view-related adjustment with actual comparable sales showing buyers paid more for a similar view; a view alone doesn't trigger an automatic percentage increase.

What's the difference between a "Beneficial" and "Neutral" view rating?

These are standardized Uniform Appraisal Dataset ratings every appraisal must assign to the subject property and each comparable sale. "Beneficial" means the view positively affects value and marketability; "Neutral" means it doesn't meaningfully move either way; "Adverse" means it works against value, for example a view dominated by power lines or a busy street.

Is it true that appraisers can't adjust more than 15% or 25% for any one feature, including a view?

That cap existed in Fannie Mae's older guidance but was removed around 2015. Fannie Mae's current Selling Guide states explicitly that there is no specific limit on net or gross adjustments; every adjustment just has to be supported by real market data.

Roughly how much could a hillside or Valley view add to my home's value?

There's no Woodland Hills-specific figure, but national appraisal-industry ranges suggest mountain or hillside views commonly add 3% to 10%, city-light views 5% to 15%, and golf course views 3% to 8%, depending on how open and exclusive the view is.

Can a bad view actually lower my appraisal?

Yes. An "Adverse" view rating, for something like power lines, a busy arterial, or looking directly into a neighbor's yard, can be documented the same way a beneficial view is, just working against value instead of for it.

Does an online estimate like Zillow's account for my view the way an appraiser does?

No. Automated estimates generally don't inspect or rate a property's actual view; an appraiser visiting in person and pulling comps with a similar UAD view rating is a fundamentally different process, which is part of why online estimates and real appraisals can land far apart on a view-heavy property.
