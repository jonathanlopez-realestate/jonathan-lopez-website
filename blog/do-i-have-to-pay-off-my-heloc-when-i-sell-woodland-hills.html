I Have a HELOC on My Woodland Hills Home. Do I Have to Pay It Off Completely to Sell?

Jonathan Lopez, Woodland Hills Realtor · Jim Sandoval Group · CA DRE #02282844 · Updated November 2026

A good number of Woodland Hills homeowners used a home equity line of credit at some point, for a remodel, to pay down higher-rate debt, or just as a standing source of flexible cash. That's a completely normal thing to carry into a sale. What catches sellers off guard is assuming it works like a credit card balance they can pay down on their own schedule after moving. It doesn't. A HELOC is secured against the house itself, and that changes everything about how it has to be handled at closing.

Why it can't just transfer or stay open. A HELOC is a lien recorded against the property, not a personal loan tied to you as a borrower. When you sell, the buyer is purchasing the home free and clear of your liens, so your HELOC lender's claim against the property has to be satisfied and released before title can legally transfer. The line can't move with you to a new house, and it can't sit open against a home you no longer own. If you want a HELOC on your next home, you reapply for a new one secured against that property.

The actual payoff order at closing. This is the part that surprises sellers who assume they get a lump check and handle the HELOC themselves afterward. The title company or escrow holder manages the whole disbursement directly from your sale proceeds: your first mortgage gets paid first, then your HELOC balance (including any interest and fees that have accrued), and only after both liens are cleared does any remaining money come to you as net proceeds. You never write a separate check to the HELOC lender, and you don't have discretion to pay it off later, the payoff happens as part of the closing transaction itself, before you get your own money.

What happens if the math doesn't work. The scenario worth planning for honestly is being underwater, where your first mortgage plus your HELOC balance adds up to close to, or more than, what your home will actually sell for. If that's the situation, you generally have a few real options: bring outside funds to closing to cover the shortfall yourself, ask your HELOC lender to approve a short sale (where the lender agrees to accept less than the full balance as payment in full, which requires lender sign-off and can take weeks or months to process), negotiate converting the unpaid HELOC balance into an unsecured personal loan with the lender, or hold off on selling until your equity position improves. None of these are decisions to make at the closing table, they all take real lead time, which is exactly why this is worth figuring out before you list, not during escrow.

The fee that's easy to miss. Separate from the balance itself, many HELOC agreements include an early-closure or prepayment fee if the line is paid off and closed within the first few years of opening it. This fee doesn't show up in a simple "balance owed" estimate, and it can meaningfully change your expected net proceeds if you don't account for it. Before you estimate what you'll walk away with, call your HELOC lender directly and ask for your current full payoff amount, including any early-termination fee, not just the outstanding balance showing on your online statement.

How this interacts with your overall net proceeds. This post covers what happens to an existing HELOC specifically. It's a separate question from the standard selling costs (commission, transfer tax, escrow and title fees) covered in the general walk-away-number math for Woodland Hills sellers. A HELOC payoff comes out of the same pool of sale proceeds as those other costs, so a seller carrying a sizable HELOC balance needs both pieces together, the standard cost percentages and the actual payoff quote from their HELOC lender, to know their real number before committing to a listing date.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do I have to pay off my HELOC completely when I sell my Woodland Hills home?

Yes. A HELOC is a lien against the property, so it has to be paid in full, along with any accrued interest and fees, before the sale can close and title can transfer to the buyer. It cannot stay open or transfer with you.

Who actually pays off the HELOC, and when?

The title company or escrow holder handles it as part of the closing disbursement, paying your first mortgage, then your HELOC balance, directly from your sale proceeds, before any remaining money comes to you. You don't write a separate payoff check yourself.

What if I owe more on my mortgage and HELOC combined than my home will sell for?

That's an underwater position. Your main options are bringing outside funds to closing to cover the gap, requesting a lender-approved short sale, negotiating the remaining HELOC balance into an unsecured personal loan, or waiting until your equity improves before listing.

Are there fees for closing out a HELOC early?

Possibly. Many HELOC agreements include an early-closure or prepayment fee within the first few years of opening the line, separate from the balance itself. Call your lender for your exact current payoff figure, including any such fee, before estimating your net proceeds.

Does this change my commission or other selling costs?

No, a HELOC payoff is a separate line item from commission, transfer tax, and escrow/title fees. All of these come out of the same sale proceeds, so a seller carrying a HELOC needs both the standard cost percentages and their actual lender payoff quote to know their true net number.

How do I find out my exact HELOC payoff amount before I list?

Call your HELOC lender directly and request a current payoff statement, which will include your outstanding balance, accrued interest, and any early-termination fee. Get this number before you commit to a listing date, not after an offer is already in hand.
