How Much Can I Actually Ask the Seller to Cover in Closing Costs on My Woodland Hills Purchase?

Jonathan Lopez, Woodland Hills Realtor · Jim Sandoval Group · CA DRE #02282844 · Updated November 2026

Buyers negotiating a Woodland Hills purchase frequently ask for help with closing costs, but a surprising number assume the limit is whatever they can talk a seller into, or whatever number a friend's deal used. The real cap isn't negotiable in that sense; it's set by whichever loan program is financing the purchase, and it varies more than most buyers expect.

Conventional loans tie the cap to your down payment. Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac set what's called an interested party contribution (IPC) limit, and the percentage depends on how much the buyer is putting down. Put down less than 10%, and the seller can contribute up to 3% of the sale price toward closing costs. Put down 10% up to just under 25%, and that rises to 6%. Put down 25% or more, and it rises again to 9%. Buying the home as an investment property, rather than a primary or second home, caps the contribution at a flat 2% no matter how much the buyer puts down.

FHA loans use a single, more generous number. Rather than tying the cap to down payment, FHA allows interested parties to contribute up to 6% of the sale price toward closing costs, prepaid expenses, discount points, and other financing concessions. For a buyer putting down the FHA minimum, that 6% flat cap is often more generous than the 3% a low-down-payment conventional buyer would get.

VA loans split the cost categories in a way that catches buyers off guard. The VA's 4% cap doesn't apply to everything a seller might pay. It applies specifically to what VA classifies as "concessions," things like paying off a buyer's existing debts, prepaid property taxes and homeowners insurance, excess discount points, builder incentives, and the VA funding fee. Ordinary closing costs, the appraisal fee, loan origination charges, title and recording fees, are not concessions under VA's rules and are not subject to that 4% limit at all. A seller can pay those separately, in full, on top of whatever is contributed under the 4% concession cap. Buyers (and sometimes their agents) who assume the whole closing-cost conversation is capped at 4% on a VA loan are leaving money on the table.

USDA loans land in between. USDA allows seller contributions of up to 6% of the sale price toward a buyer's reasonable closing costs, similar in structure to FHA's flat percentage.

The rule that applies no matter which program you're using. Every one of these caps is a ceiling, not a guarantee, and all of them share one restriction: the contribution can never exceed what the buyer actually owes in closing costs. A seller can't contribute more than the real costs just to hand a buyer extra cash, and none of a seller contribution, under any program, can be applied toward the buyer's down payment or required financial reserves. The number on paper is a maximum, and the real-dollar amount still has to match real line items on the closing disclosure.

Why this matters when you're writing an offer. Asking for a seller credit that exceeds your loan program's actual cap doesn't just get rejected at the negotiating table; if it's written into the contract and the appraisal or underwriting catches it later, it can hold up or restructure the deal days before closing. Knowing the real number for your specific loan, down payment, and property type before you write an offer means you're negotiating with an accurate ask instead of guessing and hoping underwriting doesn't flag it later.

Discount points count against these caps too. A rate buydown, whether a temporary one that lowers your payment for the first year or two or a permanent one for the life of the loan, is typically purchased with discount points, and those points are explicitly included in the FHA, conventional, and USDA percentage caps described above. On a VA loan, ordinary discount points up to 2% generally don't count as a concession, but anything beyond that is treated as excess points and does fall under the 4% concession cap. A buyer who wants both a rate buydown and help with ordinary closing costs needs to add the two together and check the total against the real cap for their program, not treat them as separate buckets.

How the credit actually gets structured matters. A seller credit toward closing costs is written into the purchase contract as a specific dollar amount or percentage, not just verbally agreed to, and it has to be disclosed and reflected on the closing disclosure the lender prepares before closing. Some buyers and agents try to build the ask into a higher sale price instead, raising the contract price and having the seller credit back the difference, but the appraisal still has to support the full inflated contract price for the loan to go through at that price. If the appraisal comes in at the lower, real value instead, the deal can require renegotiation regardless of how the credit was originally structured. A credit asked for directly, within the real cap for the loan program, avoids that extra point of failure.

Who actually benefits from knowing this before you shop. A buyer stretching to afford a Woodland Hills purchase at a 25%+ down payment conventional loan has real room, up to 9%, to ask for meaningful closing-cost help if the seller is willing. A buyer putting down the minimum on an FHA loan has a flat 6% regardless of down payment, which is often more generous than a low-down conventional buyer's 3%. Knowing which bucket you're actually in changes what's realistic to ask for before you're sitting across from a seller's counteroffer.

Ask for it in the initial offer, not after. A seller credit request works best written into the purchase agreement from the start, alongside the price, rather than introduced later as a renegotiation after inspection or appraisal. Sellers who already know the total ask, price plus credit, are weighing one clear number; a credit request that shows up mid-escrow reads differently to a seller and tends to draw more resistance even when it's well within the loan program's actual cap.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is the seller contribution limit the same for every type of loan?

No. Conventional loans tie the limit to your down payment (3%, 6%, or 9%), FHA and USDA use a flat 6%, and VA loans cap only specific "concession" items at 4% while leaving ordinary closing costs uncapped.

I'm using a VA loan. Does that mean the seller can only cover 4% of my closing costs?

Not necessarily. The 4% cap only applies to specific concession items like debt payoff, prepaid taxes and insurance, and the funding fee. Ordinary closing costs, like the appraisal and title fees, aren't subject to that cap and can be paid separately by the seller in addition to the 4%.

Can the seller just give me extra cash at closing if my actual costs are lower than the cap?

No. Every program requires the contribution to match your actual closing costs owed; you can't use a program's percentage cap to generate extra cash, and none of it can go toward your down payment or reserves.

Does buying an investment property change my cap?

Yes, under conventional financing. Investment property purchases are capped at 2% regardless of how much you put down, well below the 3% to 9% range available on a primary residence or second home.

What's the difference between a down payment of 10% and 25% on a conventional loan?

It changes your seller contribution cap. Under 10% down caps the seller at 3% of the price; 10% up to just under 25% down allows 6%; 25% or more down allows 9%.

How do I know what my actual closing costs will be before I ask for a specific percentage?

A lender can give you a loan estimate with real, itemized closing costs once you've identified a property and loan program. Asking for a round percentage without that estimate risks asking for either too little or more than underwriting will actually allow.
