I Rented Out My Woodland Hills Home Before Moving Back In. Does That Hurt My Capital Gains Exclusion When I Sell?

Jonathan Lopez, Woodland Hills Realtor · Jim Sandoval Group · CA DRE #02282844 · Updated November 2026

A good number of Woodland Hills owners didn't buy their current home as a primary residence on day one. Maybe it was a rental property for a couple of years before a job change or a growing family turned it into the place they actually live, or it sat tenant-occupied while a prior home sold. Once that owner has lived there two of the last five years, the common assumption is that the full federal capital gains exclusion, $250,000 for a single filer or $500,000 for a married couple filing jointly, automatically applies when they eventually sell. That assumption is only half right, and the gap is a specific tax rule most sellers have never heard of.

The rule: nonqualified use gets allocated out of the exclusion. Under IRC Section 121(b)(4), added by the Housing and Economic Recovery Act of 2008, any period after 2008 during which a home was used for something other than your principal residence, most commonly a rental, before it became your main home, is called "nonqualified use." When you sell, the IRS requires you to calculate the ratio of nonqualified-use time to your total ownership period, and that same ratio of your gain gets carved out of the exclusion and taxed as ordinary capital gains. Meeting the standard 2-of-5-year ownership and use test still lets you claim the exclusion, but only on the qualified-use share of the gain, not the whole thing.

A simplified example, for illustration only. Say a Woodland Hills owner bought a property, rented it out for 2 years, then moved in and lived there as their primary residence for the next 6 years before selling, for a total of 8 years of ownership. Nonqualified use is 2 of those 8 years, or 25%. If the total gain on sale is $400,000, roughly 25% of that gain, about $100,000, falls into the nonqualified-use bucket and is not eligible for the exclusion, even though the owner meets the 2-of-5-year test on paper. The remaining 75%, about $300,000, is eligible for the exclusion and would owe no federal tax if it's under the $250,000/$500,000 cap for that portion. These numbers are illustrative only, not a real client's figures, every actual case depends on the real timeline and real gain.

The relief valve: moving out doesn't count against you. This is the part sellers most often get backward. Section 121(b)(4)(B)(i) specifically excludes from nonqualified use any period after the last date the home was used as your principal residence, as long as the sale happens within the normal 5-year lookback window. In plain terms: if you lived in the home as your primary residence, then moved out and rented it (or left it vacant) for a stretch before selling, that post-move-out period is not penalized the same way. The rule targets rental time that happened before you moved in, not rental time after you moved out. Confusing the two directions is the single most common mistake in this area.

Why this is a calculation, not a lookup. There is no flat percentage or simple yes-or-no answer here. The actual allocation depends on your specific month-by-month ownership timeline: when you bought, exactly when (if ever) you rented it out, exactly when you moved in as your primary residence, and exactly when you plan to sell. Two owners with the same total years of ownership can land on very different taxable amounts depending on where the rental period falls in that timeline. This is also separate from, and in addition to, any depreciation-recapture tax owed on a rental period, which is a related but distinct issue your CPA will need to calculate using your actual depreciation schedule.

How this is different from the other capital gains posts on this site. This post is not about whether you qualify for the exclusion at all (that's the straightforward 2-of-5-year Section 121 question), and it's not about deferring gain on an investment property through a 1031 exchange (a completely different mechanism: deferral instead of exclusion, and only available while the property stays classified as investment real estate). This post is specifically about owners who used a property as a rental first and a home second, where both mechanisms can apply in sequence and the exclusion itself gets reduced by a real allocation formula.

On today's broader Woodland Hills market (Movoto, citywide: $1,300,000 median sale price, 368 active listings, 54-day average days on market), getting your actual gain allocation right before you list can be worth tens of thousands of dollars either way.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does renting out my Woodland Hills home before I moved in disqualify me from the capital gains exclusion entirely?

No. It doesn't disqualify you, but it does reduce the portion of your gain that's eligible. The IRS allocates your gain between qualified use (time as your primary residence) and nonqualified use (the earlier rental period), and only the qualified-use share gets the $250,000/$500,000 exclusion.

What counts as "nonqualified use" under Section 121(b)(4)?

Generally, any period after 2008 when the home wasn't used as your principal residence, most commonly while it was a rental, that happened before the home became your main home. It's calculated as a ratio against your total ownership period.

If I live in my home now, then move out and rent it before I sell, does that count against me the same way?

No, and this is the part people most often get backward. Time after you move out, right up to a sale within 5 years, is not treated as nonqualified use. The rule specifically targets rental time before you moved in, not after you moved out.

How is the taxable, nonqualified-use portion of my gain actually calculated?

It's a ratio: your nonqualified-use time divided by your total ownership period, applied to your total gain. That percentage of your gain is excluded from the Section 121 exclusion and taxed as a regular long-term capital gain.

Does this also affect depreciation I claimed while it was a rental?

That's a separate, additional issue. Any depreciation you claimed during the rental period is generally subject to depreciation recapture, taxed on top of whatever gain allocation applies here. Your CPA will need your actual depreciation schedule to calculate that piece.

How do I find out my real number before I list?

There's no shortcut lookup table, since it depends on your exact ownership and use timeline. Bring your purchase date, the exact dates it was a rental, and the date it became your primary residence to a CPA before you commit to a listing date, and talk to me about timing the sale around that number.
