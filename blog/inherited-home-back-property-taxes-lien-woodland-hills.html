I Inherited a Woodland Hills Home With Back Property Taxes Owed. Does That Come Out of My Sale Proceeds?

Jonathan Lopez, Woodland Hills Realtor · Jim Sandoval Group · CA DRE #02282844 · Updated November 2026

Inheriting a home in Woodland Hills usually comes with a list of things to sort out: probate, title, maybe a disagreement among siblings about what happens next. One item that often gets overlooked until the very end of the process is the property tax bill, and whether it's actually been paid while the estate worked its way through probate. If it hasn't, the answer to "does that come out of my proceeds" is yes, and the amount owed can be larger than most heirs expect.

Property taxes are a lien against the house, not a personal debt. Under California Revenue and Taxation Code Section 2192, property tax obligations attach directly to the real property itself as of January 1 each year, the state's official "lien date." That distinction matters for an inherited home specifically: the debt isn't something the deceased owner's estate has to separately settle in probate court the way a credit card balance might be, and it isn't something an heir personally owes out of their own pocket unless they choose to pay it voluntarily. It's attached to the property, and it travels with the property until it's paid, whether that happens through regular payments, at a refinance, or at the closing table when the home is sold.

The penalty structure escalates faster than people expect. California property tax is billed in two installments: the first due November 1 (delinquent after December 10), the second due February 1 (delinquent after April 10). Missing either installment triggers a 10% penalty on that installment alone. If the full year's taxes still aren't paid by the following June 30, the parcel becomes officially "tax-defaulted," and from that point forward a redemption penalty of 1.5% per month, 18% annualized, begins accruing on the unpaid balance under Revenue and Taxation Code Section 4103, on top of the original 10% penalties already assessed. None of this requires the county to take any action against the property yet. It simply accrues, month after month, for as long as the balance sits unpaid.

This is exactly the scenario probate creates. California probate takes 12 to 18 months on average to resolve, and during that window, nobody may be clearly responsible for keeping current on a home that technically belongs to an estate rather than a living owner who's used to paying the bill every November and February. An executor or administrator can and should keep the account current once appointed, but if there's a delay in appointment, a dispute among heirs, or simply confusion about whose job it is, a full year or more of installments can pass with nothing paid. By the time the home is ready to list and sell, the unpaid balance may include not just the original tax amount but a year or more of stacked 10% and 18%-annualized penalties on top of it.

What happens at closing. When an inherited home sells, escrow and title run a full payoff demand on every lien against the property, which includes any outstanding secured property tax balance along with the current year's prorated amount. That full figure, principal, penalties, and interest, gets paid directly out of the sale proceeds before any money is distributed to the heirs. This works the same way a mortgage payoff does, except property tax liens actually take priority over a mortgage lien in California, so this gets settled first in the payoff chain, not after.

The outer-bound risk: the county's power to sell. If a property stays in tax-defaulted status for five consecutive years without being redeemed (paid off or put on an approved payment plan), California Revenue and Taxation Code Section 3691 gives the county tax collector the authority to sell the property at public auction to recover the unpaid debt. This is a genuinely rare outcome for a home that's actively being administered through probate and eventually sold, since a sale pays off the lien long before five years typically pass, but it's the real outer-bound consequence of letting a tax-defaulted balance sit unaddressed for an extended period, and it's worth heirs knowing it exists, especially in a prolonged or contested probate.

Why this is worth checking early, not at closing. Finding out mid-escrow that tens of thousands of dollars in taxes and penalties are about to come off the top of the sale proceeds is a worse time to learn this than before listing. A payoff demand can be requested from the LA County Treasurer and Tax Collector's office early in the process, well before a home is on the market, so heirs have an accurate net-proceeds picture from the start rather than a surprise at the closing statement.

The bottom line: an inherited home's back property taxes are real money that comes directly out of the sale proceeds, not a separate bill someone else has to chase down first. Knowing the actual balance, and how fast penalties have been accruing, early in the process is what turns a stressful surprise into a manageable number.

This post is general information, not legal or tax advice. A probate attorney and the LA County Treasurer and Tax Collector should confirm the exact balance, penalties and payment options for a specific property.

Frequently Asked Questions

Who is responsible for paying property taxes on a home during probate?

The estate, through its appointed executor or administrator, is generally responsible for keeping property taxes current using estate funds while probate is pending. Heirs aren't personally liable for the debt, but an unpaid balance still reduces what's ultimately available to distribute once the home sells.

Does a tax-defaulted status show up before escrow opens?

Not automatically to a buyer, but it's discoverable. A title search and a payoff demand from the county tax collector will surface the full balance, including any accrued penalties, before closing. Requesting that information before listing, rather than waiting for escrow to surface it, gives a much clearer early picture.

Can heirs set up a payment plan instead of paying it all at once?

California counties generally offer a five-year payment plan to redeem a tax-defaulted property, which can stop the clock on the county's power-to-sell timeline. This doesn't eliminate the obligation, but it can buy time if an estate needs it before a home is ready to sell.

Does this affect the home's sale price or marketability?

Not directly. A lien, including a tax lien, gets cleared at closing out of proceeds, the same way a mortgage does, and doesn't typically prevent a sale from closing. It does reduce net proceeds to the heirs by the full amount owed.

Is this different from the supplemental tax bill a new buyer might get after purchase?

Yes, it's a separate issue entirely. A supplemental or reassessment bill (covered in a separate post on this site) is triggered by a change in ownership and affects what a buyer owes going forward. Back taxes and penalties on an inherited home are a pre-existing balance from before the sale, owed by the estate, not the incoming buyer.

What if the back taxes are larger than the equity in the home?

This is uncommon but possible, especially if a home carries both a mortgage and years of unpaid tax penalties. In that scenario, a short sale or a direct conversation with the lender and the tax collector's office becomes necessary before listing, since proceeds alone may not cover every lien at the price the home would otherwise sell for.
