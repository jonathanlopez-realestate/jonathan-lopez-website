I Inherited a Woodland Hills Home That's in a Trust, Not Probate. Do I Still Have to Wait Over a Year to Sell?

Jonathan Lopez · CA DRE #02282844 · Updated November 2026

Nearly every Woodland Hills family that calls me after a parent or relative passes away starts from the same assumption: probate is coming, and nothing can happen with the house for a year or more while it works through the court. For homes held in a properly funded revocable living trust, that assumption is simply wrong, and understanding why changes how quickly a family can actually move.

Why a trust skips probate in the first place

The entire point of a revocable living trust, from an estate-planning standpoint, is that assets titled in the trust's name do not pass through the probate court when the person who created it dies. Instead, the trust document itself already names who takes over, the successor trustee, and that person's authority to manage and distribute trust property, including real estate, comes from the trust instrument, not from a judge. Under California Probate Code Section 16226, a trustee generally has the power to sell, convey, or otherwise dispose of trust property when the trust grants that authority, which the vast majority of modern California living trusts do in broad terms. No case gets filed, no Letters get issued, and no probate court calendar ever enters the picture.

What a successor trustee actually needs before listing

The process starts with practical, paperwork-level steps rather than court filings. A certified copy of the death certificate is the first document needed. Next, many trustees record an Affidavit of Death of Trustee with the county recorder, under Probate Code Section 210, which formally documents the transfer of trustee authority on public record, something a title company will want to see before closing. For escrow and the title company, the trustee typically provides a Certification of Trust under Probate Code Section 18100.5, a shorter document that confirms the trust exists and that the trustee has authority to sell, without disclosing private details like who the beneficiaries are or what they inherit. A date-of-death appraisal is also worth getting early, since it establishes the stepped-up tax basis the IRS uses if the home is sold later rather than immediately.

The beneficiary notice that exists, but does not block a sale

California law does build in a notice requirement, Probate Code Section 16061.7, which requires the trustee to notify every beneficiary and legal heir within 60 days after the trust becomes irrevocable, typically triggered by the settlor's death. That notice has to identify the trustee, the fact that the trust is now irrevocable, and the recipient's right to request a copy of the trust terms. Once served, beneficiaries have 120 days to contest the trust in court. That 120-day window exists to protect beneficiaries, but it does not legally prevent the trustee from listing and selling the property in the meantime. A cautious trustee may still choose to keep beneficiaries closely informed before and during a sale simply to avoid a dispute later, even though the statute itself does not require waiting out the full 120 days first.

So how fast can a sale actually happen?

Here the honest answer has two parts, because reasonable sources describe this differently depending on what they are measuring. Once the paperwork above is in hand, the sale itself, listing, accepting an offer, and closing escrow, can often move in a matter of weeks, closer to a standard non-probate transaction than to a probate sale that has to wait on a court process first. But finishing the entire trust administration, the notice period, resolving any creditor claims, filing final tax returns, and making the last distributions to beneficiaries, is a longer undertaking that can still run several months to well over a year depending on how complicated the estate is. A family selling the house does not need to wait for that full wind-down to finish, the sale and the broader trust administration can run on separate, overlapping tracks, which is exactly the point of using a trust in the first place.

This still compares favorably to probate

For context, a full California probate case, the path required when a home was never transferred into a trust, typically takes 9 to 18 months from filing to final distribution, and in many cases a sale cannot even go to market until a personal representative has been appointed and granted authority by the court. A trust sale skips that opening phase entirely, since the successor trustee's authority exists the moment the trust becomes irrevocable, not months later when a judge signs an order.

A note on property taxes

Transferring a home out of a trust to a beneficiary, or selling it on the open market, can trigger a reassessment question under Proposition 19, which is a separate topic from how fast the sale itself can happen. Why this matters for a Woodland Hills family

Knowing upfront that a trust-held home does not require a probate case changes the whole conversation about timing, pricing strategy, and when to start preparing the house to show. On Woodland Hills' current median sale price of $1,300,000 (Movoto, Nov 5, 2026), a well-documented date-of-death appraisal is also what protects the accuracy of the stepped-up basis calculation if the sale happens well after the date of death rather than right away.

Selling a trust-held home still has real steps, death certificate, affidavit, certification of trust, beneficiary notice, and a good appraisal, but none of them require a judge's permission. That is the single biggest difference from a probate sale, and it is usually the first thing worth confirming when a Woodland Hills family is sorting out what they inherited.

FAQ

How do I know if the home is in a trust instead of going through probate?

Check how title is held. If the deed or a recent deed of record shows the property vested in the name of a trust, for example "John Smith, Trustee of the Smith Family Trust," rather than in the individual's own name, it is trust-held and should avoid probate for that asset.

Does the successor trustee need court approval to sell the house?

No, not if the trust document grants the trustee the power to sell real property, which most modern California living trusts do. Under Probate Code Section 16226, that authority comes from the trust instrument itself, not from a judge.

What paperwork does a trustee need before listing the home?

Typically a certified death certificate, an Affidavit of Death of Trustee recorded with the county (Probate Code Section 210), and a Certification of Trust for escrow and the title company (Probate Code Section 18100.5). A date-of-death appraisal is also worth obtaining for the tax basis.

Do beneficiaries have to be notified before the trustee can sell?

Yes. Probate Code Section 16061.7 requires notice to beneficiaries and heirs within 60 days of the trust becoming irrevocable. They then have 120 days to contest the trust, but that window does not legally stop the trustee from listing and selling the property during that time.

How long does it actually take to sell a trust-held home compared to a probate home?

The sale itself can often happen in weeks once the paperwork is ready, much faster than a probate sale, which typically cannot even go to market until a court appoints and authorizes a personal representative. Full trust administration and final distribution, separate from the sale, can still take several months to over a year depending on complexity.

Does selling a trust-held inherited home still get the stepped-up tax basis?

Yes. The property's tax basis still resets to its fair market value on the date of death under federal tax rules, the same as inherited property passing through probate. A timely date-of-death appraisal is what documents that value.
