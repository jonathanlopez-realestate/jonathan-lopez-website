My Seller Is Paying For Title Insurance. Do I Still Need My Own Owner's Policy In Woodland Hills?

Jonathan Lopez, Woodland Hills Realtor · Jim Sandoval Group · CA DRE #02282844 · Updated November 2026

If you have bought a home in Woodland Hills recently, or you are deep in a search right now, you have probably already heard that sellers here customarily cover the cost of your title insurance. That is true, and it is a nice break on your closing costs. But it also means a lot of buyers hear "seller pays for it" and stop paying attention to what it actually is, which is a mistake, because the policy itself is one of the few protections in a real estate transaction that covers problems nobody involved in the sale, not you, not your agent, not your lender, could have seen coming.

Every mortgage transaction in California includes a lender's title insurance policy. Your lender requires it as a condition of the loan, and it protects the lender's financial interest in the property, specifically the outstanding loan balance, not your equity. That coverage amount shrinks every year as you pay down your mortgage, and it disappears entirely once the loan is paid off or refinanced. It is not insurance for you as the homeowner, it is insurance for the bank's investment in your home.

The owner's title policy is a different product entirely, and it is the one that actually protects you. It covers the full purchase price of the home (or, depending on the policy, its value at the time a claim is made), and unlike the lender's policy, it does not shrink over time. It stays in force for as long as you, or your heirs, own the property, for one single premium paid at closing, not an ongoing cost the way homeowners insurance or property taxes are.

What does it actually protect against? This is the part most buyers never hear explained, because it rarely comes up unless something has already gone wrong. Title insurance exists for problems hidden in the history of the property, not problems with its physical condition. A forged signature on a deed decades ago that makes the entire chain of ownership technically invalid. An heir from a prior owner's estate who was never properly notified or paid out, and who later surfaces with a legitimate legal claim to a share of the property. A contractor's mechanic's lien filed years earlier that was never properly released from the public record. A clerical error at the county recorder's office that misstates a legal description or a boundary. None of these would show up on a home inspection, a walk through, or even a careful look at recent paperwork, because they live in the public record, sometimes going back decades, not in the condition of the house itself.

There is also a meaningful coverage distinction worth asking your title officer about directly: standard CLTA coverage protects against defects that are, in principle, discoverable in the public record, even if nobody actually caught them before your purchase. An extended ALTA policy costs more but adds protection against certain matters that would not show up in a public records search at all, things like an unpermitted addition that encroaches on a setback or an easement, or certain survey-related boundary issues. Which policy you are actually being offered matters, and it is a fair, normal question to ask rather than assuming the standard version is automatically what you are getting.

On Woodland Hills' current market (Movoto: $1,300,000 median sale price, 368 active listings, 54 day average days on market), the dollar value sitting behind a typical purchase here is exactly the kind of equity worth protecting with a policy that is not shrinking the moment you close. Since the seller is customarily covering this premium in Southern California deals, there is rarely a reason to decline it to save money, the real decision worth making deliberately is confirming which policy you are getting and understanding what it does and does not cover, rather than treating it as paperwork to sign without a second look.

If you are under contract right now or about to be, this is exactly the kind of detail worth walking through with your title officer and your agent together, before you are sitting at the closing table with documents in front of you.

Frequently Asked Questions

What's the difference between a lender's title policy and an owner's title policy?

The lender's policy is required by your mortgage and protects only the bank's interest, shrinking as your loan balance goes down. The owner's policy protects your actual equity in the home, stays in force for as long as you or your heirs own it, and is a separate, optional (though strongly recommended) policy.

If my seller is paying for it, is there anything for me to actually do?

Yes. Confirm you are actually getting your own owner's policy, not just the lender's required policy, and ask your title officer whether you are getting standard CLTA coverage or extended ALTA coverage, since they protect against different things.

What does title insurance actually protect against?

Problems hidden in the property's ownership history: a forged deed somewhere in the chain of title, an undisclosed heir later claiming an ownership interest, an old unreleased contractor's lien, or a clerical recording error, none of which a home inspection would catch.

Is title insurance a recurring cost like homeowners insurance?

No. It is a single, one time premium paid at closing. There is no renewal and no ongoing payment.

What's the difference between a standard policy and an extended ALTA policy?

A standard policy covers defects discoverable in the public record. An extended ALTA policy costs more but adds protection against certain matters a public records search would not catch, like an unpermitted addition affecting a boundary or easement.

Can I decline the owner's policy to save money?

You can, but since Southern California sellers customarily pay this premium for you, there is rarely a real cost saving in declining it, and doing so would leave your actual equity in the home unprotected against the exact risks described above.
