Does My Woodland Hills List Price Actually Match How Buyers Search? Here's the Real Data

Jonathan Lopez, Woodland Hills Realtor | Jim Sandoval Group | CA DRE #02282844 · Updated September 2026

Most sellers think about list price as a negotiation starting point: price it a little high to leave room, or price it low to spark a bidding war. Almost no seller thinks about list price as a search filter. But that's increasingly the more important lens, because before a buyer ever calls their agent, drives by, or looks at a single photo, they typed a number range into a search bar on Zillow, Redfin, Realtor.com, or their own agent's portal. If the list price doesn't land inside that range, the home doesn't get seen at all. Everything else, the photography, the staging, the description, becomes irrelevant, because it never gets a chance to be judged.

Where This Idea Comes From

Real estate coach Darryl Davis laid this out clearly in an Inman column published July 9, 2026, built around a coaching call with one of his agents. The agent had just listed a home at $635,000 and was proud of the number. Davis asked a simple question: who searches for $635,000? Nobody does. Buyers searching in that price tier typically set a range like $575,000 to $625,000, or if shopping slightly higher, $625,000 to $675,000. A $635,000 listing catches the first range but completely misses the second. A clean $625,000 or $650,000 price does something different: it lands squarely inside two search ranges instead of one, which means more searches surface it, more buyers see it, and more showings happen. More showings is how offers happen.

The "Just Under a Threshold" Trick Doesn't Work the Way It Used To

For years, agents have priced homes at $619,000 or $624,900 instead of $625,000, on the theory that buyers perceive the lower number as meaningfully cheaper. Davis argues this is now working against sellers, not for them. Redfin's own blog on pricing psychology backs up the same shift: buyers increasingly filter listings using round-number range sliders on portals, not by scanning for bargains a few hundred or a few thousand dollars under a round number. A price of $624,900 doesn't catch a buyer searching "up to $625,000" any better than $635,000 does. It just avoids being the highest listing in that exact range, a much smaller advantage than most sellers assume they're getting.

What This Means at Woodland Hills' Price Point

Davis's own example of where these breakpoints commonly sit in higher-priced markets: $1,000,000 and $1,250,000. That range sits close to Woodland Hills' current market. As of the most recent Movoto update, the median sale price here is $1,348,500, meaning a meaningful share of local listings sit in a range where the $1,000,000, $1,250,000, and $1,500,000 search filters likely matter more than most sellers realize. A home priced at $1,299,000 to look like it's "under" $1.3 million can, by the same logic Davis describes, miss a $1,300,000 to $1,500,000 search entirely while only partially benefiting from the $1,000,000 to $1,300,000 range it's aiming for.

The Price-Reduction Version of This Mistake

This isn't only a listing-day decision. When a Woodland Hills home has been sitting and it's time to adjust, the same principle applies to where the new price lands. Dropping from $1,395,000 to $1,379,000 doesn't open up any new search range, it's still catching the same buyers who were already seeing it. A move to a clean $1,350,000, or further to $1,300,000, can put the home in front of an entirely different pool of buyers who were filtering it out before, not just the same pool at a slightly lower number. This is a different lever than the one covered in whether you'll have to cut your price at all, which looks at when a reduction makes sense; this post is about where a reduction should actually land once you've decided to make one.

Where Woodland Hills' Market Stands Right Now

As of the most recent Movoto update (September 2), there are 385 active listings in Woodland Hills, with 24 newly listed and 92 already price-reduced, meaning roughly a quarter of the market has already made at least one pricing adjustment. The median sale price in August 2026 was $1,348,500, with homes taking an average of 48 days to sell, up slightly from 45 days a year ago. 367 homes sold here in August 2026, up from 331 the year before (Movoto). In a market with this many active, competing listings, a list price that's a few thousand dollars off from where the nearest search range starts isn't a minor detail. It's the difference between a home that gets seen by every relevant buyer and one that quietly sits outside the search results of buyers who would have loved it.

Frequently Asked Questions

Why would a lower list price actually get fewer views than a slightly higher one?

Because buyers filter by round-number search ranges, not by scanning every listing in order of price. A home priced at $624,900 to look like a deal under $625,000 doesn't get more views than one priced at $625,000. It just misses the search range that starts at $625,000 or higher, the opposite of the intended effect (Inman, Darryl Davis, July 2026).

What are the actual round-number breakpoints buyers search around in Woodland Hills?

No public source breaks out exact search-filter data for the 91367 zip specifically. Darryl Davis's Inman column cites $1,000,000 and $1,250,000 as example breakpoints in higher-priced markets generally, a range that overlaps with Woodland Hills' current median. The safest approach for any specific home is to map out the nearest round-number thresholds above and below the target price and see which one the home should be positioned to fall inside.

Is the old "$X99,000" pricing trick still worth using?

According to both Davis's Inman column and Redfin's own pricing psychology research, it's losing effectiveness. Buyers increasingly set search filters using round-number sliders on portals rather than manually comparing exact prices, so a price like $1,299,000 doesn't reliably catch buyers the way it used to. A clean, round number that clearly lands inside a specific search range is now the more effective strategy in most cases.

Does this mean I should always price my home at a round number?

Not automatically. It means the nearest round-number search ranges above and below a target price should be part of the pricing conversation, not an afterthought. Sometimes the right strategic price is a round number; sometimes it's a specific figure just inside a range for a different reason, like matching a specific comp. The point is knowing which search ranges a price does and doesn't catch before choosing it.

If my Woodland Hills home has been sitting, does a small price cut actually help?

It depends whether the cut moves the home into a new search range or just shaves a few thousand dollars off inside the same one. A reduction from $1,395,000 to $1,379,000 likely doesn't open up new buyer searches. A reduction to $1,350,000 or $1,300,000 can put the home in front of buyers who were filtering it out entirely before.

How do I find the right search-range price for my specific home?

This takes a real conversation about comparable listings, the specific price tier, and where buyers in that tier are actually setting their filters, not a generic formula. A real pricing strategy session, not a guess based on what feels comfortable, is the right way to answer this for a specific address.
