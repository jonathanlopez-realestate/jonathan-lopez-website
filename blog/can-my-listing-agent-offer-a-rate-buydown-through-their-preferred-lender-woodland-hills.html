Can My Woodland Hills Listing Agent Offer Buyers a Rate Buydown Through Their Own Preferred Lender, or Does That Break Federal Kickback Law?

Jonathan Lopez, Woodland Hills Realtor · Jim Sandoval Group · CA DRE #02282844 · Updated November 2026

If you're selling in Woodland Hills and considering a seller-paid rate buydown to make your listing more attractive in a market where mortgage rates are still a real obstacle for buyers, you may have heard that your listing agent's brokerage has its own in-house or "preferred" lender that handles buydowns. That raises a legitimate question before anyone agrees to anything: is it actually legal for a real estate agent to steer a buyer toward their own affiliated lender, or does that cross into an illegal kickback?

The starting point is federal law, specifically Section 8 of the Real Estate Settlement Procedures Act (RESPA), codified at 12 U.S.C. Section 2607. Section 8 makes it illegal to give or accept any fee, kickback, or thing of value in exchange for referring settlement service business: mortgage origination, title insurance, escrow, and similar services tied to a real estate transaction. The idea behind the law is straightforward: a buyer's decision about which lender or title company to use should be driven by the best rate and service for them, not by which company quietly paid their agent the most to send business their way.

That said, RESPA and its implementing regulation, Regulation X (12 C.F.R. Section 1024.15), carve out a specific, well-defined exception called an Affiliated Business Arrangement, often shortened to ABA. An ABA exists when a real estate brokerage has an actual ownership stake in a mortgage, title, or escrow company, think of a large brokerage that also owns a piece of an in-house mortgage lender, and refers clients to that affiliated company. The arrangement is allowed, but only if three conditions are met every time: the brokerage gives the buyer a clear written disclosure of the ownership relationship before any referral happens, the buyer is never required to use the affiliated company and remains free to shop and compare other lenders, and the brokerage's only financial benefit is a return tied to its actual ownership percentage, not a disguised per-referral fee.

Applied to a seller-paid rate buydown specifically: if your Woodland Hills listing agent's brokerage has a genuine ownership stake in the lender offering the buydown, discloses that relationship to the buyer in writing, and doesn't require the buyer to use that lender to get the buydown credit, the arrangement is generally on solid legal ground under the ABA exception. What would cross the line is a quieter version of the same idea: an agent being paid a fee just for sending buyers to a particular loan officer, with no ownership stake behind it and no disclosure given, purely as a referral kickback. That is exactly the conduct Section 8 was written to stop, and violations can carry real penalties for the professionals involved, not the seller or buyer.

As a seller, none of this changes your own financial exposure from offering the buydown itself, that's a straightforward negotiated seller concession, usually structured as a credit toward the buyer's closing costs used to permanently or temporarily lower their interest rate. What it should change is what you ask before agreeing to route that credit through a specific lender your agent suggests. A legitimate ABA disclosure should be easy for your agent or their brokerage to produce in writing. If one can't be produced, or if the buyer is being told they have to use that specific lender to get the buydown credit at all, that's worth a direct conversation, and potentially a second opinion from a real estate attorney, before moving forward.

It's also worth separating this from ordinary, fully legal brokerage referral practices. Agents recommend lenders, inspectors, and title companies to buyers and sellers constantly, and most of the time there's no ownership relationship or fee changing hands at all, just a professional recommendation based on past experience. The ABA disclosure requirement specifically targets situations where the brokerage itself has skin in the game financially, not every informal recommendation an agent makes.

If you're weighing whether a rate buydown makes sense for your Woodland Hills listing in the first place, that's a separate, purely financial question from the RESPA compliance question above. It depends on your price point, how rate-sensitive your buyer pool is right now, and how the cost compares to simply adjusting your list price instead. Both questions are worth working through with your agent before you commit to a specific structure.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is it illegal for my listing agent to recommend their own lender for a buydown?

Not by itself. Federal law allows an Affiliated Business Arrangement when the brokerage has an actual ownership stake in the lender, discloses that relationship to the buyer in writing, and doesn't require the buyer to use that lender. What's illegal is an undisclosed referral fee with no ownership interest behind it.

What law actually governs this?

Section 8 of the Real Estate Settlement Procedures Act (RESPA), codified at 12 U.S.C. Section 2607, bans kickbacks for referring settlement services. Regulation X, at 12 C.F.R. Section 1024.15, spells out the Affiliated Business Arrangement exception and its disclosure requirements.

Does this cost me anything extra as the seller?

No. RESPA compliance is about how the referral relationship is disclosed and structured, not about the cost of the buydown itself, which is a separate, ordinary seller concession you negotiate directly.

Can a buyer be forced to use the agent's affiliated lender to get the buydown?

No. One of the three required conditions for a legal Affiliated Business Arrangement is that the buyer remains free to shop other lenders. If use of a specific lender is presented as mandatory, that's worth raising with a real estate attorney.

What should I ask for before agreeing to route a buydown through a specific lender?

A written Affiliated Business Arrangement disclosure, if one applies, showing the ownership relationship and confirming the buyer isn't required to use that lender. If your agent's brokerage can't produce one, ask directly how the relationship is structured.

Are ordinary lender or vendor recommendations from my agent a problem under RESPA?

Generally no. The ABA disclosure rule specifically targets situations where the brokerage has a financial ownership stake in the company being recommended. A simple, unpaid professional recommendation with no ownership tie isn't the same thing.
