Does Being Near a Highly Rated School Actually Raise My Woodland Hills Home's Value?

Jonathan Lopez, Woodland Hills Realtor | Jim Sandoval Group | CA DRE #02282844 · Updated September 2026

"Good schools raise home values" is one of the most repeated pieces of real estate folk wisdom, and it's usually said without any real number attached. Redfin actually put a number on it in a report published September 2, 2026, analyzing home sales that closed between January and June 2026 across 81 major U.S. metro areas, matched to the specific public high school attendance zone each sale fell inside. The Los Angeles metro, which includes Woodland Hills, was one of them, and the results are worth understanding before assuming a nearby school automatically means a home is worth more.

The National Number, and Where LA Lands Relative to It

Nationally, Redfin found the typical home located in a highly rated school zone (defined as a GreatSchools rating of 8 to 10 out of 10) costs $580,000, a 35% premium over the $430,000 typical home price across all zones. In the Los Angeles metro specifically, the premium came in at 36%: a typical home in a highly rated LA school zone costs $1,225,000 versus $899,000 for a typical LA home overall, essentially matching the national rate almost exactly, a genuinely useful, non-obvious finding since LA's overall housing costs are far from typical in absolute dollar terms.

Not Every Metro Shows a Big Premium, and That Context Matters

Redfin's report specifically highlighted Anaheim, a nearby Southern California metro, as having the smallest school-zone premium in the entire study: just 6%. The reason, per Redfin's own analysis, is that 70% of Anaheim's school zones are rated highly, the largest share of any metro studied, so there's no real scarcity driving up prices near good schools specifically, since good schools are the norm rather than the exception there. Los Angeles doesn't have that same abundance, which is part of why its premium sits much closer to the national average instead of Anaheim's unusually small one.

What This Means Specifically for a Home Zoned to Taft Charter High

West Woodland Hills' local comprehensive public high school, Taft Charter High at 5461 Winnetka Avenue, carries a 9 out of 10 GreatSchools rating, verified directly on GreatSchools.org rather than assumed from general reputation. That rating clears Redfin's own 8-10 threshold for "highly rated," meaning a home genuinely zoned to Taft isn't just in a neighborhood with a good reputation, it meets the specific bar this pricing research is actually measuring. That's a meaningfully different claim than a vague "great schools nearby" marketing line, and it's worth confirming for any specific address via GreatSchools' own attendance-boundary map, since school zone boundaries don't always follow neighborhood or even zip code lines exactly.

The Real Cost Side of This Premium, Not Just the Upside

Redfin's report also measured affordability, and Los Angeles stood out here too, in the opposite direction. Buying a home in one of LA's highly rated school zones takes 98.6% of the typical LA household's income, according to Redfin's methodology (15% down, 30-year fixed mortgage including taxes, insurance, and mortgage insurance). That's compared to 72.4% of income for a typical LA home in any zone. Both figures reflect how expensive LA housing is broadly, well above what's considered affordable anywhere, but the highly rated school zone premium pushes an already difficult market further out of reach for the median earner. This is useful context for both sides of a transaction: a seller in a genuinely highly rated zone has real leverage on price, and a buyer targeting one should expect to compete harder and pay more, not just for square footage but specifically for the attendance boundary.

Why This Is a Real Valuation Factor, Not Just Marketing Language

Too often, "great schools nearby" appears in listing copy as a generic soft-sell phrase without any backing data, the kind of claim any listing anywhere could make. Redfin's study gives sellers and their agents an actual, citable, dated (2026) data point to use instead: a specific national premium percentage, a specific LA metro premium percentage that closely tracks it, and a specific, verifiable rating for the actual school a given address is zoned to. That distinction, a real number tied to a verified rating versus an unverified reputation claim, is what separates a defensible pricing argument from a generic listing adjective. It's the same discipline worth applying to an automated Zestimate versus real comparable sales, a real number beats a general impression every time.

What This Doesn't Mean

A high GreatSchools rating for one specific school doesn't automatically apply to every address in Woodland Hills; attendance boundaries are specific and sometimes surprising, so any claim about "the highly rated school zone" needs to be checked against the actual boundary map for the specific property, not assumed from the broader neighborhood's general reputation. It also doesn't mean every home near Taft carries the full 36% premium uniformly. Redfin's own methodology notes the premium reflects location, housing stock, and neighborhood affluence together, not schools in isolation, so the actual number for any individual home depends on its own comparable sales, not a blanket metro-wide percentage applied mechanically.

What This Means Practically for a Seller Pricing a Woodland Hills Listing

If a home genuinely falls inside Taft's attendance boundary, that's a legitimate, citable talking point for pricing and marketing, backed by a dated 2026 study rather than a generic adjective. The right move is confirming the exact boundary first, then pulling truly comparable sales from inside that same boundary rather than assuming every nearby sale shares the same premium. A buyer's agent doing their own diligence will check the same boundary map, so a pricing argument built on a verified zone and a real study holds up under that scrutiny in a way a vague "top-rated schools nearby" listing line does not. It's one more factor alongside an ADU's documented value premium and whether a pool actually adds value here, real, citable data beats a general assumption every time a listing gets priced.

Bottom Line

In the Los Angeles metro, a home genuinely zoned to a highly rated public school does carry a real, data-backed price premium, roughly in line with the national 35% figure, and Taft Charter High's own 9/10 GreatSchools rating means west Woodland Hills homes zoned to it can legitimately make that case. But the premium comes with a real affordability cost on the buyer side, and it only applies to addresses that actually fall inside the verified boundary, not to the neighborhood's reputation in general. Woodland Hills' current median sale price is $1,348,500 (Movoto, updated September 18, 2026), and knowing whether your specific address sits inside a verified highly rated attendance zone is worth confirming before you price a listing or make an offer.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does my Woodland Hills home get a value boost just for being near a good school?

Only if it's genuinely inside that school's attendance zone, not just nearby. Redfin's premium data is based on verified attendance boundaries, not proximity or neighborhood reputation, and school zone boundaries can be more specific than they appear.

What GreatSchools rating counts as "highly rated" in this research?

An 8, 9, or 10 out of 10. Taft Charter High, west Woodland Hills' local comprehensive public high school, carries a 9 out of 10 rating, verified directly on GreatSchools.org.

How much of a price premium does a highly rated school zone actually add in the LA area?

Redfin's September 2026 report found a 36% premium in the Los Angeles metro overall ($1,225,000 vs $899,000 for a typical home), close to the 35% national average. Individual home premiums vary based on the specific property and comparable sales.

Why does nearby Anaheim have such a small school-zone premium compared to LA?

Because 70% of Anaheim's school zones are rated highly, the highest share of any metro studied, so highly rated schools aren't scarce there. Los Angeles doesn't have that same abundance, which keeps its premium closer to the national average.

Does this mean buying in a highly rated school zone is a good investment?

It can be, but Redfin's data shows it also means paying a real affordability premium: LA buyers in highly rated zones spend an estimated 98.6% of median household income to afford one, versus 72.4% for a typical LA home. The premium and the cost of accessing it go together.

How do I know if my specific address is actually zoned to Taft Charter High or another highly rated school?

Check the specific attendance boundary map on GreatSchools.org or LAUSD's own boundary tool for the exact address, rather than assuming based on the general neighborhood, since boundaries can split streets in ways that aren't obvious.
