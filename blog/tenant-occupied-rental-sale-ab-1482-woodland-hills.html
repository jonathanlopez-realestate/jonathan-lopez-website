I'm Selling My Woodland Hills Rental and the Tenant Is Still Living There. Can I Make Them Leave to Close?

Jonathan Lopez, Woodland Hills Realtor · Jim Sandoval Group · CA DRE #02282844 · Updated November 2026

A surprising number of Woodland Hills landlords list a rental for sale assuming that once escrow closes, the tenant's lease simply ends along with it. That assumption is wrong for most rentals in California, and getting it wrong can turn a routine sale into a legal problem with real financial exposure.

The baseline rule: selling is not just cause. California's Tenant Protection Act of 2019 (AB 1482), codified at Civil Code Section 1946.2, requires a landlord to have a legally recognized "just cause" before ending a tenancy, once a tenant has lived in a covered unit for 12 months (or at least one tenant has lived there 24 months). The statute lists specific at-fault reasons (nonpayment, lease violations, nuisance) and no-fault reasons (owner move-in, Ellis Act withdrawal from the rental market, substantial remodel, compliance with a government order). Wanting to sell the property, or having already accepted an offer, appears nowhere on either list. A termination notice served for that reason alone is not valid.

The real exception: a genuine owner or buyer move-in. The one path that actually works for a seller who needs the home vacant is the no-fault "owner move-in" just cause. Since SB 567 amended the law effective April 1, 2024, this can cover the seller, a qualifying family member, or the buyer, but the requirements are strict: the person must actually move into the home within 90 days of the tenant vacating and live there as their primary residence for at least 12 consecutive months. If that doesn't happen, or if it later comes out that the move-in was never genuinely intended, the law allows the displaced tenant to sue for treble (triple) damages plus attorney's fees. This is not a box to check casually; it only works when the buyer (or seller) truly intends to live in the home.

Notice and relocation assistance aren't optional. Even a valid owner-move-in termination requires 60 days' written notice for a tenant who has lived there a year or more (Civil Code Section 1946.1), and the law requires the landlord to provide relocation assistance to the displaced tenant equal to one month's rent, typically paid directly or applied as a waiver of the final month's rent. Skipping either step can invalidate the termination even when the underlying just cause is legitimate.

A real exemption exists, but almost nobody actually qualifies for it. The law exempts single-family homes owned by a natural person (not a corporation, REIT, or an LLC with any corporate member) from both the rent cap and the just-cause requirements. But that exemption only applies if the landlord gave the tenant a specific written notice, required under Civil Code Section 1946.2(e)(8)(B), stating the exemption applies, delivered in the lease or a signed addendum at the start of the tenancy. Plenty of Woodland Hills landlords own a qualifying single-family rental but never served that exact notice years ago when the tenant moved in, which means the exemption they assume they have does not actually apply. Checking the original lease paperwork for that specific notice is the first move before assuming an exemption exists.

What happens in most sales without a qualifying move-in. If there's no genuine owner or buyer move-in, and no valid exemption notice on file, the tenancy simply transfers to the new owner at closing along with the existing lease terms. The buyer becomes the new landlord, bound by the same just-cause and notice rules the seller was. This isn't a defect in the sale; tenant-occupied investment property sells regularly, just usually to a buyer who plans to hold it as a rental rather than move in themselves, and the purchase price typically reflects that the home is being sold with an existing tenancy rather than vacant.

Why this matters before you list. A seller who assumes they can simply hand the tenant a 30- or 60-day notice "because we're selling" and finds out mid-escrow that the notice is invalid can lose a buyer who needed a vacant home, face a tenant lawsuit, or both. Sorting out which category a specific rental falls into, exempt, eligible for a genuine owner-move-in termination, or destined to sell with the tenancy intact, is worth doing before the home ever goes on the market, not after an offer is already in hand.

It also changes who can actually buy the home. Owner-occupant buyers using FHA or VA financing generally need to move into the property, and most FHA and VA guidelines expect that to happen within 60 days of closing. A tenant with a year or more left on a lease, or one protected by the just-cause rules above, makes that kind of financing difficult to use unless the lease is ending on its own close to the closing date, or the seller is relying on a genuine owner-move-in termination that the buyer will actually complete. Cash buyers and conventional investor buyers are far more flexible about taking title subject to an existing lease, which is one reason tenant-occupied rentals in Woodland Hills tend to draw a different buyer pool than a comparable vacant home, and sometimes a different price, since an investor is pricing in the existing rent roll rather than the home's potential as owner-occupied housing.

A practical middle path: timing around the lease. Not every tenant-occupied sale needs to invoke the just-cause rules at all. If a lease is naturally expiring within a few months of a planned closing, many sellers simply list with the tenant in place, market toward investor buyers or buyers willing to wait out the remaining term, and let the lease run its course rather than trying to engineer an early termination. This avoids the 90-day move-in requirement, the 60-day notice, and the relocation payment entirely, at the cost of a narrower buyer pool until the lease actually ends. Reviewing the actual lease expiration date against a realistic listing and closing timeline, before deciding whether a move-in termination is even necessary, is often the simplest way to avoid the legal exposure described above.

What to pull together before deciding. The original lease and any addenda (to check for the Civil Code 1946.2(e)(8)(B) exemption notice), the lease's actual expiration date, and a clear answer on whether the seller or a prospective buyer genuinely intends to occupy the home are the three things to gather before choosing a path. Each leads to a different marketing strategy and a different legal process, and guessing wrong after a buyer is already under contract is where most of the real problems in these sales actually happen.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can I just tell my tenant I'm selling and they need to move out?

No, not on that basis alone. Simply selling the home, or having an accepted offer, is not a recognized just cause to end a tenancy under Civil Code Section 1946.2 once the tenant has lived there 12 months or more.

If the buyer wants to move in themselves, can they require the tenant to leave?

Only through a genuine owner-move-in termination: the buyer must actually move in within 90 days of the tenant vacating and live there as a primary residence for at least 12 months, with 60 days' notice and relocation assistance equal to one month's rent provided to the tenant. A move-in that isn't real can expose the buyer or seller to triple damages and attorney's fees.

Is my single-family rental automatically exempt from these rules?

Not automatically. The exemption only applies if the home is owned by a natural person (not a corporation, REIT, or an LLC with a corporate member) and the landlord gave the tenant a specific written exemption notice, required under Civil Code Section 1946.2(e)(8)(B), at the start of the tenancy. Without that notice on file, the exemption doesn't apply even if the ownership structure qualifies.

What happens to the lease if I sell and none of the exceptions apply?

The tenancy and existing lease terms transfer to the new owner at closing. The buyer takes over as landlord under the same rules the seller was operating under; this is a normal and common way tenant-occupied investment property changes hands.

Does this affect what my rental is worth, or who will buy it?

It can. A home that will sell with an existing tenant in place typically appeals to buy-and-hold investors rather than owner-occupant buyers, and pricing and marketing strategy usually differ accordingly. Whether a vacant sale or a tenant-in-place sale nets more depends on the specific lease, rent level, and buyer pool for that property.

Should I try to get my tenant to leave voluntarily before listing?

That's a conversation to have carefully and in writing, often with a landlord-tenant attorney involved, since a tenant who later feels pressured into leaving can raise the same legal exposure as an improper formal termination. A voluntary move-out with a documented agreement is different from an informal verbal request.
