A Tenant Lives in the Woodland Hills Home I Inherited. What Are My Rights and Responsibilities as the New Landlord?

Jonathan Lopez, Realtor serving Woodland Hills with the Jim Sandoval Group · CA DRE #02282844 · Updated October 2026

Inheriting a Woodland Hills home usually comes with decisions about probate, taxes, and timing. When the home already has a tenant living in it, there's an additional layer most heirs don't expect: they've also inherited a landlord relationship, with its own legal obligations, whether they intended to become a landlord or not.

The starting point is the lease itself. Under general California landlord-tenant law, an existing lease or rental agreement transfers automatically when a rental property changes ownership, including through inheritance. The new owner steps into the prior owner's position as landlord on the terms already in place. A tenant on a fixed-term lease generally keeps the right to stay through the remainder of that term, and a tenant on a month-to-month agreement keeps their month-to-month status, neither converts automatically just because ownership changed hands.

The bigger constraint for heirs who'd rather not keep a tenant is California's statewide Tenant Protection Act, codified at Civil Code Section 1946.2 and commonly referenced by its original bill number, AB 1482. Once a tenant has continuously and lawfully occupied a unit for 12 months, the law requires the owner to have a legally recognized just cause before ending the tenancy, stated in the written termination notice. Simply being a new owner who inherited the property and would prefer not to have tenants is explicitly not one of the recognized just causes. The law lists specific at-fault reasons (nonpayment of rent, lease violations, property damage, nuisance behavior, illegal activity) and specific no-fault reasons, including substantial renovation and owner move-in, but an heir can't terminate a qualifying tenancy simply to sell the home vacant or to avoid being a landlord.

There is a real path if an heir, or specific qualifying family members, actually want to live in the home: the owner move-in just cause. To use it, the owner, or the owner's spouse, domestic partner, child, grandchild, parent, or grandparent, has to move into the unit within 90 days of the tenant leaving and occupy it as their primary residence for at least 12 continuous months. The termination notice has to name the specific intended occupant and their relationship to the owner, and the tenant can request proof of that relationship. For this and other no-fault just causes, the law generally also requires the owner to pay the displaced tenant relocation assistance equal to one month's rent, or waive the final month's rent instead. Using owner move-in as a pretext, without actually moving in and staying the required period, carries real legal risk, including potential liability to the displaced tenant.

Security deposits add a separate obligation that catches many heirs off guard. Under Civil Code Section 1950.5, when a landlord's interest in the property is terminated, meaning ownership changes through a sale, inheritance, or otherwise, the law requires the deposit to be handled one of two ways: the outgoing landlord's estate transfers the remaining security deposit to the new owner and notifies the tenant in writing, by personal delivery or first-class mail, of the transfer amount and the new owner's contact information, or the deposit gets returned directly to the tenant along with an itemized accounting of any deductions. Once a new owner receives any portion of the deposit, they take on all the same rights and obligations toward that deposit that the original landlord had. Practically, this means an heir should get written documentation of the exact deposit amount and any existing deduction history from the estate or the prior owner's records as early as possible, since the obligation to account for that money follows the property regardless of whether the funds were ever cleanly handed over.

None of this means an inherited rental has to stay a rental forever. It means the transition has to go through the same tenant protections that would apply to any other change of ownership. An heir who wants to sell can generally do so with the tenant still in place, since California law doesn't require clearing the home of tenants before a sale, though it does affect which buyers are realistic (an investor comfortable with an existing tenant, versus an owner-occupant buyer who would need the just-cause and timing rules above to apply to themselves after closing).

Multiple heirs inheriting a tenant-occupied property together add another layer worth naming directly. If the home passes to more than one sibling and only one of them wants to move in under the owner move-in exception, that typically means working out who actually holds a large enough ownership interest to exercise that option, and whether the others are bought out, sign off on it, or disagree entirely, which starts to overlap with the broader co-ownership disagreements covered in a separate post on what happens when siblings don't agree about selling an inherited home. The tenant's rights don't change based on how many heirs are involved or whether they agree with each other, the same just-cause and deposit rules apply regardless.

It's also worth being realistic about timeline and cost before assuming a sale is automatically the simpler path. A tenant-occupied sale generally takes longer to market to the broadest buyer pool, since many owner-occupant buyers either need the home vacant or need to go through the same just-cause process themselves after closing, which some aren't willing to take on. On the other hand, forcing a vacancy through a legitimate owner move-in, if one of the heirs genuinely plans to live there, comes with its own real costs: the relocation assistance payment, the 90-day move-in window, and the 12-month occupancy commitment, none of which can be shortcut once the notice is served. Heirs who are unsure which path fits their actual plans are often better served getting a real valuation on the property both ways, tenant-occupied and vacant, before deciding which direction to take, since the dollar gap between those two scenarios varies a lot by property and tenant situation.

Frequently Asked Questions

I inherited a Woodland Hills home with a tenant in it. Can I just ask them to leave since I'm the new owner?

Generally no, not if they've lived there 12 months or more. California's Tenant Protection Act (Civil Code 1946.2) requires a legally recognized just cause to end a qualifying tenancy, and inheriting the property isn't one of the recognized reasons on its own.

Does the existing lease carry over to me as the new owner?

Yes. The lease or rental agreement transfers automatically with the property. A fixed-term lease generally runs through its remaining term, and a month-to-month tenancy continues on a month-to-month basis, neither resets just because ownership changed.

Can I move into the home myself instead of keeping the tenant?

Possibly, under the owner move-in just cause. You, or a qualifying relative (spouse, domestic partner, child, grandchild, parent, or grandparent), generally have to move in within 90 days and live there as a primary residence for at least 12 continuous months, and relocation assistance equal to one month's rent is generally required for the displaced tenant.

What happens to the tenant's security deposit?

It transfers with the property under Civil Code 1950.5. The new owner either receives the remaining deposit from the estate and notifies the tenant in writing of the transfer, or returns the deposit to the tenant directly with an accounting. The obligation to account for that money follows the new owner either way.

Can I sell the home while a tenant is still living there?

Yes. California law doesn't require a home to be vacant to sell it. It does affect the buyer pool somewhat, since an owner-occupant buyer would need to satisfy the same just-cause and timing rules to eventually move in themselves.

Do local rent control rules add anything beyond these state rules?

They can. Some California cities layer additional eviction and rent-increase rules on top of the statewide baseline. This post covers the state-level framework; a specific property's situation should be confirmed against any applicable local ordinance.
