Can I Use a 401(k) Loan for My Down Payment on a First Home in Woodland Hills?

Jonathan Lopez, Woodland Hills Realtor · Jim Sandoval Group · CA DRE #02282844 · Updated November 2026

Woodland Hills first-time buyers who've already looked into using an IRA for part of a down payment often ask the natural follow-up question: what about my 401(k)? It's a reasonable thing to assume works the same way, but it doesn't. The IRS treats 401(k)s and IRAs very differently when it comes to buying a first home, and the gap between them matters enough that it changes the real math on which account, if either, actually makes sense to tap.

The IRA option, covered in an earlier post on this site, is a true withdrawal: IRC Section 72(t)(2)(F) lets a first-time buyer pull up to $10,000 out of an IRA, penalty-free, specifically for a home purchase. There is no equivalent exception for a 401(k). A straight withdrawal from a 401(k) before age 59 and a half still triggers ordinary income tax plus the standard 10% early withdrawal penalty, home purchase or not. That's the single most important thing to understand before going further: whatever a 401(k) offers a first-time buyer, it is not the same penalty-free carve-out the IRA gets.

What a 401(k) does offer, if the specific plan allows it, is a loan against the account rather than a withdrawal from it. Under IRS rules (26 U.S.C. Section 72(p)), a participant can generally borrow up to 50% of their vested balance, capped at $50,000 total, whichever number is smaller. Because it's a loan, not a distribution, there's no income tax and no early withdrawal penalty while the loan is in good standing, the money gets repaid, with interest, back into the same account, usually through automatic payroll deduction. Standard IRS repayment terms cap a plan loan at five years, but there's a specific exception built into the rule: when the loan is used to buy the participant's own primary residence, a plan is permitted to extend that repayment window well beyond five years, in some cases up to 15 years. Not every employer's plan actually offers this extension even though the IRS allows it, so this is a question for the plan administrator directly, not an assumption to make going in.

The real exposure with a 401(k) loan isn't the borrowing itself, it's what happens if the borrower's employment ends while a balance is still outstanding, whether that's a layoff, a resignation, or a new job taken for an unrelated reason. Older rules required repayment within 60 to 90 days of leaving the job, which made job changes genuinely dangerous for anyone with an outstanding loan. That rule changed under the Tax Cuts and Jobs Act of 2017: the deadline to repay (or roll the balance into an IRA to avoid tax) is now the borrower's federal tax filing deadline, including any extension, for the year the separation happened, typically well into the following spring or fall rather than a two-or-three-month window. That's real breathing room, but it's not unlimited, and missing it converts the unpaid balance into what the IRS calls a deemed distribution: ordinary income tax on the full remaining amount, plus the 10% early withdrawal penalty if the borrower is under 59 and a half, in the same tax year.

There's a cost to a 401(k) loan even when everything goes according to plan and nothing goes wrong. The borrowed amount comes out of the market for as long as the loan is outstanding, so it doesn't participate in any growth the rest of the account sees during that period. The interest paid back goes into the borrower's own account rather than to a bank, which softens this somewhat, but it's still repaid with after-tax payroll dollars into an account that will likely be taxed again on withdrawal in retirement, a double-taxation point worth knowing about even though reasonable people disagree about how much it actually matters in practice.

One more thing worth flagging honestly: there's a bill in Congress, the Home Savings Act (H.R. 7185), introduced in January 2026, that would create a penalty-free 401(k) withdrawal option for a home purchase, which would be a genuinely new path closer to what the IRA exception already allows. As of this posting, it has not been enacted. Current law still treats 401(k) loans and IRA withdrawals as two separate systems with different rules, and buyers should plan around what's actually available today, not what might pass later.

On Woodland Hills' current market (Movoto, last confirmed Oct 30, 2026: $1,299,000 median sale price, 379 active listings, 50-day average days on market, up from 44 a year ago), a standard 3.5% FHA down payment runs roughly $45,500. A full $50,000 401(k) loan could cover that on its own, which is part of why it's an appealing option on paper, but appealing on paper and the right move for a specific buyer's job stability, plan terms, and retirement timeline are two different questions. That's exactly the conversation worth having with a lender and a financial advisor before deciding which account, if any, to borrow against.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much can I actually borrow from my 401(k) for a down payment?

Generally up to 50% of your vested account balance, capped at $50,000 total, whichever amount is smaller. The exact terms depend on your specific employer's plan, not every plan allows loans at all.

Will I owe taxes or a penalty on a 401(k) loan?

Not while the loan is in good standing. It's a loan against your own balance, not a withdrawal, so there's no income tax or early withdrawal penalty as long as you keep making the scheduled repayments.

What happens if I lose my job or change jobs with an outstanding 401(k) loan balance?

You have until your federal tax filing deadline, including extensions, for the year you separated from that employer to repay the balance or roll it into an IRA. Miss that deadline and the IRS treats whatever is unpaid as a taxable distribution, plus a 10% penalty if you're under 59 and a half.

Is a 401(k) loan the same as the IRA first-time buyer exception I've heard about?

No, and this is the most common confusion. The IRA rule (IRC Section 72(t)(2)(F)) is a true penalty-free withdrawal, up to $10,000, built specifically for first-time buyers. There is no equivalent penalty-free withdrawal exception for a 401(k). What a 401(k) offers instead is a loan you repay with interest back into your own account.

Can my plan give me longer than 5 years to repay a 401(k) loan?

Possibly. The IRS allows plans to extend the standard 5-year repayment term specifically when the loan is used to buy the borrower's own primary residence, in some cases up to 15 years, but the plan itself has to offer that extension. Ask your plan administrator directly rather than assuming it applies.

Is there a new law that lets me withdraw from my 401(k) penalty-free for a home purchase, instead of taking a loan?

Not yet. The Home Savings Act (H.R. 7185), introduced in Congress in January 2026, would create something like that, but it has not been enacted as of this posting. Current rules still treat a 401(k) withdrawal before retirement age as taxable and subject to the 10% penalty, home purchase or not.
