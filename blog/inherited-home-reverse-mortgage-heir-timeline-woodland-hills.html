I Inherited a Woodland Hills Home With a Reverse Mortgage. How Long Do I Actually Have to Sell It?

Jonathan Lopez, Woodland Hills Realtor · Jim Sandoval Group · CA DRE #02282844 · Updated November 2026

The Clock Starts the Day the Last Borrower Dies

A reverse mortgage, almost always structured as a Home Equity Conversion Mortgage (HECM), doesn't get inherited the way a regular mortgage does. The moment the last surviving borrower (or eligible non-borrowing spouse) passes away, the loan becomes "due and payable" immediately, whether or not anyone has told the heirs yet. The loan servicer is then required to send a formal due-and-payable notice stating the amount owed, the options available to satisfy it, and the applicable deadlines.

That notice is the real starting gun. According to the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau, heirs generally have 30 days after receiving it to tell the lender which path they're taking: paying off the loan, selling the home, or turning the property over to the lender to satisfy the debt. That's a short window to simply decide, let alone list and sell a house, which is exactly why so many heirs feel blindsided by reverse mortgage timelines.

The Window Is Usually Longer Than 30 Days, But Not Automatically

The CFPB notes that the 30-day decision window can often be extended, commonly up to six months total, so heirs have real time to sell the home or arrange their own financing to buy it outright. Separately, lending-industry guidance describes an additional mechanism under HUD's mortgagee letter rules: up to two 90-day extensions beyond the initial period, for a combined maximum window of roughly 12 months in some cases. That additional detail comes from secondary lending sources, not a primary HUD document re-verified this session, so if your timeline is close to that outer edge, confirm the exact extension rules directly with the servicer or a HUD-approved housing counselor rather than assuming the full 12 months applies automatically. Extensions generally aren't automatic. You typically have to request them, show you're actively working toward a sale or payoff, and sometimes provide proof like a signed listing agreement.

You Are Not Personally Liable for the Balance

This is the part that surprises most heirs: a reverse mortgage is a non-recourse loan, and the borrower's heirs are not parties to it. You are never personally responsible for paying off more than the home is actually worth. If the loan balance is higher than the home's current market value, the CFPB confirms heirs can satisfy the debt by selling the property for at least 95% of its appraised value. The federal mortgage insurance that backs HECM loans covers the difference between that sale price and whatever is still owed. In practice, this means an underwater reverse mortgage on an inherited home is rarely the financial trap it sounds like at first.

Why Timing Actually Matters Here

None of this changes the reality that you're selling on a real estate clock, not just a paperwork clock. In the 91367 zip code right now, the median home price is around $1,199,000, with homes spending somewhere between about 41 and 48 days on market depending on which recent reading you look at. If your extension window gives you, say, six months, that's enough time to list, market, and close under normal conditions, but it leaves very little room for a slow start, a price miscalculation, or a deal that falls through and has to be re-listed. Heirs who move quickly to get an accurate valuation and a realistic list price tend to have a much smoother path through this than heirs who wait weeks to even start.

What To Do First

Confirm whether you've actually received the due-and-payable notice yet, since the clock in most of these timelines runs from that notice, not from the date of death itself. Contact the loan servicer directly to understand exactly which deadlines apply to your specific loan, and consider a free consultation with a HUD-approved housing counselor if the paperwork is confusing. Then get a realistic, current valuation of the home so you know whether a straightforward sale clears the loan comfortably or whether you're looking at a near-the-wire 95%-of-appraised-value situation.

Bottom Line

Inheriting a home with a reverse mortgage attached isn't a crisis, but it is a real deadline, and the deadline is usually shorter than people expect. Knowing which notice started your clock, how much extension time you can realistically request, and what the home is actually worth right now are the three things that turn this from a stressful scramble into a manageable sale.

Frequently Asked Questions

When does the clock actually start on a reverse mortgage after the borrower dies?

The loan becomes due and payable immediately when the last surviving borrower (or eligible non-borrowing spouse) dies, but the practical deadline clock starts when the servicer sends the formal due-and-payable notice, not necessarily the date of death itself.

How long do heirs actually have to sell the home?

The CFPB describes an initial 30-day window after the due-and-payable notice to tell the lender your plan, which can often be extended, commonly up to six months total. Some lending-industry sources describe additional 90-day extensions under HUD guidance, up to roughly 12 months combined, though that detail should be confirmed directly with your servicer.

Am I personally responsible for paying off the full loan balance?

No. A reverse mortgage is a non-recourse loan, and heirs are not parties to it. You're never personally liable for more than the home is worth.

What if the home is worth less than what's owed on the reverse mortgage?

You can typically satisfy the debt by selling the home for at least 95% of its current appraised value. Federal mortgage insurance covers the gap between that sale price and the remaining loan balance.

What happens if I do nothing after receiving the notice?

If heirs don't respond or act within the applicable deadline, the servicer can move toward foreclosure to satisfy the debt. Requesting an extension and showing active progress toward a sale or payoff is generally how heirs avoid that outcome.

Where do I start if I've just learned about a reverse mortgage on a home I inherited?

Confirm whether a due-and-payable notice has been sent, contact the servicer to get your specific deadlines in writing, and get a current, realistic valuation of the home so you know which path, payoff, sale, or surrender, actually makes sense.
