I Inherited a Woodland Hills Home With a Leased Solar System. Do I Have to Keep Paying For It?

Jonathan Lopez · Woodland Hills Realtor, Jim Sandoval Group · CA DRE #02282844 · Updated November 2026

A Common Inheritance Surprise in This Neighborhood

Solar is everywhere in Woodland Hills, and that's generally a selling point. But a meaningful share of residential solar in this area was never purchased outright. It was leased, or financed through a power purchase agreement (PPA), where the homeowner pays a monthly fee or per-kilowatt-hour rate to a solar company that still owns the equipment. If you've inherited a home with one of these contracts still running, the panels on the roof come with a legal relationship most heirs have never had to think about.

Whose Debt Is This, Actually?

The first question almost every heir asks is whether they personally owe the remaining lease payments. Generally, no. A solar lease or PPA becomes an obligation of the deceased owner's estate, not a personal debt automatically passed to heirs by blood. Personal liability typically only attaches if an heir affirmatively takes the contract on, by signing a formal transfer or assumption agreement, or in some cases by knowingly taking the property subject to the contract without resolving it. In other words, inheriting the house does not, by itself, make you personally responsible for the solar bill. Signing paperwork accepting that bill is what does.

The Real Problem Isn't the Payment. It's the Title.

Here's the part that catches heirs off guard when it's time to sell. Solar leases and PPAs are commonly recorded against the property itself as a UCC-1 fixture filing, a type of lien notice that shows up when a title company runs a search. It doesn't mean you personally owe money, but it does mean the system is attached to the home in a way that has to be resolved before a clean sale can close. A buyer's mortgage lender will typically want that filing cleared, either by having the lease paid off in full or formally transferred to the new owner with the solar company's approval. Fannie Mae's own underwriting guidance requires lenders to review a home's solar lease or PPA terms before approving the buyer's loan, which can reduce how much the buyer is able to borrow if the lease terms are unfavorable, adding one more reason this needs to surface early, not during escrow.

Your Three Real Paths When You're Ready to Sell

Most heirs end up choosing from three options. You can pay off the remaining lease or PPA balance before listing, clearing the lien outright and selling the home free of the obligation. You can transfer the contract to your buyer, which requires the solar company's approval and typically a credit review of the new owner, something that works for both cash buyers and financed buyers whose lender signs off on the lease terms. Or, less commonly, you can negotiate with the solar company directly if the system is near the end of its term or has unusual terms worth questioning.

What to Request Before You Decide Anything

Contact the solar company directly and ask for three things: the complete lease or PPA agreement (not a summary), a written payoff quote with a clear expiration date, and their specific transfer-approval requirements. Some heirs have reported solar companies attempting to attach conditions, like requiring a new, longer-term contract, before releasing funds or approving a transfer; reviewing the actual contract language, ideally with a probate attorney, before agreeing to anything protects you from signing into an obligation you didn't actually owe.

Why This Matters More in a Market Like This One

In the 91367 ZIP code right now, the median sale price sits around $1,199,000, with homes averaging about 41 days on market (Movoto). At that pace, a title complication discovered mid-escrow, rather than resolved before listing, is one of the more common ways a good offer falls apart or a closing date slips. Getting the solar company's payoff quote and transfer requirements in hand before you list turns this from a last-minute scramble into a normal line item on your closing statement.

Bottom Line

Inheriting a home with a leased solar system isn't a personal financial trap, but it is a real title issue that has to be handled before a sale closes cleanly. Knowing the difference between an estate obligation and personal liability, and getting the solar company's paperwork early, is what keeps this from derailing your closing later.

This isn't legal advice. A probate attorney can confirm whether your specific contract creates personal liability.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do I personally inherit my parent's solar lease when I inherit their home?

Generally no. The lease or PPA becomes an obligation of the estate, not an automatic personal debt for heirs. Personal liability typically only arises if you affirmatively sign a transfer or assumption agreement taking on the contract yourself.

What is a UCC-1 fixture filing, and why does it matter here?

It's a lien-like notice recorded against the property when a solar lease or PPA is put in place, showing up in a title search. It doesn't mean you personally owe money, but it does mean the system is legally attached to the home and has to be resolved, paid off or transferred, before a sale can close cleanly.

Can I just have the panels removed instead of dealing with the lease?

Usually not without the solar company's involvement. The panels and the contract are tied together, and removing equipment without resolving the underlying agreement can itself trigger a breach of contract. Resolving the lease directly with the company is the standard path.

Will this actually affect my ability to sell the inherited home?

It can, if it's not addressed before escrow. Buyers' lenders generally require the lease paid off or transferred with the solar company's approval before closing, and Fannie Mae's guidelines require lenders to review the lease terms as part of underwriting the buyer's loan.

What should I ask the solar company before doing anything else?

Request the complete lease or PPA agreement, a written payoff quote with an expiration date, and their specific requirements for transferring the contract to a new owner.

Does this apply to solar systems the original owner actually purchased outright?

No. An owned, fully paid-off solar system transfers with the home like any other home improvement, with no lease, PPA, or UCC-1 fixture filing to resolve. This issue is specific to leased or PPA-financed systems.
