Is My Woodland Hills Home Actually "Luxury" By LA's Own Standards?

Jonathan Lopez, Woodland Hills Realtor | Jim Sandoval Group | CA DRE #02282844 · Updated September 2026

Woodland Hills sellers hear the word "luxury" thrown around a lot, especially once a home's price crosses the million-dollar mark. It's a fair question to ask before spending real marketing dollars on it: is a $1.3-$1.5 million Woodland Hills listing actually a luxury property, or does it just feel that way because the number is big? The answer depends entirely on which yardstick you use, and the two most relevant ones point in different directions.

By the National Yardstick, Yes, Comfortably

Realtor.com's August 2026 Luxury Housing Report, released September 10, 2026, defines the national entry point to luxury housing as the price where a listing lands in the top 10% of all U.S. listings. As of August 2026, that threshold was $1,200,005, down 4.0% both month over month and year over year, marking the 29th consecutive month of decline. Woodland Hills' current median sale price of $1,348,500 (Movoto, live-verified September 16, 2026) clears that national bar by roughly $148,000. By a national measuring stick, a typical Woodland Hills home is a legitimate top-10% property.

By the Local Yardstick, Not Even Close

The same Realtor.com report ranks the Los Angeles-Long Beach-Anaheim metro as having the single highest entry point to luxury of any metro area measured nationwide: $3,919,381, more than 3 times the national threshold (a 3.3x multiple, per the report's own comparison). That's higher than Kahului-Wailuku, Hawaii ($3,908,500) and Bridgeport-Stamford-Danbury, Connecticut ($3,881,000), the next two highest metros in the country. LA's own ultraluxury tier, the top 1% of listings, starts even higher: $21,961,624, one of the widest entry-to-ultraluxury gaps (5.6x) of any major U.S. market, behind only Miami and a couple of smaller metros.

What That Gap Actually Means for a Woodland Hills Listing

A home priced at $1,348,500 sits at roughly 34% of the way to LA's own luxury entry point, meaning it is nowhere near the pool of properties competing for LA's actual luxury buyers, the ones shopping Bel Air, Holmby Hills, Beverly Hills, and Malibu inventory that starts at $3.9 million and routinely runs into eight figures. Realtor.com's own senior economist, Anthony Smith, put it this way in the report: "Luxury is not defined by one national price point. The distance between the top 10% and the top 1% shows how each market is structured." In a market like LA, where that distance is unusually wide, a home at Woodland Hills' median isn't competing anywhere near the top of the local ladder, even though it would be a legitimate luxury listing in most of the country.

A Genuinely Good Number for Woodland Hills Sellers, Buried in the Same Report

LA's own million-dollar-plus listings carry a median of 54 days on market, faster than the 72-day national median for that price tier, and faster than several other high-end metros in the report (Naples-Marco Island at 119 days, Kahului-Wailuku at 115 days, Cape Coral-Fort Myers at 136 days). Whatever tier a Woodland Hills home falls into locally, homes at this price level in the LA market are not sitting for months on end; they're moving at a healthy pace relative to comparable price points elsewhere in the country.

The Practical Marketing Takeaway

None of this means a Woodland Hills home above $1 million shouldn't be marketed well; it means the marketing plan should be built around the buyer pool that's actually shopping this price range in LA, not a generic "luxury" template built for $5 million-plus buyers. That distinction affects real decisions: how a listing is staged, what kind of photography and video budget makes sense, what comparable properties get used to set expectations, whether marketing toward an all-cash buyer even makes sense at this price point, and how the listing is positioned in marketing copy. A $1.3-$1.5 million Woodland Hills home competing against $4 million-plus Westside estates in its marketing language and visual style risks reading as either overreaching or, worse, undervaluing what the home actually offers to the buyer who's genuinely looking at this price point.

The Bottom Line

Woodland Hills homes are luxury-tier by the numbers that matter nationally, and mainstream by the numbers that matter locally. Knowing which one your marketing plan should actually be built around is the difference between a strategy that resonates with real local buyers and one built for a buyer pool that isn't looking at this price range in the first place. This is also worth thinking about alongside whether your home is even sized like the properties LA's luxury buyers expect, a related but separate question from price percentile.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is a $1.3 million home in Woodland Hills considered a luxury home?

Nationally, yes: it clears Realtor.com's national luxury threshold of $1,200,005 (August 2026 data), putting it in the top 10% of home prices across the country. Locally, no: it sits at roughly 34% of the Los Angeles metro's own $3,919,381 luxury entry point, the highest local threshold of any metro in the nation.

Why is the Los Angeles luxury threshold so much higher than the national one?

Realtor.com's report attributes the gap to LA's unusually deep pool of high-end inventory, including 634 listings priced above $10 million, one of the largest counts of any U.S. metro. That concentration of ultra-high-end properties pulls LA's own luxury percentile thresholds far above the national figure.

Does this mean my Woodland Hills home should be priced or marketed differently?

It means the marketing plan should target the buyer pool actually shopping this price range in LA, rather than the true luxury buyer pool shopping $4 million-plus Westside and Malibu inventory. Pricing itself should still be based on real local comparable sales, not on either luxury threshold.

How fast are million-dollar homes actually selling in the LA market right now?

A median of 54 days, according to Realtor.com's August 2026 data, faster than the 72-day national median for homes at that price tier and faster than several other top-10 luxury metros in the same report.

Is this the same thing as the earlier post about home size and luxury marketing?

No. The earlier post compared Woodland Hills' median home size (2,203 square feet) to the national luxury-buyer size benchmark (about 4,250 square feet). This post is about price percentile specifically, a different, complementary measure of where a home actually sits in the local market.

What's the real risk of marketing a mid-six-figure-to-low-seven-figure home as "ultraluxury"?

It can misalign buyer expectations, staging and photography spend, and comparable-property positioning with a buyer pool that isn't actually shopping in that price range in LA, while underselling what the home genuinely offers to the buyer who is.
