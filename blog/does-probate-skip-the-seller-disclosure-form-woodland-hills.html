Do I Have to Fill Out California's Standard Seller Disclosure Form When I Sell My Inherited Woodland Hills Home Through Probate?

Jonathan Lopez, Woodland Hills Realtor · Jim Sandoval Group · CA DRE #02282844 · Updated November 2026

If you're handling the sale of an inherited home in Woodland Hills, whether as the executor of an estate, a successor trustee, or a court-appointed administrator, you've probably run into the standard California seller paperwork and wondered whether all of it even applies to you. The short answer is that one major piece of it usually does not: the Transfer Disclosure Statement, commonly called the TDS, the multi-page form most California home sellers fill out describing the condition of the property room by room.

California Civil Code Section 1102.2 lays out a list of transactions exempt from the TDS requirement, and two of them cover most inherited-home sales directly. Subsection (b) exempts sales made pursuant to a court order, which explicitly includes sales ordered by a probate court during the administration of an estate. Subsection (d) separately exempts sales or transfers made by a fiduciary, meaning an executor, administrator, or trustee, in the course of administering a trust, guardianship, conservatorship, or decedent's estate. Between those two subsections, the large majority of inherited Woodland Hills homes moving through either a formal probate proceeding or a trust administration after the owner's death qualify for the exemption.

There is a real limitation worth understanding, though, especially for family trustees. The exemption in subsection (d) does not apply when the trustee is a natural person, the trust is revocable rather than one created at death, and that same trustee was either a former owner of the property or lived in it within the preceding year. That scenario comes up when a surviving family member who already lived in or co-owned the home steps into the trustee role for a still-revocable trust, as opposed to a trust that became irrevocable on the original owner's death, which is the far more common setup in a straightforward inheritance. If you're not sure which category your situation falls into, that's a question for the probate attorney or trust administration attorney handling the estate, not something to guess at.

Here's the part that trips people up even after they confirm the exemption applies: being exempt from the TDS form is not the same as being exempt from disclosure obligations generally. California law still holds a seller, fiduciary or not, to a duty not to conceal known material defects. If you know the roof leaks, that a room was added without permits, or that the foundation has a documented problem, that knowledge still has to make its way to the buyer in some form, TDS or no TDS. Real estate attorneys who write about this exemption consistently flag that it removes the required form, not the underlying honesty requirement. That's exactly why many agents, including on inherited and trust-sale listings, still have the seller fill out a shorter form, the California Association of Realtors' Exempt Seller Disclosure, sometimes called the ESD. It's not legally mandated the way the TDS is, but it gives the fiduciary a documented, dated record of what they did and didn't know about the property, which protects everyone involved if a dispute comes up after closing.

It's also worth being clear about what this exemption does not touch. The Natural Hazard Disclosure report, which tells a buyer whether the property sits in a flood zone, an earthquake fault zone, or a high fire hazard severity zone, among other designations, comes from a different part of the Civil Code and isn't waived by the TDS exemption. The same goes for Mello-Roos or HOA-related disclosures where they apply, and for lead-based paint disclosure requirements on homes built before 1978. None of those are addressed by Section 1102.2's TDS carve-out, and a fiduciary selling an inherited home still needs to confirm separately, usually with help from escrow or title, which of those apply to the specific property.

For a Woodland Hills estate or trustee sale, where the current citywide median sale price sits at $1,300,000 (Movoto, data as of November 5, 2026), getting this right matters beyond just paperwork correctness. A buyer who later discovers an undisclosed known defect has legal recourse regardless of whether the TDS was technically required, and a clean, well-documented disclosure process, even using the shorter exempt-seller form, tends to make for a smoother escrow and fewer post-closing disputes on a transaction that's often already emotionally complicated for the family involved.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do I have to fill out California's Transfer Disclosure Statement to sell an inherited Woodland Hills home?

Generally no. Civil Code Section 1102.2 exempts both court-ordered probate sales and transfers by a fiduciary, such as an executor, administrator, or trustee, administering a trust, guardianship, conservatorship, or decedent's estate, from the TDS requirement.

Does the exemption mean I don't have to disclose anything about the home's condition?

No. The exemption only removes the required form. A seller, fiduciary or not, still has a legal duty not to conceal known material defects. Many agents use the shorter C.A.R. Exempt Seller Disclosure form to document known facts even when the TDS itself isn't required.

Is there a situation where the fiduciary exemption doesn't apply?

Yes. It doesn't apply when the trustee is a natural person administering a still-revocable trust and that trustee was previously an owner or recent occupant of the property. This is a family-specific legal question best confirmed with a probate or trust attorney.

Does this exemption cover the Natural Hazard Disclosure report too?

No. The Natural Hazard Disclosure report comes from a separate part of California law and is not waived by the TDS exemption under Section 1102.2. Confirm separately with your escrow or title company which other disclosures apply.

Why would I still use the Exempt Seller Disclosure form if it's not required?

It gives the fiduciary a dated, documented record of what was and wasn't known about the property at the time of sale, which can help protect the estate and the fiduciary personally if a dispute comes up after closing.

Does this exemption apply the same way to a regular (non-inherited) home sale?

No. A typical owner-occupant seller who isn't acting under a court order or as a fiduciary for an estate or trust does not qualify for this exemption and must complete the standard TDS.
