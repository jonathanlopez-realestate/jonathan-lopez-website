Should I Offer Buyer-Agent Compensation on My Woodland Hills Listing, or Let Buyers Negotiate It Themselves?

Jonathan Lopez, Woodland Hills Realtor · Jim Sandoval Group · CA DRE #02282844 · Updated November 2026

If you're getting ready to list a home in Woodland Hills, there's a decision buried in the listing paperwork that's easy to overlook: whether to offer the buyer's agent a commission at all, and if so, how much. Before August 2024, this wasn't really a decision most sellers made consciously, offering roughly 2.5% to 3% to the buyer's side was close to automatic, and it got advertised right inside the MLS listing itself. The settlement of several class-action lawsuits against the National Association of Realtors changed the mechanics of that, and a lot of sellers are now unsure what actually changed and what's still up to them.

Here's the part that changed: offers of buyer-agent compensation can no longer be listed as a field inside the MLS. A listing can no longer say, in the MLS data itself, "seller offers 2.5% to buyer's agent." That field was removed industry-wide as part of the settlement's practice changes.

Here's the part that didn't change as much as a lot of headlines suggested: a seller can still offer buyer-agent compensation. It just has to happen off-MLS, through a sign, a flyer, a listing website, or a direct conversation between agents, rather than through the old MLS field. And according to Redfin's own commission tracking, most sellers are still doing exactly that. Redfin's data (covering recent quarters through 2025) put the national average buyer's agent commission at approximately 2.42% to 2.43%, which is essentially back to where rates sat before the settlement after a brief dip to around 2.36% in the months right after the rule change took effect. Separately, Clever Real Estate's February 2026 survey of 533 real estate agents found a national average total commission of 5.70%, splitting out to roughly 2.88% for the listing side and 2.82% for the buyer's side, numbers that track closely with Redfin's own figures.

So why does the rate keep landing in roughly the same place the old norm sat at, if sellers aren't required to offer anything anymore? The practical answer agents give is pool size. A buyer whose own agent expects to be paid, and isn't offered compensation by the seller, has a few options: ask the seller to cover it anyway as part of the offer, negotiate it into their own closing costs, or walk away from that listing and look at one where compensation is offered. Any of those outcomes works against you as the seller. Either your net proceeds end up lower once that cost gets folded into the offer price buyers are actually willing to write, or your pool of interested, financeable buyers simply shrinks, since not every buyer in Woodland Hills' current price range has the cash on hand to cover their own agent's fee on top of a down payment and closing costs.

That's the actual tradeoff worth weighing, not a question of whether the rule requires you to pay anything, because it doesn't. Offering compensation is a cost you control and set upfront, visible to you before an offer ever comes in. Not offering it doesn't make that cost disappear, it just moves the negotiation to happen inside each individual offer instead, where it's harder to predict and harder to compare apples to apples across multiple offers.

None of this means every seller should default to offering the old 2.5% to 3% without thinking about it. Your specific price point, how competitive your property is expected to be, and how thin or deep the buyer pool is for homes like yours right now all factor into whether a smaller offer, a larger one, or none at all makes sense for your situation.

There's also a mechanical wrinkle worth knowing about before you list. Because the offer can no longer live inside the MLS data field, buyer's agents can't just filter listings by "compensation offered" the way they used to when scanning the MLS for homes to show their clients. That information now has to travel through other channels: your listing agent communicating it directly to buyer's agents who ask, a line on your own marketing materials, or a conversation that happens once a buyer's agent is already interested enough to call. In practice this means the offer still reaches serious buyer's agents, but it reaches them a step later in the process than it used to, through a conversation rather than a filter, which is part of why having your listing agent proactively communicate your position (rather than waiting to be asked) matters more now than it did before the settlement.

It's also worth being clear about who actually negotiates this now, since that part genuinely did change. Before the settlement, the amount a seller was offering buyer's agents was effectively set and visible to everyone the moment the listing went live. Now, because that figure sits outside the MLS, there's more room for it to vary listing to listing, and even buyer by buyer, since a buyer's agent working with a highly motivated buyer might accept a smaller offered amount, or none, in a way they wouldn't have had to negotiate before. That flexibility cuts both directions. It can work in a seller's favor in a market where buyer's agents are competing hard for business, and it can work against a seller in a market, like parts of Woodland Hills right now, where days on market have stretched out and a thinner buyer pool gives agents less incentive to flex on their own fee.

Locally, Movoto's Woodland Hills page, updated October 30, 2026, shows a median sale price of $1,299,000 for September, 379 active listings, and an average 50 days on market, up from 44 a year ago, a market where the size of your buyer pool is still a real variable, not an afterthought. Frequently Asked Questions

Can I still offer the buyer's agent a commission after the NAR settlement?

Yes. The settlement removed the ability to list a compensation offer as a field inside the MLS itself, but a seller can still offer compensation off-MLS, through a sign, a flyer, a listing website, or direct agent-to-agent communication.

What's the typical commission sellers are offering now?

Redfin's recent data puts the national average buyer's agent commission at roughly 2.42% to 2.43%, close to pre-settlement levels after a brief dip. Clever's February 2026 survey of 533 agents found a 5.70% average total commission, split about 2.88% listing side and 2.82% buyer's side. These are national figures, not Woodland Hills-specific.

What happens if I don't offer any buyer-agent compensation?

A buyer whose own agent expects to be paid has to either negotiate it into their offer, cover it out of pocket, or look at other listings that do offer it. Any of those can mean a lower effective offer to you or fewer buyers considering your home at all.

Is offering compensation the same as the old pre-settlement practice?

Functionally similar in amount for many sellers, but mechanically different: it's now a direct, off-MLS offer you control rather than an MLS-advertised field. You can also choose not to offer it, which wasn't really a practical option before.

Does the amount I offer affect my asking price or net proceeds?

It can factor into your net proceeds the same way any seller-paid cost does, but it's a cost you set and see upfront, unlike a cost buried inside a lower net offer from a buyer covering their own agent's fee.

How do I decide what's right for my specific listing?

It depends on your price point, how competitive your home is expected to be, and current buyer pool conditions in your area. I can walk through the actual tradeoffs for your specific home before you decide.
