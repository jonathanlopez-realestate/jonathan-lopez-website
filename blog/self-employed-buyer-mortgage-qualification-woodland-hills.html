I'm Self-Employed. How Do I Qualify for a Mortgage to Buy My First Home in Woodland Hills?

Jonathan Lopez, Realtor serving Woodland Hills with the Jim Sandoval Group · CA DRE #02282844 · Updated October 2026

Buying a first home in Woodland Hills while running your own business comes with a question almost every self-employed buyer asks at some point: will my income even count? The honest answer is yes, but it gets documented differently than a W-2 paycheck, and which path a lender uses can change both how much you qualify for and how fast the process moves. Buyers who skip this research often walk into a pre-approval conversation assuming the worst, a flat no or a drastically reduced loan amount, when the real issue is usually just picking the documentation path that matches how their specific business actually reports income.

Start with who actually counts as "self-employed" for underwriting purposes. Fannie Mae's Selling Guide, Section B3-3.2-01, draws a specific line: anyone with a 25% or greater ownership interest in a business, whether that's a sole proprietorship, partnership, S corporation, or corporation, is treated as self-employed for income documentation purposes. That threshold matters because it catches people who might not think of themselves as "business owners," a part-owner in a small practice or a majority partner in an LLC, not just someone running a solo freelance operation.

The standard conventional path requires two years of signed personal and business federal tax returns, with all applicable schedules attached, or IRS transcripts covering the same period. A lender doesn't just read the bottom line. The file gets run through a cash flow analysis, commonly Fannie Mae's Form 1084 or an equivalent, which adjusts the reported taxable income for items like depreciation and other non-cash or non-recurring entries to arrive at a stable, continuing income figure. In most cases, that figure becomes a two-year average, not simply the most recent year's number, which is why a buyer having one strong year and one weaker year doesn't automatically get full credit for the stronger year.

There's a narrower exception that lets a lender use only one year of returns instead of two. It requires the business to have existed for at least five consecutive years, and the borrower to have held that same 25% or greater ownership stake for the entire five years. Even then, a cash flow analysis is still required on the single year provided. For a buyer whose business is newer than five years, or who only recently crossed the 25% ownership threshold, the two-year standard applies.

For buyers whose tax returns understate their actual cash flow, which is common when a business legitimately maximizes deductions, a second underwriting path exists outside Fannie Mae's conventional framework: bank statement loan programs, a category of non-qualified mortgage (non-QM) lending built specifically around this problem. Instead of tax returns, these programs qualify a borrower using 12 to 24 months of personal or business bank statement deposits. Lenders commonly require a minimum 640 credit score and at least 10% down, and the qualifying income gets calculated by taking total eligible deposits, applying an expense factor (a flat 50% is the common default across published 2026 program guides, meaning only half of deposited revenue counts as income before ownership percentage and loan term math), and spreading that over the 12 or 24-month statement period. The tradeoff for skipping tax-return documentation is pricing: published 2026 guides put non-QM bank statement rates roughly 1 to 2 percentage points above a comparable conventional rate.

Neither path is universally better. A self-employed buyer with strong net income on paper and at least two full years in the same ownership structure will usually get a better rate through the conventional Fannie Mae path. A buyer whose tax strategy legitimately minimizes taxable income, or whose business is newer than the five-year exception window, may actually qualify for more home, despite the rate premium, through a bank statement program that looks at real cash flow instead of post-deduction taxable income. The right move is finding out which situation you're actually in before assuming either path is closed to you.

It's also worth knowing who this 25% threshold leaves out. A buyer who holds a W-2 job but earns extra 1099 income on the side from a small ownership stake under 25%, or freelance work that isn't tied to owning a business at all, generally doesn't get pulled into the full self-employed documentation path for that income. Lenders still want to see it's stable and likely to continue, usually through a shorter history than two full years in some cases, but it isn't automatically subject to the same two-year averaging and cash flow analysis that applies once someone crosses the 25% ownership line. Buyers sometimes assume any non-W-2 income triggers the stricter self-employed rules; the actual trigger is ownership percentage, not how the income shows up on a 1099.

Practically, a self-employed buyer in either scenario is better off gathering documentation before shopping for a home, not after finding one. That means pulling the last two years of personal and business returns with every schedule, year-to-date profit and loss if a lender asks for one, and, if a bank statement path looks more realistic, 12 to 24 months of statements for every account income actually runs through. Getting pre-qualified under both paths, conventional and bank statement, before writing an offer avoids discovering mid-escrow that the qualifying income came in lower than expected under one method and higher under the other.

Frequently Asked Questions

I'm self-employed. Does that automatically mean I need two years of tax returns to buy a home?

Under Fannie Mae's standard rule, yes, two years of personal and business returns with all schedules. A narrower exception allows one year only if your business has operated at least five years and you've held 25%+ ownership that entire time.

What counts as "self-employed" if I'm a partner in a business, not the sole owner?

Fannie Mae's threshold is 25% or greater ownership interest in any business, sole proprietorship, partnership, S corp, or corporation. If you own at least a quarter of the business, the self-employed documentation rules apply to you, even if you also receive a W-2 from that same business.

My tax returns show less income than I actually make because of deductions. Does that hurt me?

It can under the conventional path, since underwriting generally works from the income shown on your returns after a cash flow analysis adjustment, not your gross revenue. This is exactly the gap bank statement loan programs are built to address, since they qualify you on actual deposits instead.

What is a bank statement loan, and who qualifies?

It's a non-QM mortgage that uses 12 to 24 months of bank deposits instead of tax returns to establish income. Programs commonly require a 640 minimum credit score and at least 10% down, with income calculated by applying an expense factor (commonly 50%) to your total deposits.

Is a bank statement loan more expensive than a regular mortgage?

Generally yes. Published 2026 non-QM program guides put bank statement loan rates roughly 1 to 2 percentage points above a comparable conventional rate, reflecting the lower documentation standard.

Which path should I actually use?

It depends on how your tax returns compare to your real cash flow and how long you've owned your business at 25%+ ownership. A strong two-year income history usually favors the conventional Fannie Mae path and its better rate; a business that legitimately shows lower taxable income, or one younger than five years at your ownership stake, often qualifies for more home through a bank statement program despite the rate premium.
