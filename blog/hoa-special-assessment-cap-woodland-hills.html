My Woodland Hills Condo's HOA Just Approved a Special Assessment. Is There a Limit on How Big It Can Get?

Jonathan Lopez, Woodland Hills Realtor · Jim Sandoval Group · CA DRE #02282844 · Updated November 2026

If you're buying, selling, or already own a condo in Woodland Hills, there's a specific moment that catches a lot of people off guard: a letter arrives from the HOA announcing a special assessment, sometimes for a few hundred dollars, sometimes for tens of thousands, and it isn't always obvious whether the board can simply decide that number on its own. It turns out California law does put a real limit on that authority, and knowing where the line sits matters whether you're the one reading that letter or the one deciding whether to make an offer on a unit where one just landed.

Condo and townhome associations in California operate under the Davis-Stirling Common Interest Development Act, and one of its more practical consumer protections lives in Civil Code Section 5605. That section draws a clear boundary around what a board can do without asking the membership first. A board may raise regular (ongoing) assessments by up to 20% over the prior year's regular assessment without a membership vote. For special assessments, the cap is tighter: a board can impose a special assessment totaling up to 5% of the association's budgeted gross expenses for that fiscal year on its own authority. Go above either number, and the board needs approval from members casting more than 50% of the association's votes, typically gathered through a ballot process under the association's bylaws.

There's an important exception, and it's the one that explains most of the genuinely large, scary special assessment notices people actually receive: emergency situations. Civil Code Section 5610 allows a board to levy an assessment beyond the normal caps, without a prior membership vote, when the assessment is necessary to repair or maintain the common areas or a facility affecting health or safety, and the need could not have been reasonably foreseen by the board when it adopted its regular budget. A burst sewer main, a structural repair ordered by the city, storm damage, that kind of situation. The law still requires the board to issue written notice to members explaining the basis for the emergency assessment.

So when you see a special assessment that looks larger than 5% of the budget, the first question isn't "is this legal," it's "which category is this," a membership-approved assessment (there should be a vote on record) or a board-declared emergency (there should be a written explanation of what made it unforeseeable). If neither applies and the board simply levied more than 5% without either process, that's worth raising directly with the board or a common-interest-development attorney before you pay it.

For buyers specifically, this connects directly to the document review you're already supposed to be doing. Civil Code Section 4525 requires the seller to give you the association's governing documents, a written statement of current regular and special assessments, any unpaid assessments or fines, assessment changes the board has already approved but that aren't yet due, and the board's meeting minutes from the past 12 months on request (our separate post on that disclosure duty walks through the full list). The assessment statement will tell you what's currently owed or approved. The minutes will often tell you what's coming, a board discussing a reserve shortfall or a major repair bid for several months before it turns into a formal special assessment notice.

That reserve connection matters more than it might seem. Associations that frequently reach for special assessments are often associations whose reserve fund hasn't kept pace with the building's actual repair needs, the same underfunded-reserve pattern that can also make a building harder to finance conventionally (our Fannie Mae condo-review post covers that angle). A pattern of repeated special assessments in the minutes is a signal worth taking seriously, not just a one-time inconvenience.

If a special assessment gets approved after you're already in escrow, it typically becomes a negotiation point rather than an automatic deal-breaker. Depending on timing and what your purchase contract says, you and the seller may need to work out who's responsible for an assessment that's already been levied versus one that's merely been discussed, and whether it's paid in a lump sum or financed by the HOA over time with monthly installments that would then show up as part of your ongoing dues. Your agent should flag this in writing during the contingency period rather than letting it get resolved informally.

The practical takeaway for a Woodland Hills condo buyer: don't just skim the assessment statement for a dollar figure. Check whether the association has stayed inside the 5% special-assessment and 20% regular-dues caps on its own authority, or whether a given assessment came through a membership vote or a documented emergency. For owners thinking about selling, a clean assessment history (or a well-documented emergency when one was unavoidable) is one more thing that makes your building look healthy to a buyer's lender and a buyer's agent, not just to the buyer themselves.

This is general information about how California's common interest development law works, not legal advice about any specific HOA, assessment, or building. Read your own association's governing documents and the specific notice you received, and talk to a real estate attorney if the numbers involved are significant.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can my HOA board just decide on any special assessment amount it wants?

No. Under Civil Code Section 5605, a board can impose a special assessment of up to 5% of that year's budgeted gross expenses on its own authority. Anything larger needs approval from owners holding more than half the association's votes, unless it qualifies as an emergency assessment under Section 5610.

What counts as an "emergency" assessment that can skip the vote?

One necessary to repair or maintain the common areas or a facility affecting health or safety, where the board could not have reasonably foreseen the need when it set its regular budget. The board still has to provide written notice explaining the basis for it.

How is this different from the HOA documents a seller has to give me?

Civil Code Section 4525 requires the seller to disclose current and approved assessments as part of the document package. That's a disclosure duty. Section 5605's 5% and 20% caps are a separate question: a legal limit on what the board can impose without a membership vote in the first place.

Can regular monthly dues also go up without a vote?

Yes, up to 20% over the prior year's regular assessment, separate from any special assessment, under the same Civil Code section.

What should I do if a special assessment shows up mid-escrow?

Flag it with your agent immediately and review whether it was already approved and billed, or just proposed. Your purchase contract may allow you to negotiate who covers it, and the timing (lump sum versus financed installments) affects your ongoing costs.

Does a history of special assessments affect a condo's value or financeability?

It can. Associations with a pattern of special assessments often have underfunded reserves, which can also affect whether the building qualifies for standard conventional financing (see our post on Fannie Mae's condo project review standards).
