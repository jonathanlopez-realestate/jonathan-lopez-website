Will Medi-Cal Take Money From Our Inherited Woodland Hills Home?

Jonathan Lopez, Woodland Hills Realtor | Jim Sandoval Group | CA DRE #02282844 · Updated September 2026

If you're settling a parent's estate in Woodland Hills and a Medi-Cal Estate Recovery notice shows up in the mail, the instinct is to panic. Most of what circulates online about "Medi-Cal taking your house" is either outdated or exaggerated. The real rule, straight from the California Department of Health Care Services' own program page, is narrower than most people assume, and it hinges on a few specific facts about how the home was titled and what benefits your parent actually received.

What Estate Recovery Actually Is

DHCS is required to seek repayment from the estates of certain deceased Medi-Cal beneficiaries. That repayment only applies to benefits received on or after the beneficiary's 55th birthday, and only from beneficiaries who owned assets at the time of death. If someone died owning nothing in their own name, nothing is owed, no matter what benefits they received over their lifetime.

The Titling Distinction That Matters Most

Since a 2017 change to California law (SB 833), DHCS repayment is limited only to estate assets that pass through probate. This is the single biggest factor in whether your family's inherited Woodland Hills home is exposed at all. If the home was held in a living trust, owned in joint tenancy with right of survivorship, or transferred using a transfer-on-death deed, it passes to heirs outside of probate, and DHCS has nothing to attach. This is the same trust-versus-probate distinction that determines how long the transfer takes and what it costs, and it does double duty here: proper titling protects against both a lengthy probate process and a Medi-Cal claim.

Which Benefits Actually Create a Claim

Even when a home does go through probate, not every Medi-Cal recipient's estate gets a claim. DHCS repayment is limited to specific services: nursing facility care, home and community based services, and the related hospital and prescription drug costs tied to that care. General Medi-Cal health coverage, routine doctor visits, or benefits received before turning 55 do not create a claim. A parent who used Medi-Cal for standard health insurance in their 40s or 50s, but never received nursing home or in-home long-term care, typically has no Estate Recovery exposure at all.

What Happens If a Claim Does Apply

Whoever is handling the deceased's affairs is required to submit a Notice of Death to DHCS within 90 days, along with a copy of the death certificate. DHCS then determines whether a claim exists and, if so, sends a claim letter. Importantly, DHCS has the authority to waive its own claim entirely if collecting it would create a substantial hardship for the heirs, and that hardship waiver request has to be submitted within 60 days of the date on the claim letter. Certain assets tied to American Indian and Alaska Native beneficiaries are separately exempt under federal rules.

How Big a Program This Actually Is

It helps to see the scale before assuming the worst. In the last fiscal year before the 2017 reform narrowed the program to probate-only assets, DHCS collected roughly $59 million statewide through Estate Recovery, against total Medi-Cal spending of about $56 billion that same year, well under 1% of the program's budget, spread across roughly 4,000 closed cases averaging about $15,000 each. Since the 2017 change, recovery is limited to a smaller subset of that already modest total, since anything passing outside probate no longer counts. This is a narrow, specific program aimed at recovering a fraction of long-term care costs, not a broad estate tax on every Medi-Cal recipient's family.

The Timing Usually Isn't the Bottleneck

California probate itself typically takes 12 to 18 months to complete, start to finish. The 90-day window to submit a Notice of Death to DHCS sits comfortably inside that timeline, and DHCS confirming or waiving a claim generally isn't what holds up a sale. In most cases, the probate process itself, getting an executor formally appointed by the court, sets the real pace, with any Estate Recovery question resolved well before the home is ready to list.

This Is a Separate Issue From an Existing Mortgage

It's worth keeping Estate Recovery distinct from another common inherited-home question: what happens to a parent's existing mortgage. Those are two unrelated legal mechanisms. A home can be completely clear of any Medi-Cal claim and still have a loan balance the heirs need to address, or the reverse, a home with no mortgage at all could still have a probate-only Estate Recovery claim against it if the ownership and benefit history line up that way. Don't assume clearing one question answers the other. And if the estate has multiple heirs, this question is also separate from what happens when co-inheriting siblings disagree about selling.

Where This Leaves a Woodland Hills Family

None of this changes the real estate side of what happens next. If the home passes outside probate, or a claim gets confirmed not to apply, you're free to move toward a sale on a normal timeline. If a claim is confirmed, it becomes a line item to resolve, similar to a lien or outstanding debt of the estate, before or at closing, not necessarily a reason to delay listing. The people who can actually answer "does this apply to us" are a probate attorney, who can review how the home was titled and what benefits your parent received, and DHCS itself, which will confirm any claim in writing rather than leaving a family to guess from a form letter. Once that question is answered, the real estate side (pricing, timing, and handling a sale on a property that may not have been updated in years) is where a local agent's help actually matters.

The bigger mistake families make isn't underestimating a real claim, it's overestimating how often one applies and delaying a sale out of fear over a scenario that may not even exist. Get the actual answer in writing first, then move forward on whatever timeline the home itself, not a worst-case assumption, actually calls for.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does every Medi-Cal recipient's estate get an Estate Recovery claim filed against it?

No. A claim only exists if the beneficiary owned assets that pass through probate at death, and only for specific long-term care and home and community based services received on or after age 55.

Does putting the home in a trust protect it from Medi-Cal Estate Recovery?

Generally yes. Since 2017, California law limits DHCS repayment to assets that pass through probate. Homes held in a living trust, joint tenancy with right of survivorship, or transferred by a transfer-on-death deed pass outside probate, so DHCS has nothing to attach.

Does Medi-Cal health coverage before age 55 ever create a claim?

No. Repayment only applies to nursing facility care, home and community based services, and related hospital and prescription costs received on or after the beneficiary's 55th birthday. Routine health coverage at any age doesn't count.

What's the deadline to notify the state after a parent's death?

The person handling the estate must submit a Notice of Death to DHCS within 90 days of the date of death, along with a copy of the death certificate.

Can a hardship waiver stop the state from collecting?

Yes. DHCS can waive a claim entirely if paying it would create a substantial hardship for the heirs. The waiver request must be submitted within 60 days of the date on the DHCS claim letter.

Who do I actually need to talk to before listing an inherited home in this situation?

A probate attorney can confirm how the home was titled and whether your parent's specific Medi-Cal history creates a real claim. DHCS will also confirm any claim in writing. Once that's settled, I can help with pricing, timing, and the sale process itself.
