Does Converting My Garage Into Living Space Actually Raise My Woodland Hills Home's Value?

Jonathan Lopez, Woodland Hills Realtor · Jim Sandoval Group · CA DRE #02282844 · Updated November 2026

A garage conversion is one of the most common home improvement projects homeowners in Woodland Hills consider, usually to pick up an extra bedroom, a home office, or a small rental unit without touching the footprint of the house. It can also be one of the more confusing projects to think through from a value standpoint, because the same basic change, turning a garage into finished living space, can either raise what your home appraises for or quietly work against it, depending almost entirely on one thing: whether it was permitted, and permitted as what.

The legal path: a garage conversion ADU. California's state Accessory Dwelling Unit law, codified at Government Code Section 65852.2, has specifically encouraged garage conversions since the major 2020 reforms (originating in bills like SB 13 and AB 68, and amended in subsequent sessions since) because they're the lowest-cost way to add a legal second unit. No new foundation is typically needed, and the framing, roof, and utility stubs are often already roughed in. Our earlier post on ADUs cites a 2026 Los Angeles cost guide putting a garage-conversion ADU at roughly $100,000, compared with $120,000 to $400,000 for a ground-up detached or attached ADU. State law also includes a provision that specifically helps garage conversions pencil out: when a garage, carport, or covered parking structure is converted in connection with building an ADU, cities generally cannot require you to replace the parking spaces you just lost. That removes what would otherwise be one of the more expensive constraints on this kind of project, having to build new parking elsewhere on the lot.

Done this way, with permits and as a legally recognized ADU, a garage conversion behaves appraisal-wise much like the ground-up ADUs covered in our earlier post: it typically adds counted, finished square footage and can support rental income, both of which appraisers and comparable-sales analysis can credit.

The informal path: an unpermitted conversion. This is where most of the risk actually sits, and it's extremely common. A homeowner frames in the garage, adds drywall, flooring, and maybe a window unit for heat and air, and starts using it as a bedroom or office, without pulling a permit or filing anything with the city. It feels like free square footage. From an appraisal standpoint, it usually isn't.

Appraisers working from standard Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac guidelines generally will not count unpermitted finished space toward a home's Gross Living Area, the figure that drives most of the dollar-per-square-foot comparison against similar homes nearby. Instead, that space often gets treated, if it's credited at all, as a minor functional amenity rather than genuine living area, and the appraiser is expected to note the discrepancy between what the public record shows (a garage) and what's actually there. On top of that, losing a covered garage space without it being recognized as a legal ADU is frequently treated as a negative adjustment in the comparable-sales approach, specifically because the home now offers less covered parking than similar homes in the same neighborhood that still have an intact garage. You can end up in the unusual position of adding square footage that doesn't get counted while simultaneously losing credit for the parking that disappeared to make room for it.

Los Angeles, like most California cities, generally requires a minimum amount of covered or garage parking for single-family homes under its municipal code. An unpermitted conversion that removes that covered parking without replacing it through a proper ADU permit can leave the property out of compliance, a problem that tends to resurface exactly when you don't want it to: during a buyer's inspection, during a lender's appraisal, or during a routine permit pull for something unrelated.

What this means at resale. If you're selling a home with an unpermitted garage conversion, California's seller disclosure rules (covered in more depth in our post on what you're legally required to disclose) generally require you to disclose known unpermitted work. A buyer's lender may also require the space be restored to a legal garage, or properly permitted as an ADU, as a condition of loan approval, particularly on FHA and VA loans where appraisers tend to scrutinize code compliance more closely. None of that necessarily kills a sale, but it does tend to slow it down and give a buyer's agent real leverage in negotiation, exactly the kind of thing that's better to resolve before you list than to discover mid-escrow.

The practical choice. If you want the extra space and you're willing to do it properly, converting the garage as a permitted ADU is usually the more value-positive route: lower cost than building new, no mandatory parking replacement under state law, and square footage an appraiser can actually count. If you've already done an informal conversion, the question worth asking before you list isn't whether to disclose it (you generally have to), it's whether it's worth the cost to legalize it after the fact, versus restoring the garage and taking the parking credit back, versus selling as-is and letting the price reflect the uncertainty. That's a decision best made with real numbers for your specific property, not a general rule of thumb.

Locally, Movoto's Woodland Hills city data, last pulled November 5, 2026, puts the median sale price near $1,300,000 with 368 active listings and an average 54 days on market. In a market where buyers are comparing multiple similar homes, a converted garage without clean permits is one more variable a buyer's lender, appraiser, or agent is likely to flag, which can affect both your price and your timeline.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does converting my garage automatically make it an ADU?

No. It only becomes a legal ADU if it's permitted as one through your city's ADU process. An unpermitted conversion, even one that looks finished and livable, doesn't carry the same legal status or appraisal treatment.

If I convert my garage into an ADU, do I have to build new parking somewhere else?

Generally no. California's state ADU law specifically exempts garage, carport, or covered-parking conversions done in connection with an ADU from most parking-replacement requirements.

Will an appraiser count my unpermitted garage conversion as extra square footage?

Usually not as part of Gross Living Area. Standard appraisal practice generally excludes unpermitted finished space from that figure, and may separately note the lost covered parking as a negative adjustment.

What does it cost to convert a garage into a legal ADU versus building a new one?

Roughly $100,000 for a garage-conversion ADU in the Los Angeles area versus $120,000 to $400,000 for a ground-up unit, per the 2026 cost guide cited in our earlier ADU post. Costs vary by scope, finish level, and contractor.

Can I still sell a home with an unpermitted garage conversion?

Yes, but you generally have to disclose it, and a buyer's lender may require it be restored to a legal garage or properly permitted before closing, particularly on FHA or VA loans.

Does this apply to detached garages too, or only attached ones?

The same basic principles (permitting status, Gross Living Area treatment, parking requirements) apply to both, though a detached garage conversion is more often treated and permitted as a standalone ADU, while an attached conversion sometimes gets handled as a simple room addition instead. Which path fits depends on your specific property and local zoning.
