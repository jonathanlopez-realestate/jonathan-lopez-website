Can My Real Estate Agent Represent Both Me and the Seller on the Same Woodland Hills Home?

Jonathan Lopez, Woodland Hills Realtor · Jim Sandoval Group · CA DRE #02282844 · Updated October 2026

Woodland Hills buyers who fall for a listing sometimes run into a wrinkle they did not expect: the agent holding the sign is with the same brokerage, or is literally the same person, as the agent representing the seller. That is not a conflict a lawyer has to invent, California law addresses it directly, and it is worth understanding before you write an offer on a home where it applies.

California permits what is called dual agency, one agent or brokerage representing both the buyer and the seller in the same transaction. It is legal, but it is not automatic, silent, or something that only gets disclosed if you happen to ask. Civil Code section 2079.17 requires the agent to tell both the buyer and the seller, as soon as practical, whether they are acting as a dual agent, and that disclosure has to be confirmed in writing. The confirmation can live inside the purchase contract itself or in a separate signed document, but either way it has to happen before or at the same time the contract is executed, not after the fact.

The statute even specifies the form the confirmation takes: a checkbox-style acknowledgment naming the brokerage and the individual agent, and stating plainly whether each is acting for the buyer, the seller, or both. If you sign a California purchase contract and that box is checked for dual agency, that is not boilerplate, it is the actual legal notice that the person representing you is also representing the other side.

The more practical question for most buyers is what a dual agent can and cannot do with what you tell them. Civil Code section 2079.21 answers that directly: without your express permission, a dual agent cannot disclose your confidential information to the seller. The statute defines confidential information specifically as facts about a client's financial position, motivations, or bargaining position that could affect price, the example the code itself gives is whether a buyer is willing to pay more than the price offered. The same wall runs the other direction, your dual agent also cannot tell you that the seller would actually take less than their asking price without the seller's permission.

What this means for a Woodland Hills buyer is straightforward in practice, even if the underlying legal mechanics are not something most people think about. Dual agency is not inherently improper in California, and plenty of transactions close this way without issue. But it is also not something you are stuck with by default. If you would rather have an agent whose only job is representing your interests, with no confidentiality wall limiting what they can tell you about the seller's side, you are entitled to ask for your own buyer's agent, separate from the listing brokerage, on any home you are considering. That is a conversation worth having before you are deep into writing an offer, not after you notice the name on the listing sign matches the name on your own paperwork.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is dual agency?

One real estate agent, or one brokerage, legally representing both the buyer and the seller in the same transaction. California permits it under Civil Code sections 2079.13 through 2079.24, but only with disclosure and consent.

Does my agent have to tell me if they are a dual agent?

Yes. Civil Code 2079.17 requires the agent to disclose, as soon as practical, whether they are acting as a dual agent, and that has to be confirmed in writing before or at the same time you sign the purchase contract.

Can a dual agent tell the seller what I am willing to pay?

Not without your express permission. Civil Code 2079.21 defines that as confidential information (facts about your financial position, motivation, or bargaining position) and bars a dual agent from sharing it without consent. The same protection applies to the seller's information.

Can I refuse dual agency and get my own agent?

Yes. You can ask for your own buyer's agent, separate from the listing brokerage, on any home you are considering, rather than proceeding with a dual agency arrangement.

Is dual agency the same as both agents working for the same brokerage?

Not necessarily the same thing, but related. Dual agency specifically means one agent, or one brokerage acting through its agents, representing both sides. Two different agents at the same large brokerage can sometimes trigger a related disclosure depending on the brokerage's internal policies.

Is this legal advice?

No. This explains what the statutes require as general information. For a specific transaction, review the actual disclosure form with your agent and, if you want it, independent legal counsel.
