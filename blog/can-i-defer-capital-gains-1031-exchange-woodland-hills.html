Can I Defer Capital Gains on My Woodland Hills Rental With a 1031 Exchange?

Jonathan Lopez, Woodland Hills Realtor | Jim Sandoval Group | CA DRE #02282844 · Updated September 2026

Woodland Hills has a real base of investment property owners, people who bought a second home here as a rental, inherited a property they never moved into, or held onto a former residence and converted it to a rental after moving. For that group, selling eventually means facing a capital gains bill that a primary-residence seller doesn't have to think about in the same way. A Section 1031 like-kind exchange is the tool that can defer that tax, but it comes with two sets of rules stacked on top of each other: the federal deadlines, and a separate California reporting requirement that catches a real number of investors off guard.

What a 1031 Exchange Actually Does

Named for IRC Section 1031, a like-kind exchange lets an investor sell a property held for investment or business use and roll the proceeds into a new investment property without immediately paying capital gains tax on the sale. The gain isn't eliminated, it's deferred: the tax liability carries forward into the replacement property's basis and becomes due when that property is eventually sold in a transaction that isn't itself another exchange. This is fundamentally different from the primary-residence capital gains exclusion under Section 121, which permanently excludes up to $250,000 or $500,000 of gain rather than deferring it, and which only applies to a home you've actually lived in, not a rental.

The Federal Deadlines Are Strict, and They Run Simultaneously

From the moment the relinquished property's sale closes, an investor has exactly 45 calendar days to identify replacement property in writing, signed and delivered to a qualified intermediary. Separately, but starting on the same closing date, there's a 180-day window to actually close on the replacement property (or the tax filing deadline for that year, including extensions, if that comes first). These two windows run concurrently, not one after the other, and neither one gets extended even if the deadline lands on a weekend or a holiday. Missing the 45-day identification deadline generally disqualifies the entire exchange, turning what was meant to be a deferral into an immediate, unplanned tax bill.

The California-Specific Catch: Form FTB 3840

This is the part of the process that trips up out-of-state exchanges specifically, and it's a real, ongoing filing obligation, not a one-time form. Per the California Franchise Tax Board's own instructions: any taxpayer, regardless of residency, who exchanges real property located in California for like-kind property located outside California must file Form FTB 3840, and must keep filing it every single subsequent tax year the gain remains deferred, until the replacement property is eventually sold, inherited, or donated. This isn't a form filed once at the time of the exchange and forgotten. An investor who exchanges a Woodland Hills rental for a property in, say, Nevada or Texas has to keep filing this form annually for as long as they hold that replacement property, even in years with no other California filing requirement at all.

What Happens If the Annual Filing Lapses

Per the FTB's own instructions, if a taxpayer fails to file Form FTB 3840 for a year in which the gain remains deferred, and doesn't file an income tax return that year either, the FTB can issue a Notice of Proposed Assessment, effectively estimating the deferred income and assessing California tax on it directly, plus applicable penalties and interest. This can happen even though the investor hasn't actually sold or disposed of the replacement property, the failure to file the annual information return itself is what triggers the assessment risk. For an investor who exchanges out of California and later works with a different accountant, or simply forgets this is an annual requirement rather than a one-time filing, this is a real, avoidable financial exposure.

A Common Mistake Worth Flagging: "Boot"

A 1031 exchange only defers gain to the extent all the proceeds from the sale get reinvested into the replacement property, and any new debt on the replacement property is equal to or greater than the debt that was paid off on the relinquished property. If an investor pulls cash out of the transaction, or trades down into a less expensive or less leveraged replacement property, the difference (commonly called "boot") is immediately taxable in the year of the exchange, even though the rest of the exchange still qualifies. This is a frequent source of a smaller-than-expected deferral, not because the exchange failed, but because it wasn't structured to defer 100% of the gain in the first place.

For an Investor Who Doesn't Want to Actively Manage Another Property

A full, direct 1031 exchange requires taking on a new specific property, which isn't appealing to every seller, especially someone who has been actively managing a Woodland Hills rental and is looking to step back from landlord responsibilities rather than take on new ones. A Delaware Statutory Trust (DST) is one commonly used alternative: it allows an investor to exchange into a fractional, passive ownership interest in institutional-grade real estate (such as an apartment complex or commercial property) managed by a professional sponsor, while still qualifying as like-kind property under Section 1031. DSTs carry their own tradeoffs, including limited control over the underlying asset and sponsor-specific risk, and are worth evaluating with both a real estate professional and a securities-licensed advisor, since DST interests are typically offered as securities, not just real estate.

Why This Matters Specifically for Woodland Hills

Anchored to Woodland Hills' current market (Movoto, updated September 18, 2026): 389 active listings, 38 new, 81 price-reduced, $1,348,500 median sale price (August 2026), 48-day average DOM. An investment property here sitting on substantial appreciation is exactly the scenario a 1031 exchange is built for, but an owner planning to exchange into property outside California, whether for cash flow, diversification, or simply lower purchase prices elsewhere, needs to go in knowing about the annual FTB 3840 obligation before closing, not discover it during next year's tax season.

Bottom Line

A 1031 exchange is a legitimate, valuable tool for deferring tax on a Woodland Hills investment property sale, but it isn't a one-time paperwork event. The 45-day and 180-day federal clocks demand having a plan before the relinquished property even closes, and exchanging out of state adds a standing annual filing requirement with real financial consequences for skipping it.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does a 1031 exchange work for the home I actually live in?

No. A 1031 exchange applies only to property held for investment or business use. A primary residence sale is covered by the separate capital gains exclusion under IRC Section 121, a different mechanism that permanently excludes gain rather than deferring it.

How much time do I actually have to complete a 1031 exchange?

Two deadlines running at the same time from the day the sale closes: 45 calendar days to identify replacement property in writing, and 180 days total to close on it (or the tax return due date if earlier). Neither deadline extends for weekends or holidays.

What happens if I miss the 45-day identification deadline?

The exchange is generally disqualified entirely, meaning the full capital gains tax becomes due on the original sale as if no exchange had been attempted.

What is Form FTB 3840, and who has to file it?

It's California's required annual information return for any taxpayer who exchanges California real property for like-kind property located outside California. It must be filed for the year of the exchange and every subsequent year the gain remains deferred.

What happens if I forget to file Form FTB 3840 in a later year?

The Franchise Tax Board can issue a Notice of Proposed Assessment estimating the deferred income and assessing California tax, penalties, and interest, even though the replacement property hasn't been sold.

Does this affect a Woodland Hills investor exchanging into another California property?

The annual FTB 3840 filing requirement specifically applies to exchanges into property located outside California. An exchange entirely within California doesn't trigger this particular ongoing filing obligation, though the standard federal 1031 deadlines still apply.
