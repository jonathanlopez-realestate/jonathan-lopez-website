Can I Bring My Property Tax Base When Relocating To Woodland Hills?

Jonathan Lopez, Woodland Hills Realtor | Jim Sandoval Group | CA DRE #02282844 · Updated September 2026

Woodland Hills draws a steady stream of relocating buyers: retirees and near-retirees moving to be near adult children and grandchildren, empty-nesters downsizing out of bigger, more expensive coastal markets, and professionals relocating for a new chapter in the San Fernando Valley. A question comes up often enough from this group that it's worth answering directly, with the actual rule rather than the version that gets repeated informally: can I bring the lower property tax base from my old home with me? For anyone moving from outside California, the answer is no.

What Proposition 19 Actually Allows

California voters passed Proposition 19 in November 2020, and it took effect April 1, 2021. Among its provisions, Prop 19 lets certain homeowners transfer the assessed (taxable) value of their existing primary residence to a new one, rather than having the new home reassessed at full market value the way property purchases normally work under Proposition 13. The eligible groups are homeowners 55 or older, severely disabled homeowners, and victims of a wildfire or other qualifying natural disaster. This transfer can happen up to three times in a homeowner's lifetime (disaster victims aren't subject to that limit), and the replacement home can be anywhere in California, not just the same county, a significant expansion from the more limited version of this benefit that existed before Prop 19.

The Part That Trips People Up: Both Homes Have To Be In California

The California State Board of Equalization, the state agency that administers Prop 19, states plainly that both the original property and the replacement property must be located in California and must qualify for the homeowners' exemption or disabled veterans' exemption. There is no provision, and no equivalent workaround, for a homeowner selling a house in another state and buying in California. This isn't a quirk that a particular county interprets narrowly, it's the structure of the law itself: Prop 19 transfers an existing California assessed value forward, and a home in Arizona, Texas, Nevada, or anywhere else doesn't have a California assessed value to transfer in the first place, since those states don't use California's Proposition 13 system at all.

Why The Confusion Is Understandable

Prop 19's "move anywhere in California and keep your tax base" message has been widely marketed and widely understood among California homeowners, particularly retirees, since it took effect. It's genuinely one of the more valuable tax benefits in the state's property tax system. But that same message can create a reasonable, if incorrect, assumption for someone relocating from a different state: that some version of the same portability might apply to them too. It doesn't. A homeowner moving from Scottsdale, Nashville, or anywhere else outside California into Woodland Hills is, from a property tax standpoint, treated exactly like any other new buyer: the home gets reassessed at its full purchase price the moment escrow closes. This is a different mechanism than the one covered in why an existing owner's property tax bill can look so different from market value, which is about a longtime California owner's assessed value staying capped under Prop 13, not a new buyer's starting point.

What That Actually Costs, In Real Numbers

Woodland Hills' current median sale price is $1,348,500 (Movoto, updated September 9, 2026). Los Angeles County's property tax rate starts with the state's constitutional 1% base rate, but the effective rate homeowners actually pay typically lands between roughly 1.2% and 1.35% once voter-approved local bonds and assessments are layered in, depending on the exact address and school district. Using an illustrative 1.25% effective rate (not a quote for any specific property; actual rates vary by parcel) against Woodland Hills' current median, a newly reassessed home would carry an estimated first-year property tax bill of roughly $16,850. A homeowner who was able to transfer a lower assessed value under Prop 19 could see a meaningfully smaller bill, sometimes dramatically smaller for a longtime California homeowner whose old assessed value had stayed low for decades under Prop 13's annual growth cap. That gap is exactly what out-of-state relocating buyers don't have access to.

What Relocating Buyers Can Do Instead

There isn't a substitute benefit for portability from another state, but there are a few things worth knowing. First, the property tax on a newly purchased home is based on the purchase price, not an inflated assumption, so getting an accurate purchase price, and not overpaying relative to comparable sales, directly controls the tax bill going forward. Second, California's Proposition 13 still caps annual assessed value growth at 2% per year once a home is purchased, so while a relocating buyer starts at full market value, that value is protected from the kind of unpredictable annual jumps some other states allow. Third, homeowners who did sell a home within California first, not out of state, before buying in Woodland Hills should specifically ask whether they qualify for Prop 19 portability; that benefit is real and valuable, it simply doesn't extend across state lines. This is the kind of planning worth pairing with the practical logistics covered in what closing on a Woodland Hills home actually looks like when you're relocating from out of state.

The bottom line: if you're relocating to Woodland Hills from another state, budget for property tax based on your actual purchase price, not on whatever you were paying on your prior home elsewhere. If you're moving within California, ask about Prop 19 before you assume you're starting from zero.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can I transfer my out-of-state property's tax assessment to a home I buy in Woodland Hills?

No. California's State Board of Equalization requires both the original home and the replacement home to be located in California for a Proposition 19 base year value transfer. A home in another state doesn't have a California assessed value to transfer, since other states don't use California's Proposition 13 system.

Who actually qualifies for Prop 19's property tax base transfer?

Homeowners 55 or older, severely disabled homeowners, and victims of a wildfire or other qualifying natural disaster, and only when both the original and replacement homes are in California.

How many times can someone use the Prop 19 transfer?

Up to three times in a lifetime for the 55-or-older and severely disabled categories, since the transfer took effect April 1, 2021. Disaster victims are not subject to that three-time limit.

What will my property tax actually be if I relocate to Woodland Hills from out of state?

It will be based on your home's full purchase price, reassessed at close of escrow, at Los Angeles County's effective property tax rate of roughly 1.2% to 1.35% depending on the specific address and local bonds. At Woodland Hills' current median sale price of $1,348,500, that works out to an estimated $16,850 or so in the first year, illustrative math, not a quote for a specific property.

Does my new Woodland Hills home's assessed value still grow every year after I buy it?

Yes, but it's capped. Once your home is reassessed at purchase price, California's Proposition 13 limits annual assessed value growth to 2% per year going forward, regardless of how much market values rise.

I sold my previous home in California before moving to Woodland Hills. Does Prop 19 apply to me?

Possibly, yes. If both your original home and your new Woodland Hills home are in California and you meet the age, disability, or disaster-victim requirements, you may be able to transfer your prior assessed value. That's a real, valuable benefit, worth asking about directly with the county assessor, and it's a different situation from relocating from out of state.
