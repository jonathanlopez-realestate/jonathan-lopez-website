My Relocation Package Includes a Lump Sum. Will It Actually Cover Closing Costs on a Woodland Hills Home?

Jonathan Lopez, Woodland Hills Realtor · Jim Sandoval Group · CA DRE #02282844 · Updated November 2026

Relocating to Woodland Hills for a new job usually comes with some kind of financial help from the new employer: a lump-sum check, a capped reimbursement, or occasionally a full-service managed relocation program. Whatever the structure, it's worth stress-testing against this specific market's actual numbers before building a home-search budget around it, because the figure a corporate HR department quotes is often built from a national formula, not a Woodland Hills one.

Start with what closing actually costs here. Buyer-side closing costs in California are commonly cited, across CAR materials, major lenders, and title companies, at roughly 2% to 4% of the purchase price. That figure covers loan origination and underwriting fees, appraisal, title insurance, escrow fees, recording fees, and prepaid items like the first year of homeowners insurance and a few months of property tax impounds. It does not include the down payment itself, which is a separate number entirely. Applied to Woodland Hills' current median sale price of $1,300,000 (Movoto), that range works out to roughly $26,000 on the low end and $52,000 on the high end, a real swing depending on loan type and lender, but a floor worth planning around either way.

Here's where relocation packages can fall short. Corporate relocation policies are frequently written once, at a national or even global level, and applied uniformly across every office and every market an employer has. A policy that caps closing-cost assistance at a flat dollar figure, or hands out a flat lump sum meant to cover "moving costs" broadly, wasn't necessarily built with a $1.3 million purchase price in mind. An employee relocating from a market with a $350,000 median home price to one with a $1.3 million median is facing a fundamentally different closing-cost bill, in raw dollars, even though the percentage math is identical. If the HR department quoting the package never adjusted for that gap, the employee is the one who finds out at the closing table, not before.

The tax change makes the gap wider, not just the same. As covered in a separate post on this site, the One Big Beautiful Bill Act permanently ended the old tax-free treatment of employer-paid relocation expenses starting with the 2026 tax year. A lump sum that already wasn't sized for Woodland Hills now also loses a meaningful chunk to ordinary income tax and payroll withholding before it ever reaches a buyer's closing costs. The two issues compound: a package that might have been borderline-adequate pre-tax can become clearly short once the after-tax number is the real figure in play.

Two concrete steps before relying on the number in an offer letter. First, find out exactly how the relocation package is structured: a true lump sum (a flat check, no receipts required, fully taxable, spend it however needed) behaves very differently from a capped reimbursement program (submit actual invoices up to a category-by-category limit, sometimes grossed up for taxes, sometimes not). Knowing which one applies changes how to plan. Second, get a Loan Estimate from a lender early, before house-hunting seriously begins, so the real, lender-specific closing cost figure for an actual target price range is known in writing rather than estimated from a rule of thumb. Federal TRID rules require lenders to issue a Loan Estimate within three business days of a completed application and a final Closing Disclosure at least three business days before closing, so this number is available well before escrow closes, not after.

Why this matters specifically in Woodland Hills. The area sits within commuting distance of sizable employers across healthcare, finance, and entertainment-adjacent industries in the San Fernando Valley, which means a steady stream of relocating buyers arriving with a package quoted by an HR department that may never have priced a Woodland Hills purchase before. With 368 active listings and a 54-day median time on market (Movoto, November 5, 2026), buyers here have real inventory and real time to plan, which makes an early, accurate budget more useful than a late, painful surprise mid-escrow.

The bottom line: a relocation lump sum is a real benefit, but "generous-sounding on the offer letter" and "enough to cover Woodland Hills closing costs" are two different questions. Running the actual local math, and getting a real Loan Estimate, before committing to a purchase price is what closes that gap.

This post is general information, not legal, tax, or lending advice. The 2% to 4% closing cost range is a rule of thumb, not a quote; confirm actual costs with a Loan Estimate from a licensed lender.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does a relocation lump sum have to be spent on the move itself?

Typically no. A true lump sum is usually paid as a flat amount with no requirement to submit receipts or prove it was spent on moving-related costs, which is part of why it's fully taxable as ordinary income rather than a reimbursement of documented expenses.

Is a capped reimbursement program always better than a lump sum?

Not automatically. A reimbursement program may cover more of the actual cost if the caps are generous and the employee's spending stays within each category, but it also requires paperwork and sometimes a wait for reimbursement after the expense is incurred. A lump sum is less paperwork and immediate cash, but caps out at a flat number regardless of actual local costs.

Can I negotiate a bigger relocation package if I'm moving somewhere expensive?

It's a reasonable thing to ask, especially for a market like Woodland Hills where the local closing-cost math is meaningfully higher than a national average. Whether an employer will adjust the offer varies by company and by how much leverage exists in the specific hire, but pointing to the actual local numbers, not just a general request for "more," tends to be the stronger ask.

Do closing costs include the down payment?

No. Closing costs and the down payment are separate line items. The 2% to 4% range discussed here covers fees and prepaid items, not the equity contribution toward the purchase price itself, which is typically the much larger number in any home purchase.

What's the fastest way to know my real closing cost number?

Get pre-approved with a specific lender and ask for a Loan Estimate once there's a realistic target price range in mind. Federal rules require it within three business days of a completed application, and it will break out the actual fees for that lender, loan type, and price point rather than a generic percentage range.

If my relocation package falls short, are there other ways to close the gap?

Some loan programs allow seller-paid closing cost credits as part of contract negotiation, and some lenders offer temporary or permanent rate buydowns that shift costs around rather than eliminate them. These are deal-specific and worth discussing with an agent and a lender once an actual offer is being written, not something to assume in advance.
