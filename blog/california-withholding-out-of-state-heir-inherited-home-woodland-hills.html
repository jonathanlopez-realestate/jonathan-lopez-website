I Inherited a Woodland Hills Home and Live Out of State. Why Is Escrow Withholding Part of My Sale Proceeds?

Jonathan Lopez, Woodland Hills Realtor · Jim Sandoval Group · CA DRE #02282844 · Updated November 2026

It's one of the most common setups in Woodland Hills probate sales: a parent passes away, the house goes to two or three adult children, and at least one of them has lived in another state for years. Everyone agrees to sell, the home closes in escrow, and then the out-of-state heir gets a surprise at the closing statement: a chunk of their share of the proceeds, often tens of thousands of dollars, never shows up in their bank account. It went to the California Franchise Tax Board instead. This isn't an error, and it isn't optional. It's California's real estate withholding law, and it catches out-of-state heirs more often than almost any other seller because they reasonably assume that living somewhere else exempts them from a California tax process. It doesn't, at least not automatically.

California Revenue and Taxation Code Section 18662 requires withholding on the sale of California real property by any seller, California resident or not. Escrow is the one that actually withholds and remits the money, using FTB Form 593, and the default rate if no exemption is filed is 3 1/3% (3.333%) of the entire sale price, not the profit, the whole price. On a sale at Woodland Hills' current median of $1,299,000 (Movoto, last confirmed Oct 30, 2026), that's $43,300 withheld automatically unless the seller does something about it before escrow closes.

That "unless" matters a lot for heirs specifically, because of how inherited property gets taxed in the first place. Under IRC Section 1014, an inherited home's tax basis isn't what the original owner paid decades ago, it steps up to the property's fair market value on the date of the owner's death. If the heirs sell reasonably close to that date, and reasonably close to that same value, their actual taxable gain on the sale can be small, or in some cases essentially zero. California's withholding rules account for this: instead of the flat 3.333% of the full sale price, a seller can elect to have escrow withhold 12.3% of the estimated gain instead (the top California personal income tax rate, applied only to profit, not total price), or, if the numbers truly show no gain or even a loss, certify that directly on Form 593 and avoid withholding on that basis entirely. For an heir selling a stepped-up-basis home at or near its date-of-death value, this alternative calculation is frequently the difference between a five-figure holdback and close to nothing.

There's a separate, narrower exemption worth knowing about too: the IRC Section 121 principal-residence exclusion, which requires having owned and lived in the home as a main residence for at least two of the five years before the sale. Most out-of-state heirs won't qualify for this one, since by definition they haven't been living in the Woodland Hills home, but it's relevant for the less common case where an heir actually relocates into the inherited property before eventually selling it.

The paperwork timing is the part that trips people up most. Form 593, with whichever exemption or alternative calculation applies, has to be completed, signed by the seller, and submitted to escrow before the close of the transaction. It isn't something to sort out afterward or appeal once the funds are already gone. If it's not filed in time, the default 3.333%-of-sale-price withholding simply happens, and the seller is left to recover any overwithheld amount later rather than avoiding it at the source.

That recovery path does exist, and it's worth being clear about: withholding is not an additional tax on top of whatever is actually owed. It's a prepayment toward it. A nonresident seller reconciles the withheld amount on a California Form 540NR nonresident return. If the withholding exceeded the real tax liability, which is common for heirs selling near the stepped-up basis value, the difference comes back as a refund. It just means the money is tied up for months rather than available at closing, which is exactly the kind of cash-flow surprise that catches out-of-state heirs off guard if nobody flags it before escrow opens.

One more wrinkle specific to multiple heirs selling together: withholding is calculated per seller, based on each heir's proportional share of the proceeds. A California-resident heir selling alongside out-of-state siblings can typically certify California residency and avoid this withholding category altogether on their share, while the nonresident heirs still need their own exemption certification or default withholding on theirs. It's not an all-or-nothing determination across the whole sale.

None of this changes what the home is actually worth or how it should be marketed and priced, but it absolutely changes what each heir should expect to see land in their account the day escrow closes, and planning for it ahead of time means nobody is caught off guard by a number that's smaller than they expected.

Tax rules change and every estate is different; confirm your withholding options with a CPA or tax attorney before escrow closes. This is general information, not tax or legal advice.

Frequently Asked Questions

I live out of state. Does that automatically mean California withholds extra tax from my share of an inherited home sale?

Not extra tax, but yes, withholding does apply by default. California's real estate withholding law covers every seller of California property regardless of residency. Without an exemption filed with escrow before closing, 3.333% of the full sale price gets withheld and sent to the Franchise Tax Board, reconciled later on your tax return.

What's the actual withholding rate, and is there a better option than the flat percentage?

The default is 3 1/3% (3.333%) of the total sale price. Sellers can instead elect to have 12.3% withheld on just the estimated gain, or certify no gain or loss at all if the numbers support it. For an heir selling near the property's stepped-up value, the gain-based option or the no-gain certification is often far smaller than 3.333% of the whole sale price.

How does the stepped-up basis actually reduce what gets withheld?

Inherited property's tax basis resets to its fair market value on the date of death, not what the original owner paid. If you sell close to that same value, your real taxable gain is small or zero, which supports electing the lower gain-based withholding rate or certifying no gain on Form 593, instead of defaulting to 3.333% of the entire sale price.

My sibling still lives in California and I don't. Do we both get withheld the same way?

No. Withholding is calculated per seller, based on each heir's share of the proceeds. A California-resident heir can typically certify residency and avoid this category of withholding on their portion, while an out-of-state or nonresident heir still needs their own exemption certification or faces default withholding on theirs.

If too much gets withheld, how do I get it back?

Withholding is a prepayment, not a final tax bill. A nonresident seller reconciles the withheld amount on a California Form 540NR return; if the withholding exceeded the actual tax owed, the difference comes back as a refund. It just takes time, which is why planning ahead of closing matters.

What if I actually moved into the inherited Woodland Hills home before selling it?

That can open up the separate IRC Section 121 principal-residence exclusion, which requires owning and living in the home as your main residence for at least two of the five years before the sale. Most out-of-state heirs selling shortly after inheriting won't meet this, but it's worth raising with a CPA if your situation is different.
