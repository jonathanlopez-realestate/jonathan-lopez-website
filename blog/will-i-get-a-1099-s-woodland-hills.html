Will I Get a 1099-S When I Sell My Woodland Hills Home, Even If I Owe No Capital Gains Tax?

Jonathan Lopez, Woodland Hills Realtor · Jim Sandoval Group · CA DRE #02282844 · Updated November 2026

Most Woodland Hills sellers who ask me about taxes are really asking one question: will I owe anything? That's the right question for figuring out your net proceeds, but it isn't the only paperwork question tied to a home sale, and it's not the one that catches people off guard months after closing. The one that does is a form called 1099-S, and it has nothing to do with whether you actually owe tax.

Form 1099-S, "Proceeds From Real Estate Transactions," is a federal information return. Under Internal Revenue Code Section 6045(e), the person who closes your transaction, almost always the escrow or title company in a California sale, has to report the gross sale price to the IRS and send you a copy, the same way an employer sends a W-2 or a bank sends a 1099-INT. The IRS gets a copy whether or not you make a dollar of taxable profit. The form exists to let the IRS match what it was told against what you report on your own return, not to tell you what you owe.

There is a real exemption, and it's worth understanding because it's where the confusion starts. Treasury Regulation 1.6045-4 and the IRS's own instructions for Form 1099-S let a closing agent skip filing the form if the seller signs a written certification stating, under penalty of perjury, that the property was their principal residence, they meet the ownership and use tests for the Section 121 exclusion, they haven't excluded gain from another home sale in the prior two years, and the entire gain is excludable. Critically, that certification option is only available in the first place if the sale price itself is $250,000 or less for a single seller, or $500,000 or less for a married couple who both sign. Above those sale-price thresholds, a 1099-S gets filed regardless of how much of the gain, if any, actually ends up being taxable.

That price threshold is the whole story for Woodland Hills. Movoto's Woodland Hills page, last confirmed updated October 30, 2026, puts the median sale price at $1,299,000. A live refetch of Movoto's page while writing this didn't return data this session, so that October figure is the most recent confirmed number on file rather than a same-day pull, worth a quick re-check with your own closing agent before you rely on exact figures for your sale. Either way, at a seven-figure median, the overwhelming majority of homes here sell for well above both the $250,000 and $500,000 certification thresholds. To put that in real numbers: a married couple selling at that $1,299,000 median is roughly $799,000 over the $500,000 certification ceiling (illustrative math based on that one data point, not a universal rule), so the 1099-S gets filed automatically regardless of the actual gain calculation. A seller who clears the full $500,000 married exclusion and genuinely owes nothing in federal tax will still get a 1099-S in the mail, simply because the sale price itself was too high to qualify for the paperwork exemption. Owing no tax and receiving no 1099-S are two different things, and in this market, most sellers get the form even when they owe nothing.

It's also worth separating this from a different form some Woodland Hills sellers run into: California's own real estate withholding under Revenue and Taxation Code Section 18662, filed on FTB Form 593, which came up recently in a separate post about an out-of-state heir selling an inherited Woodland Hills home. Form 593 involves actual money, a percentage of the sale price or gain genuinely held back from your proceeds at closing and sent to the Franchise Tax Board. Form 1099-S is purely informational; no money is withheld because of it, it's just a record sent to the IRS. A single transaction can generate both forms, one about cash actually held back, the other about data reported, and confusing the two is an easy mistake when you're juggling paperwork during escrow.

Here's where it actually matters. Per the IRS's own Publication 523 guidance, once you receive a 1099-S, you're expected to report the sale on your tax return, specifically on Form 8949, and use code H in column (f) to flag that the gain is excluded under Section 121. If you qualify for the full exclusion and never receive a 1099-S, you can generally leave the sale off your return entirely, nothing to reconcile. But if the form was issued, the IRS already has a record of your gross sale proceeds sitting in its system. If your return doesn't show a matching entry, the agency's automated matching process can generate a CP2000 notice, a letter proposing additional tax based on unreported income, even when the real answer is that you owed nothing. Responding to one of those notices after the fact, explaining a transaction that happened a year or more earlier, is a far more annoying process than just reporting it correctly the first time.

A few practical takeaways follow from this. First, expect a 1099-S on almost any Woodland Hills sale; don't be alarmed by it, and don't assume it means you owe money. Second, keep your closing (settlement) statement and any gain-exclusion worksheet your CPA prepares, since you'll want the Form 8949 entry to tie cleanly back to the actual numbers from escrow. Third, if you're selling something smaller than a typical Woodland Hills home, a bare lot, an ADU-only parcel sale, or a property genuinely priced near or under the $250,000/$500,000 thresholds, ask your escrow officer about the certification option early, since it has to be signed before closing, not added afterward. And fourth, this is a reporting and recordkeeping issue, not a strategy for reducing what you owe; nothing here changes your actual tax liability under Section 121, it only changes whether paperwork follows the sale.

None of this is personalized tax advice, and the exact filing mechanics depend on your specific situation, your CPA is the right person to confirm it for your actual return. What I can do on the real estate side is make sure your closing statement and sale price are accurate and documented from day one, so whatever your CPA needs to reconcile against the 1099-S is sitting in one clean file instead of scattered across emails months later.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is Form 1099-S?

A federal information return that your escrow or title company files with the IRS under Internal Revenue Code Section 6045(e), reporting the gross sale price of your home. You get a copy too. It reports the sale happened, it doesn't calculate your tax.

Does getting a 1099-S mean I owe capital gains tax?

No. The form is triggered by the sale price, not by whether you have a taxable gain. A seller who owes zero tax under the Section 121 exclusion can still receive a 1099-S if the sale price was too high to qualify for the paperwork exemption.

Can I avoid getting a 1099-S on my Woodland Hills sale?

Only if the sale price is $250,000 or less for a single seller or $500,000 or less for a married couple who both sign a written certification, and the entire gain is excludable under Section 121. At Woodland Hills' roughly $1,299,000 median (Movoto, updated October 2026), almost no typical home sale here qualifies for that exemption.

What do I do if I receive a 1099-S but owe no tax?

Per IRS Publication 523, you still report the sale on Form 8949 and use code H to show the gain is excluded under Section 121. Skipping this because "I don't owe anything" can trigger an IRS CP2000 mismatch notice later, since the IRS already has the sale on file from the 1099-S.

Is a 1099-S the same thing as California's real estate withholding?

No. California's withholding (Revenue and Taxation Code Section 18662, FTB Form 593) actually holds back a percentage of your proceeds at closing. Form 1099-S withholds nothing; it's purely informational. You can receive both forms on the same sale for two unrelated reasons.

Who actually sends me the 1099-S, and when?

Your escrow or title company, as the closing agent, typically issues it shortly after closing, with a copy also going to the IRS. Ask your escrow officer directly if you're unsure whether one will be filed on your transaction.
