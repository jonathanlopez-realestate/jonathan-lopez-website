I'm Relocating to Woodland Hills. Does California Start Taxing My Income the Day I Move, or Is There a Grace Period?

Jonathan Lopez, Woodland Hills Realtor · Jim Sandoval Group · CA DRE #02282844 · Updated November 2026

A lot of the buyers I work with in Woodland Hills are relocating from states that do not charge income tax at all, Texas, Nevada, Florida, Washington. Many of them keep their current job and work remotely for their out-of-state employer after the move. The assumption I hear most often: "my paycheck still comes from a Texas company, so California cannot tax it." That assumption is wrong, and it is worth understanding before you close on a home here, not after your first California tax season.

Here is the part that surprises people who have only dealt with states that use a bright-line day count, places like New York or Connecticut with their well-known 183-day rules. California does not work that way. Its residency test asks where your closest connections actually are, not how many nights you spent in the state. Factors the Franchise Tax Board actually weighs include where your family lives and where your kids go to school, the size and real use of any home you still own outside California, where you work and conduct business day to day, where your doctors, attorneys, and other professional relationships are, and where your driver's license, voter registration, and vehicle registration sit. No single factor controls on its own. A Nevada driver's license does not mean much if your spouse, your house, and your cardiologist are all now in Woodland Hills.

California does have one statutory bright line, but it runs in the direction of a presumption, not a safe harbor. Revenue and Taxation Code Section 17016 creates a rebuttable presumption of residency if you are physically present in California for more than nine months of the tax year. The key word is presumption, not threshold. If your real life, home, job, and family have already relocated to Woodland Hills, the FTB can determine you are a resident well before you hit nine months, potentially from the date you actually move, because the underlying test is about where you live with intent to stay, not a countdown clock. There is no symmetrical rule that protects someone who spends fewer than nine months in the state; spending less time here simply means one fewer piece of evidence pointing toward residency, not an automatic pass.

Once residency actually starts, the tax consequence is broad. A California resident is taxed on worldwide income, every dollar, regardless of where the money was earned or where the paying employer is headquartered. If you move to Woodland Hills on, say, June 1 and keep working remotely for your Texas-based employer through the rest of the year, the income you earn from June 1 forward is taxable by California even though your employer never set foot in the state. People who move partway through the year file as part-year residents on Form 540NR, which taxes all income earned during the California-resident portion of the year plus any California-source income earned during the nonresident portion.

The rate itself is not small. California's top marginal income tax bracket is 13.3%, made up of a 12.3% top rate plus an additional 1% Mental Health Services Tax (created by Proposition 63 in 2004) on taxable income above $1 million. For a household moving from a zero-income-tax state, that is a real, permanent shift in take-home pay, not a one-time closing cost. There is also a safe harbor that works in the opposite direction, useful mainly as a contrast: someone domiciled in California who leaves under an employment contract for at least 546 consecutive days is generally treated as a nonresident while away. There is no equivalent waiting-period exemption for someone moving into California; residency, and the tax obligation that comes with it, can begin the day your real connections land here.

None of this changes the real estate side of a relocation purchase, Woodland Hills' current market sits at a $1,300,000 median sale price with 368 active listings and homes averaging 54 days on market (Movoto). But it is exactly the kind of fact that belongs in the budgeting conversation before you write an offer, not after your first paycheck shows a very different net number than you expected.

This is general tax information, not advice for your specific situation, residency determinations depend on your full set of facts. Frequently Asked Questions

If I keep working remotely for my out-of-state employer, does California still tax that income after I move here?

Yes. California taxes a resident's full worldwide income regardless of where the employer is based, once residency is established the location of your paycheck's source company does not shield that income.

Does California use a 183-day rule like New York or Connecticut?

No. California's test looks at where your closest connections actually are (family, home, job, professional relationships, driver's license and registrations), not a simple day count. A day-count threshold exists only as a one-directional presumption, not a safe harbor.

What is the nine-month presumption?

Revenue and Taxation Code Section 17016 creates a rebuttable presumption that you are a California resident if you spend more than nine months of the tax year physically present in the state. It is a presumption that can be triggered, not a grace period you get to use before taxes apply.

Is there a grace period before California starts taxing someone who just relocated?

No automatic one. If your real-life connections (home, family, job) have already moved to Woodland Hills with intent to stay, the FTB can treat you as a resident from the date you actually relocated, not after some waiting period.

What tax rate am I looking at as a new California resident?

California's top marginal income tax bracket is 13.3%: a 12.3% top rate plus a 1% Mental Health Services Tax on taxable income above $1 million. Lower income levels fall into lower brackets.

How do I file for the year I actually move?

As a part-year resident, using Form 540NR, which taxes all income earned during the portion of the year you lived in California plus any California-source income earned during the nonresident portion.
