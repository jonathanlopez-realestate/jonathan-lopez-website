I'm Relocating to Woodland Hills From a State With No Earthquakes. Do I Actually Need Earthquake Insurance?

Jonathan Lopez, Woodland Hills Realtor · Jim Sandoval Group · CA DRE #02282844 · Updated November 2026

If you're relocating to Woodland Hills from a state where earthquakes just aren't a thing, there's a detail about California homeownership that catches a lot of newcomers off guard: your standard homeowners insurance policy does not cover earthquake damage, period. It's not a sub-limit or a higher deductible buried in the fine print, it's a flat exclusion. If you want that protection, you have to buy it as its own separate policy.

The dominant provider is the California Earthquake Authority, a publicly chartered but privately funded entity that sells its policies through participating residential insurance companies rather than directly. As of 2026, CEA wrote roughly 67% of all residential earthquake insurance policies in the state, making it the default answer for most homeowners who decide to get covered.

Here's the part that surprises people the most: most Californians don't get covered at all. Only about 14% to 16% of homeowners statewide currently carry earthquake insurance. That's not a new trend. Take-up briefly spiked above 30% right after the magnitude 6.7 Northridge earthquake hit the San Fernando Valley on January 17, 1994, causing an estimated $40 billion in damage according to USGS, but it settled back down into a 10% to 15% range for most of the two decades that followed and sits in that same neighborhood today. The main reason people cite isn't doubt about the risk, it's the cost and the deductible structure once you actually price it out.

And the deductible structure is the thing relocating buyers most need to understand before they assume this works like a typical insurance deductible. CEA deductibles aren't a flat dollar amount, they're a percentage of your coverage limit, and you choose from 5%, 10%, 15%, 20%, or 25%. But if your home is valued over $1 million, or it was built before 1980 on a raised foundation without a verified seismic retrofit, you don't get the lower options; you're limited to 15%, 20%, or 25%.

That restriction matters a lot in Woodland Hills specifically, where the current median sale price is $1,300,000 (Movoto), putting most local homes squarely in that over-$1-million tier. Illustratively, if you insured a home at that value and chose the lowest deductible available to you, 15%, you'd be looking at $195,000 of damage you'd have to cover yourself before the policy pays a dollar. At the 25% tier, that's $325,000. These are illustrative numbers based on the city's median, not a quote for any specific property, but they explain why plenty of homeowners look at the math and decide to self-insure instead.

It's not all deductible, though. A few pieces of CEA coverage apply with no deductible at all: Loss of Use, which covers your temporary living expenses if the home becomes unlivable, and the first $1,500 of Emergency Repairs needed right after a quake. CEA policies also include a $10,000 Building Code Upgrade benefit to help bring a damaged home up to current code during repairs, with higher limits available.

None of this makes the decision for you, and no lender requires earthquake insurance the way they require fire coverage, but it's a decision worth making with real numbers in front of you rather than assuming either "it's automatically covered" or "it's not worth it" without pricing your actual options.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does my regular California homeowners insurance cover earthquake damage?

No. Standard homeowners policies in California exclude earthquake damage entirely; coverage only exists if you buy a separate earthquake policy, most commonly through the California Earthquake Authority (CEA).

Is earthquake insurance required to get a mortgage in California?

No. Unlike fire insurance, no lender requires earthquake coverage as a condition of the loan. It's an optional policy you choose to add or skip.

How does the CEA deductible actually work?

It's a percentage of your coverage limit, not a flat dollar amount, with options of 5%, 10%, 15%, 20%, or 25%. Homes valued over $1 million, or pre-1980 homes on a raised foundation without a verified retrofit, are limited to the 15%, 20%, or 25% tiers only.

What would a deductible actually cost on a Woodland Hills-priced home?

Illustratively, on a $1,300,000 coverage limit (Woodland Hills' current median sale price), a 15% deductible works out to $195,000 and a 25% deductible to $325,000, both out of pocket before the policy pays anything. This is illustrative math based on the city median, not a quote.

How many California homeowners actually have earthquake insurance?

Only about 14% to 16% statewide as of 2026, a rate that's held in the 10% to 15% range for most of the past two decades, well down from the over-30% take-up seen right after the 1994 Northridge earthquake.

Is any part of a CEA policy exempt from the deductible?

Yes. Loss of Use (temporary living expenses) carries no deductible, the first $1,500 of Emergency Repairs carries no deductible, and a $10,000 Building Code Upgrade benefit is included to help bring repairs up to current code.
