I Inherited a Woodland Hills Home With My Siblings. Do We All Have to Sign Before It Sells?

By the time a Woodland Hills family calls about selling an inherited home, the sale itself is usually already decided. Every heir wants to list it, split the proceeds, and move on. What catches people off guard is a more mechanical question nobody thought to ask earlier: whose actual signature has to appear on the listing agreement, and later the deed, before any of that can happen.

Once title passes to the heirs, they hold it as tenants in common. When a probate estate closes or a trust distributes a home to more than one beneficiary, California law does not create a single combined owner. Each heir holds an individual, undivided share of the whole property as a tenant in common with the others, the default form of co-ownership for inherited real estate. No heir's share is physically tied to a specific room or portion of the lot. Everyone owns a percentage of the entire home.

That ownership structure is exactly why every heir has to sign. A real estate listing agreement is a contract, and under California Civil Code Section 1624(a)(4), an agreement authorizing a broker or agent to sell real property must be in writing and signed by the party to be charged, the statute of frauds rule that applies to essentially every real estate contract in the state. Because each heir independently owns a share of the whole property, each heir is a separate "party to be charged." One sibling signing the listing agreement only binds that sibling's own share, not the others'. The same logic carries through to the grant deed at closing: a deed conveying the whole property needs every titled owner's signature, or the buyer and their lender will not get clear title to what they are paying for.

A single heir cannot unilaterally commit the others to a sale. This surprises people who assume a sibling with extra motivation, maybe they live locally and the others are out of state, can just handle things and sign on everyone's behalf. They cannot, not without separate legal authority. What a heir can do without anyone else's consent is sell or transfer their own individual, undivided share, though in practice almost no buyer wants to purchase a fractional interest in a house they would then co-own with strangers, so this rarely resolves a practical sale.

A written, recorded power of attorney is the real workaround. If one heir genuinely cannot be present, say they are overseas, deployed, or simply want someone else handling the paperwork, California Civil Code Section 2309 allows them to grant another person written authority to sign on their behalf. Because the underlying listing agreement and deed must be in writing, the power of attorney authorizing someone to sign those documents must also be in writing. It cannot be granted verbally or by a casual text message agreeing to let a sibling "handle it." In practice, escrow and title companies go a step further than the statute requires: they typically want the power of attorney document itself recorded with the county recorder where the property sits before they will allow the sale to close, not simply produced at the signing table. Building in time to get a power of attorney drafted, signed, notarized, and recorded, often weeks before closing, avoids a last-minute scramble.

If probate is still open, the signing authority sits somewhere else entirely. All of the above assumes the home has already been distributed to the heirs directly. While an estate is still working through probate, the heirs are not yet the titled owners and do not sign the listing agreement at all. Instead, the court-appointed executor or administrator signs under the authority granted by their Letters of Administration or Letters Testamentary, the same authority covered in more depth in our separate post on selling a Woodland Hills home before probate fully closes. Individual heir signatures only become relevant once the estate actually distributes the property and each heir is recorded as a titled co-owner.

What happens if one heir simply won't sign. Everything above assumes every heir is willing to sell, just slow, distant, or disorganized about the paperwork. If an heir actively refuses to sell at all, that is a different situation with a different remedy, a court-ordered partition action under Code of Civil Procedure Section 872.210, which we cover in detail in our separate post on what happens when a sibling won't agree to sell an inherited Woodland Hills home. The short version: a signature problem caused by logistics gets solved with a power of attorney or patience; a refusal to sell at all gets solved, if it has to be, through a partition case in court.

Why this matters on a real Woodland Hills timeline. Zip code 91367's current median sale price sits around $1,199,000 (Movoto, 2026-11-24), with roughly 212 homes actively listed and about 41 days on market on average. A missing signature doesn't just delay paperwork on a file that size, it can push a closing date past a buyer's rate lock, cost a lender re-underwriting fees, or cause a buyer to walk entirely if the delay runs long enough. Confirming at the very start of the process who holds title, how many signatures are actually needed, and whether anyone will need a power of attorney, belongs on the first conversation with your agent, not a surprise escrow discovers two weeks before closing.

Woodland Hills families going through this. The signature question is rarely the hard part emotionally, it is usually the part everyone assumes is already handled. Confirming it early, before a home ever goes on the market, is one of the simplest ways to keep a multi-heir sale moving on schedule.

Frequently Asked Questions

How is title actually held after multiple heirs inherit a Woodland Hills home?

As tenants in common by default, unless the estate planning documents specify otherwise. Each heir owns an individual, undivided percentage share of the entire property, not a separable piece of it.

Can one heir sign the listing agreement for everyone else?

No, not without written legal authority from the others. Under Civil Code Section 1624(a)(4), the listing agreement must be signed by each party it binds. One heir's signature only commits that heir's own ownership share.

Can a heir sell just their own share without the others agreeing?

Yes, an heir can transfer their individual undivided interest without the other co-owners' consent, but almost no buyer wants to purchase a fractional interest in a home they would then co-own with people they don't know, so this rarely results in an actual closed sale.

What if a sibling lives out of state or can't be at the signing?

A written power of attorney under Civil Code Section 2309 lets someone else sign on their behalf. Escrow and title companies typically also want that power of attorney document recorded with the county before they will close, so start this process early, not at the last minute.

Does an executor sign instead of the heirs if the estate is still in probate?

Yes. While probate is open, the court-appointed executor or administrator signs the listing agreement under their Letters, not the individual heirs. Heirs only sign directly once the estate distributes title to them.

What if one heir refuses to sell at all, not just refuses to sign paperwork?

That is a different problem with a different remedy: a court-ordered partition action under Code of Civil Procedure Section 872.210. See our separate post on what happens when a sibling won't agree to sell an inherited Woodland Hills home for how that process works.
