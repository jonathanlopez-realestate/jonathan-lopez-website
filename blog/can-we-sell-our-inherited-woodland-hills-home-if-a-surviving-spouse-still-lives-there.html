My Father Left Our Woodland Hills Home to Me and My Siblings, But His Second Wife Still Lives There. Can We Sell It?

Jonathan Lopez, Woodland Hills Realtor · Jim Sandoval Group · CA DRE #02282844 · Updated November 2026

Inheriting a Woodland Hills house is rarely as clean as a deed changing hands. One of the most common real-world complications involves a blended family: a parent remarries, dies, and the will leaves the house to the children from the first marriage, but the second spouse is still living in it. The heirs often assume that because the will is clear, they can list the house right away. That is not always true, and the reason is a specific, limited protection built into California probate law.

What a probate homestead actually is. California Probate Code Chapter 3, Sections 6520 through 6528, lets a probate court set aside a residence, or an interest in one, for the use of the decedent's surviving spouse or minor children while the estate is being administered. This is called a "probate homestead." It is a temporary right to occupy the home, not a transfer of ownership, and it is narrow in who can even ask for it: only a surviving spouse or a minor child qualifies, not an adult stepchild, a girlfriend or boyfriend, a roommate, or any other occupant.

It is not automatic. Nothing about this protection kicks in on its own the moment someone dies. The surviving spouse (or someone on behalf of a minor child) has to file a petition in the probate case asking the court to set the homestead aside. The heirs who want to sell get notice and a chance to be heard. The court then decides, case by case, whether to grant it, what property to use, and for how long.

The court does not ignore the will. A real misconception here is that a probate homestead petition automatically overrides a will that leaves the house to someone else. It does not. The statute directs the court to weigh several things together: the needs of the surviving spouse and any minor children, existing liens and encumbrances on the property, claims from creditors of the estate, the interests of the heirs or beneficiaries who are actually entitled to the property, and the decedent's expressed plan for the estate, meaning what the will or trust actually says. A will that clearly leaves the house to the children is a real factor working against granting (or extending) a homestead over that specific property, it just is not an automatic bar by itself.

Which property gets used first. When selecting what property to set aside, the law gives first preference to community property, quasi-community property, or property the decedent owned jointly with the person getting the homestead. Only after that does it reach property the decedent owned separately. In a blended-family situation where the house was the decedent's separate property, owned before the second marriage and willed outright to the children, that preference order matters: it is not the first place the statute points the court, even though a judge can still consider it.

How long it lasts. If a court does grant a probate homestead, the order has to set a limited period. The statute is explicit that the homestead can never extend beyond the surviving spouse's own lifetime, or, for a minor child, beyond the point the child turns 18. It is not a permanent right to the house, and it is not guaranteed to run for the spouse's full lifetime either, the court sets the actual length in the order.

What this means for heirs who want to sell. If a probate homestead has been granted and is still in effect, heirs generally cannot force a sale of that specific property or remove the occupying spouse or child while the order stands, even though the will names them as the owners. That does not mean the house can never be sold. It means the timeline and the occupant's rights have to be sorted out through the probate court, usually with an attorney involved on both sides, before a listing makes sense. Trying to list or market a home over an active probate homestead dispute, or pressuring an occupying spouse out informally, can create real legal exposure on top of family strain.

The honest limit here. This is a general framework, not a prediction of how a specific family's case comes out. Whether a homestead gets granted, what property it covers, and how long it lasts all depend on the judge weighing the actual facts: how the house was titled, what the will or trust actually says, what else is in the estate, and the real financial situation of the spouse or children involved. No two blended-family inherited-home situations resolve exactly the same way.

On today's broader Woodland Hills market (Movoto, citywide: $1,300,000 median sale price, 368 active listings, 54-day average days on market), knowing where a property actually stands, occupied under a court order or not, matters before any pricing or timeline conversation makes sense.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can my siblings and I force the sale of our late father's Woodland Hills house if his second wife still lives there?

Not automatically. If she has petitioned for and been granted a probate homestead under Probate Code Sections 6520 through 6528, you generally cannot force a sale or her removal while that court order is in effect, even if the will names you as the owners.

Does a probate homestead apply to anyone living in the house, like a roommate or adult child?

No. Only the decedent's surviving spouse or minor children can petition for a probate homestead. An adult stepchild, a girlfriend or boyfriend, or any other occupant has no claim to this specific protection.

Does the court just ignore what the will says?

No. The decedent's expressed estate plan, meaning the will or trust, is one of the factors the court explicitly weighs, along with the needs of the spouse/children, existing liens, creditor claims, and the interests of the heirs. A clear will favoring the heirs is a real factor against granting or extending the homestead, not something the court disregards.

How long can a probate homestead last?

Whatever period the court's order sets, but never longer than the surviving spouse's lifetime, or, for a minor child, past that child's 18th birthday. It is not automatically a lifetime right.

Is this the same as the Garn-St. Germain mortgage-assumption rule you've written about before?

No. That earlier post covers whether an heir can keep an existing mortgage on an inherited home without refinancing. This is a separate question about a non-owner occupant's legal right to stay in the house itself.

What should heirs actually do if this situation comes up?

Get a probate attorney involved before attempting to list the home or pressure anyone to leave. Whether a homestead has been petitioned for, granted, or doesn't apply at all depends on real facts specific to that estate, and this isn't something to sort out informally between family members under stress.
