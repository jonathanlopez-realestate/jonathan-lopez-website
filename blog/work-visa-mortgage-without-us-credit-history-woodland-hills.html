Relocating to Woodland Hills on a Work Visa: Can I Get a Mortgage Without US Credit History?

Jonathan Lopez, Woodland Hills Realtor · Jim Sandoval Group · CA DRE #02282844 · Updated November 2026

Woodland Hills pulls in a steady wave of relocating professionals, engineers, physicians, and executives moving here on H-1B, L-1, O-1, and similar employer-sponsored work visas for jobs in the Warner Center office corridor and across the Valley. For a lot of them, the first real obstacle isn't finding a home, it's figuring out whether they can even get a mortgage with little or no US credit history. The honest answer is that it depends entirely on which loan type they're pursuing, and the landscape shifted in a meaningful way in 2025.

Start with the option that's no longer on the table for most of these buyers: FHA financing. HUD issued Mortgagee Letter 2025-09 on March 26, 2025, revising residency requirements across FHA's Title I property improvement and manufactured home programs, Title II single-family loans, and Home Equity Conversion Mortgages. Effective May 25, 2025, "non-permanent residents," a category that includes every type of temporary work visa holder, DACA recipients, and asylum seekers without a green card, is no longer eligible for new FHA-insured loans. Only US citizens, lawful permanent residents, and a narrow set of other statuses (citizens of the Federated States of Micronesia, the Marshall Islands, and Palau) qualify for case files assigned on or after that date. The real-world effect was immediate and large: HousingWire's review of lending data comparing the year before the change to the period after found non-permanent residents' share of FHA purchase originations fell from 5.8% nationally to roughly 0.1%. That's a national figure, not specific to Woodland Hills or even Los Angeles County, but it illustrates how completely this door closed. A work-visa buyer who assumes FHA's lower down payment and more flexible credit standards are available to them, because a friend used FHA a few years ago, needs to know that assumption no longer holds.

The good news is that conventional financing didn't change. Fannie Mae continues to purchase and securitize mortgages made to non-permanent residents under essentially the same terms available to US citizens, provided the borrower has a valid Social Security number and an acceptable visa classification or Employment Authorization Document. In practice, lenders are generally looking for a credit score in the 620-and-up range (the same floor used for conventional loans broadly), a two-year US work history, or a transfer from the same global employer abroad followed by US employment, and a two-year credit history. That last requirement trips up a lot of recent arrivals, since a credit file built in another country usually doesn't carry over to a US score. Canadian credit reports are a specific, named exception some lenders will accept directly, but most other countries' credit histories aren't portable in that same way.

That gap, a recent arrival with strong income and a clean financial history abroad but no US credit file, is exactly what ITIN loan programs exist to solve. These are non-QM loans, meaning they're not backed by Fannie Mae or Freddie Mac, built specifically for borrowers using an Individual Taxpayer Identification Number instead of a Social Security number, or for borrowers who have a valid visa but haven't yet built a conventional US credit score. Instead of leaning on a credit score the borrower doesn't have, these programs lean harder on everything else: a larger down payment, commonly reported in the 15 to 25% range across multiple 2026 mortgage-industry sources, documented proof of employment and income, rent and utility payment history as a substitute for a traditional credit file, and sometimes foreign credit reports, bank reference letters, or a CPA letter confirming income and tax filings. It's a real path, and a common one for relocating tech, medical, and finance professionals, but it comes with a real cost in the form of a bigger down payment and, usually, a higher interest rate than a conventional loan would carry.

There's a second friction point that catches relocating buyers off guard even after they've picked a loan type: sourcing the down payment itself. Lenders generally require down payment and reserve funds to be "sourced and seasoned," meaning the money has sat in a verifiable account for a defined period, usually around two months, with a documented paper trail for where it came from. A buyer wiring savings in from a foreign bank account for the first time, right before closing, can trigger extra underwriting scrutiny or delay, since the lender has to trace the funds back to a legitimate source rather than just seeing a large deposit appear. Moving relocation savings into a US account early, well before an offer is written, and keeping the paper trail (transfer records, pay stubs, an employer relocation letter if one applies) organized from the start, avoids a scramble that can otherwise slow down or jeopardize a closing date a seller is counting on.

It's worth distinguishing this from a separate category: pure "foreign national" loan programs, built for buyers who don't live or work in the US at all, often purchasing investment or vacation property from abroad. Those programs typically require 20 to 40% down, since there's no US income or employment history to underwrite against at all. A work-visa holder actually relocating here for a job, with a real paycheck and a lease or home search underway, is in a meaningfully stronger position than that category, and shouldn't assume the higher foreign-national down payment requirement automatically applies to them.

None of this replaces a conversation with a lender who actually works these loan types regularly. Minimum credit scores, exact down payment percentages, and which alternative documentation a given lender will accept all vary, and a visa category that qualifies cleanly for conventional financing at one lender might get routed to an ITIN program at another, depending on how long the borrower has been in the country and what documentation they can produce quickly. Getting pre-qualified early, before house hunting seriously begins, matters more for this group of buyers than almost any other, since the honest answer to "what can I actually borrow" depends on details a buyer can't always assess from memory of how mortgages worked in their home country.

Frequently Asked Questions

I'm on an H-1B visa with no US credit history. Can I still get a mortgage in Woodland Hills?

Yes, through more than one path. If you have a valid Social Security number, a two-year US (or qualifying foreign-to-US transfer) work history, and can build a two-year credit history, conventional Fannie Mae financing is available on the same terms as a US citizen. If you don't yet have that credit history, ITIN loan programs are built specifically for this situation, trading a larger down payment and alternative documentation for the missing credit score.

Can I use an FHA loan since I don't have much saved for a down payment?

Not since May 25, 2025. HUD's Mortgagee Letter 2025-09 removed FHA eligibility for non-permanent residents, which includes every category of work visa holder. That option is closed regardless of down payment size; the path forward is conventional or ITIN financing instead.

What's the real difference between a conventional loan and an ITIN loan for someone in my situation?

Conventional financing requires a Social Security number and an established two-year US credit history, with terms matching what a US citizen would get. ITIN loans are built for borrowers without that credit file, substituting a larger down payment (commonly 15 to 25%) and alternative proof like rent, utility payments, and foreign bank or CPA letters for the missing credit score, usually at a higher rate than a conventional loan.

I'm not living in the US yet, I'm still finishing my visa process abroad. Does the same financing apply to me?

Not quite. Programs built for borrowers actually relocating for US employment are different from pure foreign-national loan programs aimed at non-resident buyers purchasing investment property from abroad, which commonly require 20 to 40% down since there's no US income to underwrite. Once you're actually employed here with documentable income, you're generally in a stronger position than that category.

Will my credit history from my home country transfer over?

Generally no, with one specific named exception: Canadian credit reports are accepted by some US lenders directly. Most other countries' credit histories don't carry over to a US score, which is exactly the gap ITIN loan programs and alternative-documentation underwriting are designed to bridge.

How early should I talk to a lender if I just found out about my relocation to Woodland Hills?

As early as possible, ideally before serious house hunting starts. Which financing path fits, conventional, ITIN, or something else, depends on details like exactly how long you've been in the country, what income documentation you can produce quickly, and your specific visa category, all of which a lender needs time to evaluate before you're ready to write an offer.
