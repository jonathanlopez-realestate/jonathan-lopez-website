Can an HOA Problem Make My Woodland Hills Condo Harder to Appraise or Finance?

Jonathan Lopez, Woodland Hills Realtor · Jim Sandoval Group · CA DRE #02282844 · Updated November 2026

If you own a condo in Woodland Hills and you're thinking about listing it, there's a question worth asking before you set a price: can a typical buyer's lender actually finance a unit in your building at all? It sounds like an odd thing to wonder about your own home, since the unit itself might be in great shape. But condo financing works differently than single-family financing. Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac don't just underwrite the borrower and the unit, they underwrite the whole HOA, its budget, its reserves, its insurance, and its delinquency rate. If the building fails that review, it doesn't matter how well your specific unit shows. Conventional financing simply isn't available there, and that changes who can buy your home and what they can pay.

Fannie Mae issued Lender Letter LL-2026-03 on March 18, 2026, announcing a set of changes to how it reviews condo and co-op projects. The headline change is the retirement of what's called the "Limited Review," a streamlined, lighter-touch process lenders used to be able to apply to many established condo buildings. For loan applications dated August 3, 2026 and after, most established projects with more than 10 units no longer qualify for that streamlined path (a narrow waiver option still exists for smaller projects, generally 10 units or fewer, with its own restrictions). Instead, those buildings go through a Full Review: a lender has to actually examine the HOA's budget, reserve account, insurance coverage, and how many owners are behind on dues, rather than relying on a shortcut.

The second change lands a little later but matters just as much. Starting January 4, 2027, the minimum reserve funding standard Fannie Mae requires rises from 10% to 15% of the HOA's annual budgeted assessment income. In plain terms, on an HOA that collects $1,000,000 a year in dues, the old standard asked for roughly $100,000 held in reserves; the new one asks for roughly $150,000, unless the HOA has something better: an independent reserve study, completed or updated within the past three years, that recommends an even higher funding level and the HOA is actually funding at that level. A reserve study that's older than three years, or an HOA funding only a bare-minimum baseline instead of its own study's recommendation, doesn't satisfy the new rule.

Here's why this matters to you as a seller rather than just to HOA boards and property managers. A condo project that doesn't meet these standards is often described as "non-warrantable," meaning Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac, which back the large majority of conventional mortgages in the country, won't purchase loans secured by units in that building. That doesn't mean your condo can't sell. It means the buyers who can finance it shrink to people using portfolio loans (held by the original lender instead of sold to Fannie or Freddie, typically at a higher rate and with a larger down payment required), non-QM or DSCR loan programs, or cash. Each of those narrows your buyer pool compared to a warrantable building where any conventional buyer can get a standard 30-year mortgage at a standard rate. A smaller buyer pool, even for a well-maintained individual unit, tends to put downward pressure on what the market will actually pay, and it can slow down how long your unit sits before the right buyer, one with the right financing, shows up.

It's worth understanding why these rules exist at all. Reserve funding and project review standards across the mortgage industry have tightened steadily since 2021, after a series of HOA financial failures and underfunded reserves drew national attention to how many associations were quietly years behind on necessary maintenance while charging dues that didn't come close to covering it. Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac both responded with progressively stricter condo project rules over the following several years, and LL-2026-03 is the latest step in that direction rather than an isolated one-time change. The practical effect for anyone selling a condo today is that lenders are looking harder, and earlier, at exactly the kind of HOA paperwork that used to get a lighter look.

Freddie Mac runs a parallel, though not identical, set of condo project standards and its own reserve and review requirements. A building can pass one agency's review and fail the other's, or vice versa, which is part of why checking in advance matters: knowing your building is warrantable with one agency doesn't automatically mean every lender a buyer might use will reach the same conclusion. In practice, most lenders will try Fannie Mae or Freddie Mac eligibility first since that's where the best rates and terms are, and only fall back to a portfolio or non-QM option once a conventional path is ruled out.

The practical takeaway isn't to panic about your HOA. Plenty of Woodland Hills condo buildings run healthy reserves and will sail through a Full Review without issue. The point is that this is now something worth checking before you list, not something you find out mid-escrow when a buyer's loan falls through because an underwriter flagged the HOA's reserve study as three years and two months old. Your listing agent or the HOA's management company can usually tell you where the building actually stands: when the last reserve study was done, what percentage of the budget is currently reserved, and whether there's pending litigation or a delinquency rate that would also factor into a lender's review.

Locally, the numbers around you still matter too. Movoto's Woodland Hills city page, updated October 30, 2026, shows a median sale price of $1,299,000 for September, 379 active listings, and an average 50 days on market, up from 44 a year ago, a market where every buyer in the pool counts a little more than it would in a faster one. Frequently Asked Questions

What is Fannie Mae's "Limited Review," and why does losing it matter?

Limited Review was a streamlined, lighter version of Fannie Mae's condo project review that many established buildings previously qualified for. Lender Letter LL-2026-03 retires it for loan applications dated August 3, 2026 and after, for most established projects over 10 units, meaning those buildings now go through a more thorough Full Review of the HOA's finances.

What changes with reserve funding requirements, and when?

Starting January 4, 2027, Fannie Mae's standard minimum reserve funding level rises from 10% to 15% of the HOA's annual budgeted assessment income, unless the HOA has an independent reserve study, updated within the last three years, that recommends an even higher level and the HOA is funding at that level.

What does "non-warrantable" mean for a condo?

It means the building doesn't meet Fannie Mae's or Freddie Mac's project standards, so those agencies won't buy loans made on units inside it. Buyers there typically need a portfolio loan, a non-QM program, or cash instead of a standard conventional mortgage.

Does this affect my unit even if my own condo is in perfect condition?

Yes. Condo financing reviews the HOA and the building as a whole, not just the individual unit. A well-maintained unit inside a building with thin reserves or no current reserve study can still fail to qualify for conventional financing.

How do I find out if my building's HOA would pass a Full Review?

Your HOA's management company or board can usually tell you when the last reserve study was completed, what percentage of the budget is currently held in reserves, and whether there's pending litigation. Checking before you list avoids finding out mid-escrow.

Should I wait to sell until my HOA fixes any reserve issues?

Not necessarily. It depends on how far out of compliance the building actually is and how much it affects your realistic buyer pool. I can help you figure out where your building actually stands before deciding on timing.
