Does Selling My Woodland Hills Condo Now Require Disclosing a Balcony Inspection Report?

Jonathan Lopez, Woodland Hills Realtor · Jim Sandoval Group · CA DRE #02282844 · Updated November 2026

If you own a condo in Woodland Hills and you're starting to think about selling, there's a piece of paperwork that didn't exist in your disclosure packet a year ago: a balcony inspection report. As of January 1, 2026, it's mandatory, and skipping it isn't an option.

Here's how the two laws fit together. Back in 2019, California passed SB 326, which requires every HOA with three or more units to inspect its exterior elevated elements, balconies, decks, elevated walkways, and stairways, if they sit more than six feet above the ground and are supported by wood or wood-based materials. The inspection has to be done by a licensed structural engineer, architect, or, since July 2024, a civil engineer. Existing associations had to complete their first inspection by January 1, 2025, and from there the law calls for a repeat inspection at least once every 9 years. HOAs that blow the deadline can face fines of $100 to $500 per day, so most associations that own this kind of common-area structure have already had the inspection done or are actively working through it.

SB 410, officially Chapter 516 of the Statutes of 2025, took that existing inspection report and pulled it directly into the sale process. It amended Civil Code sections 4525, 4528, 5200, 5210, and 5551, and the practical effect for a seller is simple: the most recent SB 326 inspection report now has to be included in the same Civil Code 4525 disclosure packet you were already required to hand a buyer. That packet already covered things like the CC&Rs and other governing documents, the HOA's budget and reserve study, board meeting minutes, pending litigation notices, and the written assessment statement showing any current or special assessments. The balcony report is simply the newest line item on that list, not a separate process you have to manage on your own.

For a seller, the real-world impact shows up in two places: cost and timing. On cost, Civil Code 4528 caps what the HOA can charge for assembling the full packet at its actual cost of preparation, and in practice associations commonly charge somewhere in the few-hundred-dollar range to pull everything together, the inspection report included. That's not a huge number against a Woodland Hills sale, but it's a real line item worth planning for rather than discovering at the last minute.

Timing is where sellers actually get hurt if they wait too long to start. Buyers typically get about 15 days under a standard C.A.R. purchase contract to review the disclosure packet and decide whether to remove their contingencies. If your HOA is slow to pull together governing documents, financials, and the balcony report all at once, that 15-day clock can run out before the buyer has everything they're entitled to see, which either delays closing or gives the buyer grounds to extend or cancel. The fix is straightforward: request the full Civil Code 4525 packet, balcony report included, the same week you decide to list, not the week you go into escrow.

It's also worth being clear about what this law does and doesn't change for you personally. SB 410 doesn't require you, as the individual seller, to commission a new inspection or pay for the engineering work yourself. The inspection obligation sits with the HOA, and if your association has already completed its January 2025 deadline, as most should have by now, you're simply handing over a report that already exists. The exposure for most sellers is procedural, making sure the request goes in early, not financial or technical.

One more thing worth flagging if you're weighing whether this even applies to you: the balcony inspection requirement is about exterior elevated elements the association is responsible for maintaining, so it's most relevant to multi-unit condo buildings with shared balconies, walkways, or stairways built on wood-framed structures. A single-family home with no HOA, or an HOA with fewer than three units, isn't covered by SB 326 at all.

If you're sitting on a Woodland Hills condo and starting to plan a sale, the smartest first move under this new law is simple: find out today whether your HOA has already completed its SB 326 inspection, and if so, request the full disclosure packet before you ever go live with a listing. I'm happy to help you get that request started and keep your timeline from getting squeezed later in escrow.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is SB 410 and when did it take effect?

SB 410 is Chapter 516 of the Statutes of 2025, effective January 1, 2026. It amended California Civil Code sections 4525, 4528, 5200, 5210, and 5551 to add the HOA's most recent SB 326 balcony inspection report to the standard disclosure packet a condo seller must give a buyer.

What is SB 326, and is it a different law?

Yes, a separate, older law. SB 326, signed in 2019 and codified at Civil Code 5551, requires HOAs with three or more units to have a licensed structural engineer, architect, or civil engineer inspect exterior elevated elements like balconies, decks, walkways, and stairways. The first inspection deadline was January 1, 2025, with repeat inspections required every 9 years.

Do I have to pay for a new inspection to sell my condo?

No. The inspection obligation belongs to the HOA, not you individually. If your association already completed its SB 326 inspection, which most should have by the January 2025 deadline, you're simply including that existing report in your disclosure packet.

How much does the full disclosure packet typically cost?

Civil Code 4528 caps the HOA's charge at its actual cost of preparing the packet, and in practice associations commonly charge a few hundred dollars to assemble the complete set of documents, inspection report included.

How long does the buyer get to review the disclosure packet?

A standard C.A.R. purchase contract typically gives the buyer about 15 days to review the full packet, balcony report included, before deciding whether to remove contingencies. A slow HOA can shrink that window if the request goes in late.

Does this apply to every home in Woodland Hills, or just condos?

Just properties governed by an HOA with three or more units and exterior elevated elements built on wood-framed structures the association maintains. A single-family home with no HOA isn't subject to SB 326 or this disclosure addition at all.
