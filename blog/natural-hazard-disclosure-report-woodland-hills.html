What Is a Natural Hazard Disclosure Report, and What Should I Actually Do With It When Buying in Woodland Hills?

Jonathan Lopez, Woodland Hills Realtor · Jim Sandoval Group · CA DRE #02282844 · Updated October 2026

If you are buying a home in Woodland Hills, you will almost certainly receive a document called the Natural Hazard Disclosure Statement, or NHD, somewhere in the first days of escrow. Most buyers skim it, see a page full of checked and unchecked boxes, and move on to the next form in the stack. That is a mistake. The NHD is one of the few disclosure documents in a California real estate transaction that tells you something no amount of personal inspection can: whether the government itself has mapped your future home into a zone with a known, documented hazard.

What the law actually requires. California Civil Code Section 1103 requires the seller, with the seller's agent, to disclose whether a property sits within any of six specific hazard zones. These are a Special Flood Hazard Area, as mapped by FEMA; a Dam Inundation Area, mapped by the California Department of Water Resources for flood risk from a dam failure; a Very High Fire Hazard Severity Zone, designated by CAL FIRE; a State Responsibility Area, where the state rather than a local fire department has primary wildfire suppression responsibility; an Earthquake Fault Zone, mapped under the Alquist-Priolo Act for properties near known active faults; and a Seismic Hazard Zone, covering liquefaction and earthquake-induced landslide risk. In practice, the listing agent typically orders this report from a third-party provider and delivers it to the buyer, while the seller signs the underlying statement.

Why this matters specifically in Woodland Hills. Roughly half of Woodland Hills sits inside CAL FIRE's Very High Fire Hazard Severity Zone, a designation tied to the hillside terrain and brush coverage along the Santa Monica Mountains corridor. That is not a reason to avoid buying here. It is a reason to actually read the NHD rather than treat it as a formality, because the fire-zone box checked on that form has real downstream consequences for your insurance options and your long-term carrying costs, separate from anything about the house itself. For the insurance side, see what homeowners insurance really costs in Woodland Hills.

The newest piece: the fire-hardening checklist. As of July 1, 2025, California added a real requirement on top of the standard NHD. Sellers of homes built before January 1, 2010, that sit in a High or Very High Fire Hazard Severity Zone must now also provide a checklist identifying which specific retrofit measures from the State Fire Marshal's Low-Cost Retrofit List are present on the property. That list includes items like ember-resistant attic and foundation vents, a Class A fire-rated roof, ignition-resistant siding, and enclosed rather than open eaves. If you are buying an older Woodland Hills home in the fire zone, this checklist should already be in your disclosure packet. If it is not there, ask for it directly rather than assuming it does not apply. It is a meaningfully different and newer document than the general defensible-space compliance paperwork required under AB 38 for the same fire-zone properties, which covers brush clearance and vegetation spacing around the structure rather than the structure's own construction materials.

Timing: when you should actually see this. The statute requires delivery "as soon as practicable before transfer of title," which in practice means the NHD should land in your inbox within the first few days after your purchase agreement is signed, well before your contingency periods expire. If it has not shown up by the time you are deep into your inspection period, ask your agent to chase it down. This is not a document to receive and skim on the day you are removing contingencies; it is one of the pieces of information that should inform whether you remove them at all.

What to actually do with it. Three things. First, read every box on the form, not just the fire-hazard line, since flood and seismic designations carry their own insurance and inspection implications. Second, get an actual homeowners insurance quote before you remove your financing or inspection contingencies, not after you are already locked into the purchase, since carriers price Very High Fire Hazard Severity Zone risk directly into the premium and availability can vary significantly by carrier. Third, if the home predates 2010 and sits in the fire zone, confirm the hardening checklist is included and ask what it actually shows, since a home with several of those retrofits already in place is a meaningfully different risk and cost picture than one with none.

What happens if a seller does not disclose. Under Civil Code Section 1103.13, a seller or agent who fails to make a required natural hazard disclosure can be held liable for the buyer's actual damages, which can include increased insurance costs, hazard mitigation expenses, and diminished property value discovered after the fact. That protection exists, but it is a remedy after the fact, not a substitute for reading the disclosure and acting on it before you close.

For context, Woodland Hills currently has 379 active listings with a median sale price of $1,299,000, and homes are spending a median of 50 days on the market (Movoto, updated October 30, 2026). Whatever price point you are shopping in, the NHD applies the same way, and it is worth five real minutes of your attention rather than a signature on the way to the next form.

If you want a second set of eyes on your disclosure package, or want to talk through what a Very High Fire Hazard Severity Zone designation actually means for a specific Woodland Hills address you are considering, reach out before you remove your contingencies, not after.

Hazard zone designations come from official state and federal maps and can change; always read your own property's NHD report and confirm insurance availability with a licensed agent. This is general information, not legal advice.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is a Natural Hazard Disclosure report?

A statutory disclosure required under California Civil Code 1103 that tells a buyer whether a property sits within any of six government-mapped hazard zones: flood, dam inundation, very high fire hazard, state wildland fire responsibility, earthquake fault, and seismic hazard.

What are the six hazard zones it covers?

Special Flood Hazard Area (FEMA), Dam Inundation Area (CA Department of Water Resources), Very High Fire Hazard Severity Zone (CAL FIRE), State Responsibility Area for wildfire, Earthquake Fault Zone (Alquist-Priolo), and Seismic Hazard Zone (liquefaction/landslide).

Does a Very High Fire Hazard Severity Zone designation mean I cannot get homeowners insurance?

No, but it does mean carriers price the risk into the premium and some standard carriers may decline to write a new policy, which is why getting a real quote before removing contingencies matters. See the separate homeowners insurance post for cost and availability specifics.

What is the new fire-hardening checklist, and does it apply to my purchase?

Effective July 1, 2025, sellers of homes built before January 1, 2010, in a High or Very High Fire Hazard Severity Zone must disclose which retrofits from the State Fire Marshal's Low-Cost Retrofit List, such as ember-resistant vents or a Class A roof, are present. It applies to older homes in the fire zone specifically, not to every Woodland Hills purchase.

When should I expect to receive the NHD report?

As soon as practicable after your purchase agreement is signed, generally within the first few days of escrow, well before your inspection and loan contingency periods run out.

What happens if the seller or their agent fails to disclose a hazard zone?

Under Civil Code 1103.13, they can be held liable for the buyer's actual damages, including increased insurance costs and diminished property value discovered after closing. That is a legal remedy, not a substitute for reading the report before you close.
