Can My Woodland Hills Listing Agent Keep Everything Above a Certain Price? What a "Net Listing" Really Means

Jonathan Lopez, Woodland Hills Realtor · Jim Sandoval Group · CA DRE #02282844 · Updated November 2026

If you are getting ready to list a home in Woodland Hills, odds are good your agent proposed a standard listing agreement with a percentage-based commission, the kind almost every seller in this market signs. But every so often a seller hears about a different arrangement called a "net listing" and wonders whether it is a smarter deal, or even whether it is legal at all. Here is the honest answer.

What a net listing actually is. In a normal listing agreement, your agent earns a percentage of the final sale price, so their payday and yours move in the same direction: a higher sale price means more money for both of you, in direct proportion. A net listing works differently. You and your agent agree on a specific net amount you want to walk away with, after their compensation and closing costs are subtracted. Whatever the home actually sells for above that net number becomes the agent's fee, with no cap and no fixed percentage tying it back to the sale price in a predictable way.

Is this legal in Woodland Hills, or anywhere in California? Yes. Net listings are not illegal or unethical under California law, according to the California Department of Real Estate's own published guidance, as long as the broker fully discloses their involvement and the legal effect of the arrangement to the seller. Specifically, California Business and Professions Code Section 10176(g) requires the broker to disclose to you, before you accept any offer, the full price the buyer actually paid and the exact dollar amount the broker is keeping as their fee. Skipping that disclosure is where a net listing crosses from legal into a real violation, not the arrangement itself.

Why the DRE still warns sellers away from them. Even though net listings are permitted, the Department of Real Estate's guidance recommends they only be used with sophisticated sellers, or sellers who have their own independent professional advisor reviewing the deal. The reason is a straightforward conflict of interest. In a standard percentage listing, your agent's financial incentive and your financial incentive point the same direction: get the highest price possible. In a net listing, once the price clears your net number, every additional dollar goes entirely to the agent, not split with you by percentage. That creates a real incentive for the net number itself to be set lower than your home is actually worth, since the lower that starting number, the more room there is above it for the agent to keep. A seller without independent advice has no easy way to know whether the net figure they agreed to actually reflected the home's real market value, or whether it left money on the table before the home was ever shown to a single buyer.

What this looks like in practice. Say a Woodland Hills home is genuinely worth $1,300,000 on today's market. Under a standard 5 percent commission, the agent earns roughly $65,000 and the seller nets the rest after costs, with both sides wanting the same outcome: the highest achievable price. Under a net listing set at $1,100,000, if the home sells for $1,300,000, the agent keeps the full $200,000 difference above the net figure, not a percentage of it, an outcome far more lucrative for the agent than a standard commission would have produced on the same sale. That gap is exactly why the DRE flags these arrangements as higher risk for sellers who do not have a second set of eyes on the number before they sign.

What a Woodland Hills seller should actually do with this information. You do not need to refuse a net listing outright if one is ever proposed, but you should treat it the same way the DRE recommends: get an independent opinion on what your home is genuinely worth, from a real CMA grounded in actual comparable sales, before agreeing to any net number. Most sellers in this market are better served by a standard percentage commission precisely because it keeps your agent's incentive and your own pointed in the same direction from the first conversation to closing day.

On today's market, the starting number matters even more than usual. RealtyTrac's 91367 zip-level data (read October 2026) shows a $1,100,000 median sold price, down 11.4 percent year-over-year, against 735 active listings, up sharply (80.15 percent) from a year earlier. Movoto's citywide figures put the broader Woodland Hills median at $1,300,000, also down (3.6 percent year-over-year), with 368 active listings and a 54-day average time on market. Both datasets point the same direction: more competition for sellers right now, which makes getting an accurate, independently-grounded starting price more important than it would be in a tighter market, whatever compensation structure you ultimately sign.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is a net listing legal in California?

Yes. The California Department of Real Estate's own guidance states net listings are not illegal or unethical, as long as the broker fully discloses their involvement and the arrangement's legal effect to the seller, including the full sale price and the broker's exact fee, before the seller accepts any offer, per Business and Professions Code Section 10176(g).

What makes a net listing riskier than a standard commission for a seller?

In a standard percentage commission, the agent's incentive and the seller's incentive both point toward the highest possible sale price. In a net listing, the agent keeps everything above an agreed net number with no percentage cap, which can create an incentive to set that net number lower than the home's real value, leaving the seller with less information about whether they got a fair deal.

Who should actually consider a net listing?

The DRE's guidance says net listings should only be used with sophisticated sellers, or sellers who already have their own independent advisor reviewing the numbers, precisely because of the conflict of interest described above.

What has to be disclosed in a net listing?

The broker must disclose the full sale price the buyer actually paid and the exact dollar amount the broker is keeping as compensation, and that disclosure has to happen before the seller accepts an offer, not after closing.

Why don't more Woodland Hills sellers use net listings if they're legal?

Most sellers and agents in this market use a standard percentage-based listing agreement because it keeps both parties' financial incentives aligned on the same goal, the highest achievable sale price, without the conflict-of-interest risk a net arrangement introduces.

How do I know if my listing price is accurate before I sign anything?

Ask for a real comparative market analysis built on actual recent comparable sales in your specific area of Woodland Hills, not an automated online estimate, before agreeing to any compensation structure, net or percentage-based.
