Can I Split My Woodland Hills Lot Under SB9, or Does the Fire Zone Rule Me Out?

Jonathan Lopez, Woodland Hills Realtor · Jim Sandoval Group · CA DRE #02282844 · Updated November 2026

If you own a single-family home in Woodland Hills, there's a question worth asking before you assume your home's value is just the house and the land under it: could this lot legally become two? California's SB9, in effect since January 1, 2022, gave single-family-zoned homeowners statewide a new, by-right path to add density to their own property, without a rezoning hearing or a city council vote. Whether that actually applies to your specific lot in Woodland Hills depends on a handful of real rules, including one that rules out a meaningful share of this neighborhood entirely.

SB9 actually does two separate things, codified in two different sections of the Government Code. Section 65852.21 lets an eligible single-family lot hold up to two primary units, essentially converting it into a duplex, without going through a discretionary rezoning process. Section 66411.7 is the urban lot split provision: it lets an eligible owner split one qualifying lot into two separate parcels, as long as each resulting parcel is at least 1,200 square feet and each is at least 40% of the original lot's size. A property can only use this lot-split provision once. Combine both provisions and the ceiling is four units total on what used to be a single-family lot: a duplex on each of the two new, smaller parcels. That is a different path from adding an ADU on the same lot without subdividing.

Here's the rule that matters most for Woodland Hills specifically. Both of SB9's statutory sections carry a built-in exclusion for properties located within a high or very high fire hazard severity zone, as mapped by the State Fire Marshal, unless the site has been specifically excluded from that zone designation by the local jurisdiction, or has adopted fire hazard mitigation measures that satisfy existing building standards or state fire mitigation rules. This isn't a temporary or discretionary carve-out particular to one wildfire season; it's written into the statute itself. Woodland Hills is not a uniform fire-risk neighborhood: the Los Angeles Fire Department's own Very High Fire Hazard Severity Zone designation covers roughly half of the neighborhood, concentrated on the hillside and mountain-adjacent side, while flatter sections of 91367 generally fall outside that designation. In practical terms, that means a flatter, lower-risk parcel may well be eligible for an SB9 lot split, while a hillside parcel a few blocks away, inside the mapped VHFHSZ, generally is not, through no fault of the home's condition or the owner's planning. Confirming which side of that line a specific address falls on requires checking it against LAFD's current hazard zone map directly, not assuming based on the general neighborhood.

If a lot does clear the fire-zone test and the other eligibility rules (single-family zoning, not on protected farmland or wetlands, and, as of a 2026 change under AB 1061, now potentially including historic district properties as long as the historic structure itself isn't demolished), Los Angeles layers on its own implementation requirements for an urban lot split. The applicant has to sign an affidavit affirming intent to occupy one of the units on the resulting parcel as a principal residence for a minimum of three years after the lot split is recorded; that occupancy requirement applies to the lot-split path specifically, not to a simple duplex conversion with no subdivision involved. On parking, the standard rule allows up to one covered, off-street parking space per new unit, but that requirement disappears entirely if the property sits within a half mile of a high-quality transit corridor or major transit stop, which is directly relevant to parcels near Woodland Hills' Orange Line (now G Line) busway and its Warner Center-area stations — the same corridor that comes up in questions about SB79 transit-corridor zoning.

It's worth knowing, too, that SB9's legal footing has been genuinely contested, not just politically criticized. In April 2024, a Los Angeles trial court sided with five Southern California charter cities, Redondo Beach, Carson, Torrance, Whittier, and Del Mar, ruling SB9 unconstitutional as applied to charter cities on the theory that it oversteps into purely municipal zoning authority without a close enough tie to the state's stated affordable-housing purpose. That ruling was reversed by the Court of Appeal on November 12, 2025, which sent the underlying constitutional question back to the trial court rather than deciding it outright, but which restored SB9's statewide applicability in the meantime, including in Los Angeles, itself a charter city. As of this writing, there's no active charter-city exemption in effect anywhere in California, but the door isn't fully closed on a future ruling either. Anyone planning a lot split as part of a sale or purchase decision should treat this as current, workable law today, not as settled forever.

What does this actually mean for value? A genuinely split-able lot has more development potential than one that isn't, and that potential is part of what a knowledgeable buyer, builder, or lender will factor into what they're willing to pay, separate from the existing house's finishes or square footage. Industry estimates on exactly how much a successful split adds to a property's value vary widely and come mostly from ADU and lot-split consulting and construction companies with an interest in promoting the idea, not from an independent study specific to the San Fernando Valley, so this post isn't going to repeat a specific dollar figure as fact. What is factual is the eligibility test itself: zoning, lot size and shape, fire hazard zone status, and whether the one-time split has already been used. That's a real, checkable starting point before anyone prices in development potential that may or may not legally exist on a given parcel.

Locally, Movoto's Woodland Hills page (last confirmed update Oct 30, 2026) shows a $1,299,000 September median sale price, 379 active listings, and 50 days on market, up from 44 a year ago. Frequently Asked Questions

What does SB9 actually let a Woodland Hills homeowner do?

It allows an eligible single-family lot to hold up to two units (a duplex conversion) and, separately, allows a one-time split of an eligible lot into two parcels, each at least 1,200 square feet and 40% of the original lot. Combining both tops out at four units total on the original lot.

Why doesn't SB9 apply to every lot in Woodland Hills?

State law excludes parcels inside a high or very high fire hazard severity zone, as mapped by the State Fire Marshal, unless the jurisdiction has excluded that specific site or it meets existing fire mitigation standards. LAFD's Very High Fire Hazard Severity Zone covers roughly half of Woodland Hills, mostly on the hillside side, so eligibility genuinely varies block to block.

If my lot qualifies for a split, do I have to live there?

Yes, for the urban lot split path specifically: Los Angeles requires an affidavit committing to occupy one of the resulting units as a principal residence for at least three years after the split is recorded. A duplex conversion with no lot split has no such requirement.

Do I need extra parking for a new unit?

Up to one covered space per new unit is the standard rule, but that requirement is waived entirely if the property is within a half mile of a major transit stop or high-quality transit corridor, relevant to parcels near the Orange/G Line busway.

Is SB9 still valid law in 2026, or was it struck down?

It's currently valid statewide, including in Los Angeles. A 2024 trial court ruling exempted five charter cities, but the Court of Appeal reversed that in November 2025 and restored statewide applicability, though the constitutional question itself was sent back to the trial court rather than finally resolved.

How much would splitting my lot actually add to my home's value?

There's no independent, California-specific study with a reliable dollar figure; most numbers circulating online come from ADU and lot-split industry blogs. The more useful starting question is eligibility itself: zoning, lot size, and fire zone status, which I can help you check before any value assumption.
