A PACE Assessment Just Showed Up on My Woodland Hills Title Report. Can I Still Sell?

Jonathan Lopez, Woodland Hills Realtor · Jim Sandoval Group · CA DRE #02282844 · Updated November 2026

A PACE or HERO line on a Woodland Hills title report can look like a last-minute problem that will kill the sale. It usually is not a regular mortgage, and California law does not automatically require you to pay the remaining balance because you are selling. What the law requires, and what the buyer's lender may separately require, are two different things. Mixing them up is how sellers get surprised at the end of escrow.

PACE stands for Property Assessed Clean Energy. It is a financing tool some homeowners used for work such as solar panels, HVAC systems, or windows. You repay it as a voluntary contractual assessment added to the property tax bill. Streets and Highways Code section 5898.30 is the core collection rule: the assessment, plus interest and any penalties, is a lien on the property until it is paid, and it is collected in the same manner and at the same time as the general taxes of the city or county, unless another procedure was authorized. It rides on the tax bill, and it stays attached to the house until the balance is gone.

That last point is where a lot of Woodland Hills sellers get confused, especially if they have already been through a HELOC payoff. A HELOC is a loan against the house. A PACE assessment is a tax-bill lien. California law does not automatically force a full payoff of the remaining PACE balance at the moment of sale. The state rules that govern these contracts, including Streets and Highways Code section 5913, require the program administrator to tell the homeowner, before the contract is signed, that the property will be subject to a lien and that the obligations under the contract may be required to be paid in full before the owner sells or refinances. "May be required" is the important phrase. It is a warning that a payoff can be a condition of a later sale or refinance, not a statute that always demands one.

The California Department of Financial Protection and Innovation, which licenses PACE program administrators, puts the practical risk in plainer words: a PACE assessment can make it more difficult to sell or refinance a property because a lien is placed on the home until the contract is paid off. That is a consumer warning, not a closing instruction, but it is the right way to think about the issue when you are getting ready to list.

What often turns a "may" into a "must" is the buyer's financing. Fannie Mae's Selling Guide, section B5-3.4-01 (Property Assessed Clean Energy Loans, updated October 8, 2025), says PACE loans typically have automatic first lien priority over previously recorded mortgages, and that Fannie Mae will not purchase a mortgage secured by a property with an outstanding PACE loan unless the terms of that PACE program do not provide for lien priority over first mortgage liens. If the PACE loan is structured as a subordinate lien or an unsecured loan, a first mortgage may still be underwritten to standard guidelines. California PACE, as set up under section 5898.30, is collected like property taxes and the statute applies the state's tax-assessment lien-priority rules. That is why a buyer using conventional financing often cannot close unless the assessment is paid at or before closing. When a payoff is required, it is usually handled through escrow and comes out of the seller's proceeds unless the parties agree otherwise in the contract.

If a payoff is needed, state law at least makes the mechanics cleaner than many other kinds of debt. Section 5913 also requires administrators to tell homeowners that an amount owed under an assessment contract may be repaid before it is due, with no early repayment penalty.

Sellers have a disclosure job even if the assessment will be paid off in escrow. Civil Code section 1102.6b applies to residential sales already subject to California's transfer-disclosure rules when the property has a contractual assessment under Chapter 29 of the Streets and Highways Code, the chapter that authorizes PACE. The seller must make a good faith effort to obtain the disclosure notice described in Streets and Highways Code section 5898.24 and deliver it to the prospective buyer, as long as the notice is made available. The recorded document that helps sellers meet that duty is titled "Payment of Contractual Assessment Required." It includes the annual amount, whether the assessment expires or is perpetual, the purpose of the funds, and who is paid. Section 1102.6b also allows a substantially equivalent disclosure from another source, including a copy of the most recent year's property tax bill or an itemization of current assessment amounts. In practice, the tax bill and the title report are often how a Woodland Hills seller first notices the charge at all.

One local fact is worth knowing so you do not assume the assessment vanished. Los Angeles County ended new originations under its own PACE program in 2020. Its contracts with Renovate America and Renew Financial expired on April 3, 2020, and it stopped approving new assessment contracts through PACE Funding Group on May 13, 2020. The County has been clear that existing, already-signed assessment contracts are still being serviced and still have to be paid as agreed. A program ending is not the same thing as a balance being forgiven. If the line is still on your tax bill, it is still a live lien.

That matters more in a slower sale than it does in a bidding war. Redfin's 91367 housing-market page, based on the three months ending October 2026, puts the zip code's median sale price at $1,104,934, down 3.5% from the same period a year earlier, with a median of 60 days on market. About 31.9% of homes in that window had a price drop. A late-escrow surprise that cuts into proceeds or delays the buyer's loan is harder to absorb when homes are already taking about two months to sell.

The practical sequence is straightforward. Before you list, pull the most recent property tax bill and ask the PACE administrator for a current payoff statement. Put that number on the net sheet next to the mortgage, any HELOC, and the usual closing costs, so you are looking at what you actually walk away with, not just the list price. If the improvement was solar, remember that owned solar and leased solar are valued differently; a PACE assessment is a third, separate question from either of those. And treat the contractual assessment disclosure as part of the same seller disclosure work you are already doing, not as an optional extra.

This is general information, not legal, tax, or lending advice. The exact balance, whether a particular buyer's loan can close with the assessment in place, and how the payoff is handled in escrow should be confirmed with the PACE administrator, the title company, and the buyer's lender, and with a real estate attorney or tax professional if the numbers are large.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does California law require me to pay off a PACE assessment when I sell?

No. California law does not automatically require the remaining balance to be paid off just because you are selling. Streets and Highways Code section 5913 requires administrators to tell homeowners that the balance may have to be paid in full before a sale or refinance, which is a warning, not an automatic payoff rule.

Why do so many buyers' lenders still insist on a payoff?

Because of lien priority. Fannie Mae's Selling Guide (B5-3.4-01, updated October 8, 2025) will not purchase a mortgage on a property with an outstanding PACE loan if that PACE loan has priority over the first mortgage. California PACE is collected with the property tax bill and section 5898.30 applies tax-assessment lien-priority rules, so a conventional buyer often cannot close unless the assessment is paid.

What do I have to disclose to the buyer?

Civil Code section 1102.6b requires a good faith effort to obtain the contractual assessment disclosure described in Streets and Highways Code section 5898.24 and deliver it to the prospective buyer, as long as the notice is made available. A substantially equivalent document, such as a copy of the most recent year's property tax bill or an itemization of current assessment amounts, can also satisfy that duty.

Is there a prepayment penalty if I pay it off at closing?

State law requires administrators to tell homeowners that an amount owed under an assessment contract may be repaid before it is due, with no early repayment penalty. Confirm the exact payoff figure with the administrator, because interest and fees can still be part of the current balance.

Los Angeles County ended its PACE program. Does that erase my assessment?

No. The County stopped approving new assessments under its own program in 2020, but it continues to service existing, already-signed assessment contracts, and those balances still have to be paid as agreed. A program ending is not the same thing as a balance being forgiven.

How do I find the remaining balance before I list?

Start with the most recent property tax bill, then ask the PACE administrator named on the bill or on the recorded "Payment of Contractual Assessment Required" notice for a current payoff statement. Put that number on your net sheet before you choose a list price.
