Can I Buy a Fixer-Upper in Woodland Hills With an FHA 203(k) Renovation Loan?

Jonathan Lopez, Woodland Hills Realtor · Jim Sandoval Group · CA DRE #02282844 · Updated November 2026

Woodland Hills has plenty of older homes with good bones in good locations that still need a new kitchen, a roof, or a full systems update before they're truly move-in ready. First-time buyers looking at one of these often assume the only path is to close on the home as-is and then scramble for a second loan, a credit card, or a contractor's payment plan to fund the work afterward. An FHA 203(k) loan is built specifically to avoid that: it wraps the purchase price and the renovation budget into one mortgage, with one closing and one monthly payment, instead of financing the house and the repairs as two separate problems.

HUD, which insures the program, splits it into two tiers. The Limited 203(k) (sometimes called the Streamlined 203(k)) covers up to $35,000 in repairs and is meant for cosmetic or non-structural work: a new kitchen or bathroom, flooring, a roof replacement, HVAC, or minor electrical and plumbing updates. Because the work can't touch the home's structure, HUD doesn't require a 203(k) consultant on this tier, which keeps the process closer to a normal FHA purchase. The Standard 203(k) is for everything bigger: structural repairs, room additions, moving load-bearing walls, or anything beyond that $35,000 Limited ceiling. The total Standard 203(k) loan amount is bounded by FHA's loan limit for the property's county, which is set higher for Los Angeles County than the national floor and adjusts annually, so the realistic repair budget on a Standard loan depends on the purchase price and that current limit together, something a lender needs to confirm case by case. The tradeoff for that bigger scope is a mandatory HUD-approved 203(k) consultant, who inspects the proposed work, reviews contractor bids, and signs off on each draw before money moves.

Qualifying follows the same baseline as a regular FHA loan: a minimum 580 credit score to access the standard 3.5% down payment (scores between 500 and 579 require 10% down instead), and the home has to be the buyer's primary residence. That owner-occupancy rule is a real limit on who this program is for. It's not available to someone buying a home purely to renovate and resell, and on a 1-to-4-unit property, the buyer still has to occupy one of the units themselves. A pure investor flip needs different financing entirely.

The mechanics of actually getting the repair money are different from a normal mortgage, where the full loan amount funds at closing. On a 203(k), the renovation portion of the loan is held back in an escrow-like repair account rather than handed over at the closing table. As work is completed, the contractor (who has to be licensed and is typically vetted as part of the loan file) submits for a draw, and on a Standard 203(k), the HUD consultant inspects the completed stage of work before that draw is released. HUD's program rules give buyers roughly a 6-month window after closing to complete the approved scope of work, which is workable for a kitchen remodel or a new roof but tight for a true gut renovation, a timeline worth mapping against the actual project before counting on this loan.

There are real costs that come with the convenience. A 203(k) carries standard FHA mortgage insurance premiums, both an upfront premium at closing and an ongoing annual premium built into the monthly payment, the same as any other FHA loan. Rates on 203(k) loans can run slightly above a plain FHA purchase loan, and the Standard tier adds the consultant's fee into the overall project cost. Conventional lenders offer a comparable product, Fannie Mae's HomeStyle Renovation loan, which allows a lower down payment option for some buyers and doesn't carry FHA's mortgage insurance structure, but it typically requires a higher credit score than FHA's 580 floor. Which program actually costs less depends on a specific buyer's credit, down payment, and project scope, not a blanket answer either way.

On Woodland Hills' current market (Movoto: $1,300,000 median sale price, 368 active listings, 54-day average days on market), a 3.5% down payment on a median-priced home runs about $45,500, with the repair budget added on top of the purchase price inside the same loan rather than financed separately. For a first-time buyer priced out of move-in-ready inventory but open to an older home with real potential, that single-loan structure can be the difference between a workable purchase and one that doesn't pencil out at all. The right next step is running the specific numbers, purchase price, estimated repair scope, and current FHA county loan limit, together with a lender before writing an offer on a particular fixer-upper.

Frequently Asked Questions

What's the actual difference between the Limited and Standard 203(k) loan?

The Limited 203(k) covers up to $35,000 in non-structural repairs (kitchen, bath, flooring, HVAC, roof) with no HUD consultant required. The Standard 203(k) covers larger structural work with a higher ceiling tied to FHA's county loan limit, but it requires a HUD-approved consultant to oversee the project and approve draws.

How much down payment do I need for an FHA 203(k) loan?

The same baseline as a regular FHA loan: 3.5% down at a 580+ credit score, or 10% down if your score is between 500 and 579.

Can I use a 203(k) loan on a home I plan to rent out instead of live in?

No. FHA 203(k) loans require owner-occupancy. On a 1-to-4-unit property, you have to occupy one of the units yourself; a buyer purchasing purely to renovate and resell or rent needs different financing.

How does my contractor actually get paid during the renovation?

The repair portion of the loan sits in a repair account rather than funding at closing. The contractor submits for a draw as work stages are completed, and on a Standard 203(k), the HUD consultant inspects the completed work before that draw is released.

How long do I have to complete the repairs after closing?

HUD's program rules generally allow about 6 months after closing to complete the approved scope of work, workable for a remodel or a roof but tight for a full gut renovation.

Is a 203(k) loan the same as a HomeStyle renovation loan?

No. HomeStyle is Fannie Mae's conventional renovation loan. It can offer a lower down payment option for some buyers and skips FHA's mortgage insurance structure, but it typically requires a higher credit score than FHA's 580 floor. Which one costs less depends on your specific credit and project, not a fixed answer.
