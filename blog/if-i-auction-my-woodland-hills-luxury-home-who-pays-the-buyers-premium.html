If I Auction My Woodland Hills Luxury Home, Who Actually Pays the Buyer's Premium?

Jonathan Lopez, Woodland Hills Realtor · Jim Sandoval Group · CA DRE #02282844 · Updated November 2026

Woodland Hills sellers at the higher end of the market occasionally get approached by a luxury auction house pitching an alternative to the traditional listed-sale process: put the home up for a live or online auction instead of waiting out a standard marketing period. Before agreeing to anything, it's worth understanding exactly how the money moves, because an auction sale works differently from a traditional one in ways that affect both the buyer's actual cost and the seller's net proceeds.

The single most misunderstood piece is the buyer's premium. In a traditional sale, the price a buyer bids is the price the seller receives, minus negotiated costs and commissions. In an auction, the winning bid is not the buyer's final purchase price. Auction houses add a buyer's premium on top of the winning bid, a percentage fee that the buyer pays in addition to the hammer price, and it is the buyer's obligation, not the seller's. Premiums commonly fall somewhere in the 5% to 10% range depending on the specific auction company and the terms of that sale, so a buyer who wins at $4,000,000 with a 10% premium in place is actually committing to pay $4,400,000 total, not $4,000,000. Sellers sometimes assume a higher winning bid number is good news across the board, but the premium is a separate revenue stream for the auction house, layered on top of, not subtracted from, what the seller receives from the bid itself.

Auctions also come in two structurally different formats, and the difference matters for how much control a seller keeps. A reserve auction allows the seller to set a confidential minimum acceptable price before bidding opens. If the highest bid doesn't clear that reserve, the seller is under no obligation to sell, and the property simply doesn't transact at auction. An absolute auction, sometimes called a no-reserve auction, removes that floor entirely: the home sells to whoever bids highest, no matter how low that number turns out to be. Absolute auctions tend to generate more buyer interest and urgency precisely because bidders know there's no hidden minimum working against them, but that same feature means a seller who chooses this format is accepting real downside risk in exchange for a firmer, faster sale.

None of this means an auction is free marketing for the seller. Sellers typically still pay the customary closing costs they'd pay in any sale, and auction houses generally charge a separate marketing fee to cover the advertising, events, and promotion built into the auction's short timeline. That fee structure is different from a standard listing commission, not necessarily cheaper, and should be compared directly against what a traditional listing commission and marketing budget would cost for the same home before assuming an auction saves money outright.

What an auction format reliably does deliver is time compression. A traditional luxury listing can sit on the market for weeks or months while buyers negotiate, inspect, and finance a purchase on their own schedule. An auction compresses all of that into a defined marketing window, typically a matter of weeks, followed by a single sale event with a hard deadline. For a seller who needs certainty about when a sale will actually close, that compressed timeline can be worth more than squeezing out the last few percentage points of sale price through prolonged negotiation.

Auctions also come with a qualification step most traditional buyers never encounter. Because the auction house needs assurance that whoever is bidding can actually close, bidders are typically required to register in advance and show proof of funds or financing, and often put down a refundable deposit, before they're allowed to bid at all. This filters out unqualified lookers before the event even starts, which can be a meaningful advantage for a seller who wants serious bidders only, but it also means the buyer pool at the auction itself may be smaller than the pool of people who'd casually tour a traditionally listed home.

Whether an auction actually makes sense for a specific Woodland Hills luxury home depends on the seller's own priorities: certainty and speed on one side, maximum achievable price and full control over the final number on the other. A reserve auction splits the difference by protecting a price floor while still creating bidding urgency; an absolute auction maximizes urgency at the cost of that protection. Either way, the buyer's premium, the marketing fee, and the standard closing costs all need to be modeled against what a traditional sale would net before signing an auction listing agreement, not assumed away because the headline bid number looks attractive.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is a buyer's premium, and who actually pays it?

It's a percentage fee, commonly in the 5% to 10% range depending on the auction company, added to the winning bid to produce the buyer's total purchase price. The buyer pays it, not the seller; the seller's proceeds are based on the winning bid itself, before the premium is added on top.

What's the difference between a reserve auction and an absolute auction?

A reserve auction lets the seller set a confidential minimum price; if bidding doesn't reach it, the seller doesn't have to sell. An absolute auction has no minimum at all, the home sells to the highest bidder regardless of the final number.

Does auctioning my Woodland Hills home cost me more than listing it traditionally?

Not necessarily more, but differently. Sellers still pay customary closing costs plus an auction marketing fee, which isn't the same structure as a standard listing commission. Compare both cost structures against your specific home before assuming either one is cheaper.

How much faster is an auction sale compared to a typical luxury listing?

Auctions compress the process into a defined marketing window, typically a matter of weeks, followed by a single sale event with a hard closing deadline, instead of the open-ended timeline of a traditionally listed home sitting on the market.

Do bidders have to qualify before they can bid on a luxury auction property?

Usually yes. Most auction houses require advance registration with proof of funds or financing, and often a refundable deposit, before allowing someone to bid, which filters out unqualified lookers before the event starts.

Is an auction the right fit for every Woodland Hills luxury listing?

No. It depends on whether the seller prioritizes speed and certainty (better fit for an absolute auction) or maximum achievable price with a protected floor (better fit for a reserve auction or a traditional listing). Model the real numbers for your specific home before choosing a format.
