Does My Drone Photographer Actually Need an FAA License to Shoot My Woodland Hills Luxury Listing?

Jonathan Lopez, Woodland Hills Realtor · Jim Sandoval Group · CA DRE #02282844 · Updated November 2026

Drone video has become close to a standard line item on Woodland Hills luxury marketing plans. Sellers see it quoted alongside professional photography and a 3D tour and approve it the same way, as a package detail. What almost nobody asks, including plenty of agents, is the one question that actually carries legal weight: is the person piloting that drone over your home licensed to do it for a paid job.

Hobby rules and commercial rules are not the same rules. The FAA draws a hard line between recreational drone flying and any flight connected to compensation or business purpose. A neighbor flying their own drone over their own backyard for fun generally falls under the FAA's recreational flyer exception. A photographer hired, even informally, to shoot marketing footage for your listing is operating commercially the moment money or a business relationship is involved, full stop, regardless of how casual the arrangement feels. Commercial drone operation in the United States is governed by 14 CFR Part 107, the Small UAS Rule the FAA finalized in 2016. Under Part 107, the person actually flying has to hold an FAA Remote Pilot Certificate, earned by passing the FAA's Part 107 knowledge test, or fly under the direct supervision of someone who holds one.

Why this matters more here than in a lot of other markets. Woodland Hills sits in the west San Fernando Valley, an area with real general-aviation activity nearby, including Van Nuys Airport, a towered airport with its own surrounding controlled airspace. Flying a drone inside controlled airspace, even well below the usual 400-foot recreational ceiling, generally requires an additional step beyond holding a Part 107 certificate: site-specific authorization through the FAA's LAANC (Low Altitude Authorization and Notification Capability) system, or a separate FAA waiver if LAANC isn't available for that spot. A certified pilot who skips this step isn't just bending a technicality. They're flying somewhere the FAA has specifically restricted without the matching authorization for that flight.

The exposure doesn't stay with the photographer. If an unlicensed or unauthorized drone flight causes a real problem, a crash onto a neighbor's property, an injury, a privacy complaint from someone who didn't agree to be filmed, the FAA doesn't only have the photographer to look at. The seller who commissioned the shoot and the agent who arranged it can both get pulled into the resulting liability conversation, especially if a neighbor or an insurer gets involved. This is exactly the kind of unglamorous pre-listing question that's easy to skip and expensive to have skipped.

What to actually ask before any drone leaves the ground at your home. Two direct questions cover almost all of the real risk. First, does the operator hold a current FAA Part 107 Remote Pilot Certificate, and can they show it. Second, have they checked and secured airspace authorization for this specific address and date through LAANC, not just a general assumption that "it's fine around here." A legitimate commercial drone operator will have both answers ready without hesitation, because they deal with this on every job, not just yours.

Where this fits with the rest of your marketing plan. None of this is a reason to skip drone video for a luxury listing that genuinely benefits from an aerial shot of a hillside lot or a large parcel. It's a reason to treat the vendor the same way you'd treat any other licensed professional working on your home: confirm the credential before the work happens, not after something goes wrong. Woodland Hills' 91367 ZIP code currently shows a $1,199,000 median sale price, roughly 212 active listings, and a 41-day average time on market (Movoto). At that price point, buyers expect a polished marketing package, and a properly authorized drone shoot is part of delivering it without creating a liability problem on the side.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does a real estate drone photographer actually need an FAA license?

Yes, if they're being paid or working for a business purpose, which covers essentially every real estate marketing shoot. Under FAA Part 107, the operator must hold a Remote Pilot Certificate, or fly under the direct supervision of someone who does. The FAA's recreational hobbyist exception only covers flights with no compensation or business connection at all.

What's the difference between a hobby drone flight and a commercial one?

Compensation and business purpose, not the drone itself or how casual the arrangement feels. A friend flying their own drone for fun is recreational. A photographer hired, even informally or for a discounted favor, to produce marketing footage for a listing is commercial under FAA rules.

Why does Woodland Hills specifically need an extra airspace check?

The west San Fernando Valley includes areas near Van Nuys Airport, a towered airport with its own controlled airspace. Flying inside that airspace generally requires authorization through the FAA's LAANC system for the specific flight, in addition to the operator holding a Part 107 certificate. The exact boundary should be checked through the FAA's own current tools for the address in question, not assumed.

What happens if an unlicensed or unauthorized drone flight causes a problem?

The FAA's enforcement and any resulting liability isn't limited to the photographer. A seller or listing agent who arranged an unauthorized flight can be pulled into the fallout from an injury, property damage, or a neighbor's complaint, which is why confirming credentials up front matters.

What should I ask my photographer or agent before a drone shoot at my home?

Ask directly whether the pilot holds a current FAA Part 107 Remote Pilot Certificate and whether airspace authorization through LAANC has been secured for that specific address and date. A legitimate commercial operator will answer both without hesitation.

Does this mean I should skip drone video for my luxury listing?

No. It means confirming the vendor is actually authorized to do the work, the same way you'd confirm a contractor is licensed before work starts on your home. A properly authorized drone shoot still adds real marketing value for a hillside lot or a large parcel.
