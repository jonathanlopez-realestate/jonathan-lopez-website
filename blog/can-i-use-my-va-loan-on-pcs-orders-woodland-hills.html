Relocating to Woodland Hills on PCS Orders: Can I Use My VA Loan, and Do I Have to Move In Right Away?

Jonathan Lopez · Woodland Hills Realtor, Jim Sandoval Group · CA DRE #02282844 · Updated October 2026

Military families receiving PCS orders to the Los Angeles area often start their Woodland Hills home search with an outdated assumption carried over from an earlier VA loan, or from a friend's experience years ago: that VA financing tops out at some fixed county limit well below what homes here actually cost. That assumption hasn't been accurate since 2020, and it's worth correcting before a service member rules out neighborhoods they can actually afford.

VA's own loan limits page states the rule plainly: a veteran with full entitlement has no VA loan limit, as long as the borrower can afford the loan and the property appraisal supports the purchase price. This changed with the Blue Water Navy Vietnam Veterans Act of 2020, which eliminated county-level VA loan limits specifically for borrowers with full entitlement. Before that law, VA loans were capped at the same conforming loan limits that applied to conventional financing, county by county. For 2026, the baseline conforming limit is $832,750, rising to $1,249,125 in high-cost counties, which covers most of Los Angeles County, including Woodland Hills. Those figures still matter, but only for a veteran with partial entitlement, meaning some of their VA benefit is already tied up in an existing loan. A veteran with full entitlement, generally someone who has never used their VA loan benefit before, or who paid off a prior VA loan and sold the property, isn't bound by that county figure at all.

Full entitlement doesn't mean unlimited approval, VA is clear on this point too. A lender still underwrites the loan based on the borrower's income, credit, debts, and assets, the same way any mortgage gets approved. What changes is that the VA guarantee itself doesn't cap out at a specific dollar figure tied to the county, which matters directly in a market like Woodland Hills where the median sale price already sits above the high-cost conforming limit that still governs partial-entitlement VA borrowers and conventional buyers alike.

Timing is the second question that comes up constantly with PCS moves, since military relocation timelines rarely match a tidy escrow calendar. VA loans generally require the borrower to certify an intent to occupy the home as a primary residence, with a standard window of about 60 days from closing to actually move in. PCS orders don't eliminate that certification requirement, a service member still has to document their occupancy plan, but they provide a recognized and commonly used basis for structuring around the standard timeline. Two paths come up most often in practice. First, if there's a specific, documented future date tied to the orders when the service member will actually be able to occupy the home, that date can sometimes be certified in place of the standard 60-day window. Second, and more commonly used, a spouse can satisfy the occupancy requirement by moving into the home within the standard window even if the service member themselves is still finishing out current orders elsewhere, completing a deployment, or in a training pipeline before reporting to the new duty station. VA's own guidance treats a spouse's occupancy, properly certified, as satisfying the requirement on the service member's behalf.

None of this replaces an actual conversation with a VA-approved lender about a specific service member's entitlement status, since full versus partial entitlement depends on loan history that a lender has to verify through the VA's own records, not something a buyer can self-assess from memory of a prior loan. It's also worth confirming occupancy timeline specifics directly with the lender underwriting the loan, since the exact documentation required for a PCS-based occupancy exception can vary by lender even within VA's general framework.

There's a practical sequencing question worth raising early with a lender, too: a Certificate of Eligibility has to be pulled and reviewed before anyone can say with certainty whether a specific service member has full or partial entitlement, and that step is worth doing well before house-hunting starts seriously, not after an offer is already written. A service member who assumes they have full entitlement because they've never personally used a VA loan, but who is a joint borrower on a spouse's prior VA-financed property, can find the actual entitlement picture is more complicated than expected. Getting that confirmed early avoids the scenario where a buyer falls in love with a home priced well above the conforming limit, only to find financing assumptions were off.

PCS timing also interacts with how an offer gets written in the first place. A seller weighing multiple offers generally wants clarity on when they can expect to close and when the buyer will actually take possession, and a military buyer whose report date is still being finalized is in a genuinely different position than a conventional buyer with no external timeline pressure. Being upfront about PCS orders, and about whether occupancy will be satisfied by the service member directly or by a spouse moving in first, tends to go over better with sellers and listing agents than leaving that ambiguous until after an offer is accepted. It's also worth asking a lender early whether a rate-lock extension or a longer escrow period makes sense given how firm or flexible the actual report date is, since PCS timelines sometimes shift after an offer is already in motion.

Locally, Movoto's Woodland Hills page, updated October 29, 2026, shows 378 active listings, 27 new listings, a median sale price of $1,299,000 for homes that closed in September 2026, and an average 50 days on market, up from 44 a year ago. For a full-entitlement VA buyer, that median sitting above the conforming high-cost limit isn't a barrier the way it would be on a conventional or partial-entitlement loan, and the slower pace compared to a year ago generally works in a PCS buyer's favor, since there's less pressure to waive standard contingencies just to compete. Occupancy-requirement specifics (the 60-day standard and PCS exception) are confirmed across multiple lender and military-finance sources citing VA circulars, not a direct VA.gov occupancy page. This is general information, not a substitute for confirming a specific service member's entitlement and occupancy documentation with a VA-approved lender.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is there a maximum amount I can borrow with a VA loan in Woodland Hills?

For a veteran with full entitlement, no. VA's own loan limits page confirms there's no VA loan limit for full-entitlement borrowers, as long as the lender approves the loan and the appraisal supports the price. The 2026 high-cost conforming limit, $1,249,125 for most of LA County, only applies to buyers with partial entitlement.

What's the difference between full and partial entitlement?

Full entitlement generally applies to a veteran who hasn't used their VA loan benefit before, or who paid off and sold a home financed with a prior VA loan. Partial entitlement means some of the benefit is still tied up, usually in an existing VA-financed property, which brings county loan limits back into play for the remaining amount available.

Do PCS orders mean I don't have to move in right away?

Not exactly. VA loans require a certified intent to occupy, typically within 60 days of closing. PCS orders don't remove that requirement, but they provide a recognized basis for an exception, either certifying a specific later occupancy date tied to the orders, or having a spouse satisfy the requirement by moving in within the standard window.

Can my spouse occupy the home instead of me if I'm still finishing out orders elsewhere?

Yes, with proper certification. VA's guidance treats a spouse's occupancy of the home within the standard window as satisfying the occupancy requirement on the service member's behalf.

Does Woodland Hills' home prices put it out of reach for VA buyers?

Not for a full-entitlement VA buyer. The median sale price here sits above the conforming high-cost limit that still constrains conventional and partial-entitlement VA borrowers, but full entitlement isn't capped by that figure.

Who verifies whether I have full or partial entitlement?

A VA-approved lender, through the VA's own records, specifically a Certificate of Eligibility. It isn't something to self-assess from memory of a prior loan, since VA's benefit history, not the buyer's own tracking, is the actual source of record.
