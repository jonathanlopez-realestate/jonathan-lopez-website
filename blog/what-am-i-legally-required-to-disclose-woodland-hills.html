What Am I Legally Required to Disclose When Selling My Woodland Hills Home?

Jonathan Lopez, Woodland Hills Realtor | Jim Sandoval Group | CA DRE #02282844 · Updated September 2026

Selling a home in Woodland Hills means more paperwork than just the listing agreement and the purchase contract. California law puts a specific, mandatory obligation on sellers: tell the buyer, in writing, about known problems with the property before the sale closes. The form that does this is the Transfer Disclosure Statement, and the rules around it are more detailed, and less forgiving of shortcuts, than most sellers expect.

What the TDS Actually Requires

Under California Civil Code sections 1102 through 1102.14, sellers of most residential property with one to four units must complete a statutory disclosure form covering known material defects: structural issues, roof condition, plumbing, electrical systems, pest damage, zoning violations, unpermitted structural changes, and any lawsuits affecting title, among more than 20 categories total. The form is built in three sections. Section I is the seller's own knowledge, answered with yes/no checkboxes. Section II is the listing agent's own visual inspection of accessible areas, a separate obligation that exists independently of what the seller discloses. Section III covers anything the buyer's own inspections turn up before closing.

The Knowledge Standard, and Why It Matters

The TDS only requires disclosure of what the seller actually knows, not what a more thorough inspection would have found. That distinction is the whole point: you're not required to tear open walls looking for problems, but you are required to disclose every issue you're actually aware of. A "no" checked on a defect category when the seller genuinely knew about the problem is the single most common piece of evidence in post-closing nondisclosure lawsuits, because the seller's own signature sits directly next to that box. The practical rule real estate attorneys give sellers: when in doubt, disclose it and let the buyer decide, rather than guess wrong on a form that can become the central piece of evidence months or years later.

New for 2026: Third-Hand Smoke Disclosure

Assembly Bill 455, signed in 2025 and effective January 1, 2026, added a new requirement to the statute: a seller who has actual knowledge of tobacco or nicotine residue, or any history of smoking or vaping inside the home, must disclose that in writing. It covers e-cigarettes and vaping devices in addition to traditional tobacco, and like every other line on the form, it only requires disclosure of what the seller actually knows and can't be skipped or waived by contract language.

The Buyer's Right to Cancel, and When It Resets

Once a buyer receives a completed TDS, California law gives them an unconditional right to cancel the purchase contract: 3 days if delivered in person, or 5 days if delivered by mail or as an electronic record. That clock runs even if every other contingency in the deal has already been removed. If a seller later amends the disclosure because new information surfaces, a roof leak found during a final walkthrough, for example, the amendment restarts the buyer's cancellation window from the date of the new delivery, not the original one. That's why real estate professionals push sellers to disclose everything up front rather than in pieces as things come up.

"As-Is" Doesn't Mean "No Disclosure"

One of the most common misunderstandings sellers bring into a transaction is that listing a home "as-is" gets them out of the disclosure requirement. It doesn't. California courts have consistently held that an as-is clause limits a seller's obligation to make repairs, not their duty to disclose known defects. A seller who checks "no" on a known problem is exposed to liability regardless of an as-is provision in the purchase contract.

What Happens if a Seller Gets It Wrong

Failing to properly disclose doesn't automatically unwind the sale. Instead, a seller who willfully or negligently fails to disclose a known defect is liable for the buyer's actual damages, generally the real dollar cost of fixing what wasn't disclosed. Buyers pursuing a fraud-based claim generally have 3 years from the date they discover the problem to bring it, and California's delayed-discovery rules can push that window out further if the defect wasn't reasonably discoverable at closing. That's a long tail of exposure for something that's straightforward to get right at the time of listing.

The TDS Isn't the Only Disclosure in Play

Most Woodland Hills sales also require a Natural Hazard Disclosure statement covering flood, fire, and seismic zones (based on where the property sits, not what anyone knows), a Seller Property Questionnaire many brokerages use to capture more detail than the statutory minimum, and a Megan's Law database notice. The TDS and the NHD cover genuinely different things: one is about what people know, the other is about government-mapped hazard zones. Neither one can be waived by an as-is clause or a low sale price.

What This Means Before Listing

The paperwork side of a Woodland Hills sale isn't a formality to rush through the week before closing. Getting the disclosure right, completely and honestly, at the start of the process protects a seller far more than trying to manage a problem quietly and hoping it doesn't surface. This is a separate step from figuring out what you'll actually walk away with or whether a pre-listing improvement is worth the cost, but it belongs in the same early planning conversation. If there's real uncertainty about whether something needs to be disclosed, a real estate attorney can give a definitive answer for the specific situation, and I can make sure the disclosure paperwork gets built into the listing timeline from day one rather than treated as an afterthought.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do I have to disclose a problem I already fixed?

Generally, disclosure covers what you currently know about the property's condition. A past problem that was properly repaired is different from an unresolved one, but if you have any doubt about how to characterize it, disclose the history and let the buyer's own due diligence confirm the repair.

Does an "as-is" sale mean I don't have to fill out a disclosure form?

No. An as-is clause limits your obligation to make repairs, not your duty to disclose known defects under California Civil Code 1102. You still complete the full TDS.

What happens if I forget something and remember it later, after the buyer already has the disclosure?

Amend the disclosure and redeliver it. The buyer's 3-day or 5-day cancellation window restarts from the date of that new delivery, even if they've already removed every contingency in the contract.

Is the tobacco/vaping disclosure new, and does it apply to me?

Yes, it took effect January 1, 2026 under Assembly Bill 455. It only requires disclosure if you actually have knowledge of tobacco or nicotine residue or a smoking/vaping history in the home. If you have no such knowledge, you can truthfully answer no.

What's the difference between the TDS and the Natural Hazard Disclosure?

The TDS covers your own knowledge of the property's condition and the agent's visual inspection. The Natural Hazard Disclosure covers government-mapped hazard zones (flood, fire, seismic) that apply based on location, regardless of anyone's personal knowledge.

What actually happens if I don't disclose something and the buyer finds out later?

The sale itself typically isn't voided, but you can be held liable for the buyer's actual damages, generally the real cost of fixing the undisclosed problem. Buyers generally have up to 3 years from discovering the issue to bring a claim.
