Does Measure ULA's Mansion Tax Change How I Should Price and Market My Woodland Hills Luxury Home?

Jonathan Lopez, Woodland Hills Realtor · Jim Sandoval Group · CA DRE #02282844 · Updated October 2026

Most Woodland Hills sellers never think about Measure ULA, and most never need to. It is a City of Los Angeles transfer tax that only applies to sales above $5.4 million, and the city's typical home sells for a small fraction of that. But Woodland Hills does have a genuine luxury tier, and for a seller marketing a home anywhere near that threshold, Measure ULA is not a footnote. It is a real number that should shape both the asking price and the marketing conversation from day one.

What Measure ULA actually taxes. Voters approved Measure ULA in 2022 to fund homelessness and affordable housing programs, and it took effect in 2023 with an initial threshold of $5 million. The thresholds adjust annually for inflation each July 1. For transactions closing after June 30, 2026, the current thresholds are $5.4 million and $10.9 million. A sale priced from $5.4 million up to $10.9 million is taxed at 4% of the full sale price. A sale at $10.9 million or above is taxed at 5.5% of the full sale price. The tax is seller-paid and comes out of proceeds at closing, the same way a standard county transfer tax does, just at a dramatically higher rate.

Where it applies, and where it does not. This is a City of Los Angeles tax, not a Los Angeles County tax. Woodland Hills sits within LA city limits, so it applies here. Several nearby, comparably upscale communities do not share city limits with Los Angeles, including Calabasas and Hidden Hills, both popular comparison points for Woodland Hills luxury buyers. A buyer deciding between a $6 million home in Woodland Hills and a similarly priced home in Hidden Hills is not comparing equivalent after-tax outcomes for the seller, even if the listing prices look the same, since one sale carries a 4% city transfer tax on top of standard closing costs and the other does not. That is worth naming directly in marketing materials and buyer conversations, not glossed over.

The cliff effect, and why pricing strategy matters. Because the tax applies to the entire sale price once a threshold is crossed, not just the amount above it, pricing decisions near either line carry outsized consequences. Consider two illustrative examples, using round numbers rather than a real transaction: a home that sells for $10.8 million owes 4% ULA tax, or $432,000. A home that sells for $11 million, just $200,000 more, crosses into the 5.5% tier and owes $605,000. That is a swing of more than $170,000 in tax on a $200,000 difference in sale price. The same cliff exists at the lower threshold: a sale at $5.3 million owes nothing, while a sale at $5.5 million owes 4%, or $220,000.

This does not mean every seller near a threshold should price just under it. Whether that is realistic depends on what the comparable sales in your specific neighborhood actually support, and what a buyer is genuinely willing to pay for your home. Pricing artificially low to dodge the tax only works if the market would not have supported the higher price anyway; if your home would likely sell well above the threshold regardless, underpricing it does not avoid the tax conversation, it just leaves money on the table during negotiation while the tax exposure returns the moment a buyer's offer clears the line. The more useful exercise is modeling the after-tax outcome at a few realistic price points before you set an asking price, not picking a number and hoping it lands favorably.

What this means for marketing a Woodland Hills luxury listing. A listing priced to clear $10.9 million decisively should be marketed and negotiated with that tax already built into the seller's net expectations, since trying to negotiate it away late in escrow rarely works once a buyer has anchored to the listed price. A listing that could realistically sell in the $5 million to $6 million range benefits from an early, honest conversation about whether sitting just under $5.4 million, if the comps support it, avoids the tax entirely without leaving real value on the table. Either way, this is a conversation for before the sign goes in the yard, not after an offer comes in. Frequently Asked Questions

What is Measure ULA?

A City of Los Angeles transfer tax, approved by voters in 2022, that funds homelessness and affordable housing programs. It applies only to real estate sales above a set dollar threshold within LA city limits.

What are the current Measure ULA thresholds and rates?

For closings after June 30, 2026, sales from $5.4 million to $10.9 million are taxed at 4% of the full sale price. Sales at $10.9 million or above are taxed at 5.5% of the full sale price. Thresholds adjust annually for inflation each July 1.

Who pays Measure ULA, the buyer or the seller?

The seller. It is deducted from sale proceeds at closing, similar to a standard transfer tax, just at a much higher rate for qualifying sales.

Does Measure ULA apply everywhere in Los Angeles County?

No. It is a City of Los Angeles tax, not a county tax. Woodland Hills sits within LA city limits and is subject to it. Nearby cities with separate municipal boundaries, including Calabasas and Hidden Hills, are not.

Why does the exact sale price matter so much near a threshold?

Because the tax applies to the entire sale price once a threshold is crossed, not just the amount above it, crossing from just under to just over a threshold can increase the tax owed by well over $100,000 for a relatively small increase in price.

Should I price my luxury listing just under a threshold to avoid the tax?

Only if your local comps genuinely support that price. Underpricing a home that would otherwise sell well above the threshold does not avoid the tax conversation, it just reduces your negotiating room. A real comparison of likely outcomes at a few price points is the right way to decide, not a flat rule.
