Does SB79 Change What My Property Near the G Line Is Worth in Woodland Hills?

Jonathan Lopez, Woodland Hills Realtor | Jim Sandoval Group | CA DRE #02282844 · Updated September 2026

If you own a home, duplex, or small apartment building anywhere near the G Line's Canoga station in Warner Center, a new state law that took effect this year could be quietly changing what your property is actually worth, regardless of what the zoning sign on the corner says.

The law is SB79, officially the Abundant and Affordable Homes Near Transit Act. It took effect statewide on July 1, 2026. The City of Los Angeles didn't just wait on the state law to work itself out. On June 23, 2026, the City Council adopted two of its own implementing ordinances, the Low-Rise Ordinance and the Phased Implementation Ordinance, and both became effective June 30, 2026. That matters because it means SB79 isn't a future possibility for Woodland Hills property owners. It's already law, with the city's own rules built on top of it.

Here's the part that gets confusing. A separate SB79 page from LA County Planning states that the law is delayed until around 2029 in the unincorporated areas of Los Angeles County. If you've seen that headline somewhere, it does not apply to you. Woodland Hills sits inside the incorporated City of Los Angeles, not unincorporated county land, so the county's delay has no bearing here. The city's own ordinances, already in effect, are what govern your property.

What SB79 Actually Does

SB79 allows multifamily housing to be built by right, meaning without a zone change or discretionary approval, on parcels within roughly a quarter to half mile of certain transit stops, even where the underlying zoning currently only allows single-family homes. The law sorts qualifying stops into two tiers.

Tier 1 covers the highest-frequency transit, including LA's B and D subway lines and commuter rail lines running 72 or more trips a day. Those parcels can see building heights of 65 to 95 feet and densities of 100 to 160 units per acre.

Tier 2 covers light rail lines, high-frequency commuter rail, and bus rapid transit, and this is the tier that includes the G Line. Tier 2 parcels can see heights of roughly 55 to 85 feet and densities in the range of 80 to 140 units per acre. Any project with 10 or more units has to set aside a share of them for lower income households, either 7% at extremely low income, 10% at very low income, or 13% at low income. Buildings that reach 85 feet also trigger labor standards requirements.

Why Canoga Station Matters for West Woodland Hills

The G Line is a dedicated busway rapid transit line, and its Canoga station serves the Warner Center and west Woodland Hills area directly. Under the framework above, bus rapid transit stops like Canoga generally fall into SB79's Tier 2 category, the tier that opens the door to five to eight story multifamily buildings on parcels that, as of today, might be zoned for a single house on a quarter acre lot.

That gap between what a property is zoned for on paper and what it could actually support under state and city law is exactly where value gets created or missed. A lot that looks like an ordinary single-family sale on the surface might actually be worth more as a development site once its transit-adjacent eligibility is factored in, and that number can be very different from a standard comp-based valuation. It's the same principle behind how school zone quality shows up in a home's value or how a blocked nearby development changes the local supply picture: location-specific factors can move a property's real value well outside what a generic estimate assumes.

What This Doesn't Mean

This is not a blanket rezoning of every property in west Woodland Hills, and it's not automatic. Eligibility depends on the specific parcel's distance from a qualifying stop, which tier that stop falls into, and whether the property sits inside an excluded area such as a Historic Preservation Overlay Zone. The City of Los Angeles has published an interactive map through its Planning Department showing potential eligibility citywide, and that map, not a rule of thumb, is the actual source of truth for any individual address.

It also doesn't mean every eligible owner should or will redevelop. Plenty of people will keep living in their homes exactly as they are. What it does mean is that the underlying land value calculation has changed for a meaningful number of parcels in this neighborhood, and that's worth knowing whether you're planning to sell in the next year or the next ten.

What To Do If You Might Be Affected

If your property is within walking distance of the Canoga station, or any other rail or bus rapid transit stop in the area, it's worth finding out where you actually stand before you price a sale, plan a renovation, or just wonder what your home is worth today. That starts with checking the city's own eligibility map for your specific address, and then having a real conversation about what that means for value in today's market, not a generic estimate.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does SB79 automatically rezone my property?

No. It removes certain local zoning barriers to multifamily housing on eligible parcels near qualifying transit stops, but it doesn't force any owner to build anything, and eligibility still depends on your specific parcel and the city's official map.

Is my home near Canoga station definitely eligible?

Not necessarily. Eligibility depends on exact distance from the stop, which tier it falls under, and whether the parcel is excluded for reasons like a Historic Preservation Overlay Zone. Check the City of LA's official interactive map for your address rather than assuming.

Does the LA County 2029 delay apply to Woodland Hills?

No. That delay applies specifically to unincorporated Los Angeles County. Woodland Hills is part of the incorporated City of Los Angeles, which adopted its own SB79 ordinances effective June 30, 2026.

What height and density are we talking about near a G Line stop?

Bus rapid transit stops like Canoga generally fall under SB79's Tier 2 category, which allows building heights of roughly 55 to 85 feet and densities in the range of 80 to 140 units per acre, subject to affordability set-asides.

Does this mean I have to sell or redevelop?

No. Nothing about this law requires any property owner to do anything. It simply changes what's legally possible to build on an eligible lot, which can change how that lot is valued.

How do I find out if my specific property qualifies?

Check the City of Los Angeles Planning Department's official SB79 interactive StoryMap and ZIMAS system for your address, and confirm anything specific to your parcel with a land use attorney or the Planning Department directly before making decisions.
